H1. Metal Casting

Key terms — know these words
  • Casting — shaping metal by pouring it molten into a mould cavity and letting it solidify.
  • Pattern — replica of the part (made larger) used to make the mould cavity.
  • Mould / cope / drag — sand cavity; upper / lower box.
  • Core — insert forming internal cavities.
  • Sprue / runner / gate / riser — vertical channel / horizontal channel / entry to cavity / reservoir that feeds the shrinking casting.
  • Parting line — joint face between cope and drag.
  • Fettling — cleaning: cutting gates and risers, grinding.
  • Shrinkage / machining / draft allowance — extra size or taper built into the pattern.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Sand-casting mould (cross-section): metal runs down the sprue, along the runner and gate into the cavity; the riser feeds extra molten metal as the casting shrinks.

  1. Pattern making (with allowances).
  2. Moulding: sand rammed round the pattern in cope and drag; pattern removed; cores set; mould closed.
  3. Melting the metal and pouring through the sprue.
  4. Solidification and cooling (riser feeds shrinkage).
  5. Shake-out, fettling, inspection and heat treatment/machining if needed.

Pattern allowances — CAT-1 Q5 (answer in the key)

Pattern allowances (CAT-1 Q5): the pattern is made larger than the finished component — for shrinkage on cooling and extra stock to machine off — and its vertical faces are tapered (draft) so it can be withdrawn from the sand without damaging the mould.

  • Shrinkage allowance: the pattern is made larger than the casting because the metal contracts as it cools from the liquid to the solid state; the value depends on the material and its contraction.
  • Machining allowance: as-cast surfaces are rough; extra stock is added so that it can be machined off to reach the required size and finish.
  • Draft allowance: vertical faces of the pattern (⟂ the parting line) are given a slight taper so the pattern can be withdrawn without damaging the mould.
Worked examplePart (b) of the question — what if shrinkage and draft are neglected?
No shrinkage allowance → the cast part ends up smaller than the target, so it malfunctions in service or will not fit the assembly. No draft → the pattern jams when drawn from the sand, damaging mould surfaces and causing defects in the casting.
Numerical: cast-iron shrinkage ≈ 1% (10 mm per metre): a 500 mm casting needs a pattern of 500 × 1.01 = 505 mm (before machining allowance).
MethodFeature
Sand castingcheap, any size/metal, rough finish
Die castingmetal dies, high accuracy, large volumes, non-ferrous
Investment (lost wax)fine detail, excellent finish, costly
Centrifugalrotating mould; pipes, rings, sound castings

Common defects: blowholes/porosity (trapped gas), shrinkage cavities (poor feeding), misruns and cold shuts (metal too cold), sand inclusions, hot tears.

H2. Forming Processes

Key terms — know these words
  • Plastic deformation — permanent shape change under load.
  • Hot / cold working — deforming above / below the recrystallisation temperature.
  • Forging — shaping by compressive hammering/pressing.
  • Rolling — reducing thickness by passing between rolls.
  • Extrusion — forcing metal through a die.
  • Drawing — pulling through a die (wire, tube).
  • Blanking / piercing / bending / deep drawing — sheet-metal operations.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

In forming the volume of metal is conserved while its shape is changed by plastic flow; there is little material waste and the grain flow improves strength.

ProcessHowTypical products
Forgingcompressive force in open or closed diescrankshafts, connecting rods, tools
Rollingmetal squeezed between rotating rollsplates, sheets, rails, beams
Extrusionpushed through a shaped dietubes, window frames, rods
Drawingpulled through a diewire, thin tubes
Sheet metalblanking (cut out), piercing (hole), bending, deep drawingcar panels, utensils, cans
  • Hot working: large deformation at low force, no strain hardening, poorer finish and accuracy.
  • Cold working: better finish, accuracy and strength (strain hardening), needs more force and gives less deformation.
Worked exampleRolling a plate from 20 mm to 15 mm: draft = 5 mm, reduction = 5/20 = 25%.

H3. Joining Processes

Key terms — know these words
  • Welding — joining by melting (fusion) or pressure so the joint becomes one piece.
  • Filler metal — added material that fills the joint.
  • Flux / shielding gas — protects molten metal from oxidation.
  • Soldering — joining with a filler melting below 450 °C.
  • Brazing — filler melts above 450 °C but below the base metal's melting point.
  • Weld joint types — butt, lap, T, corner, edge.

H4. Cutting (Machining) Processes

Key terms — know these words
  • Machining — removing material with a cutting tool.
  • Cutting speed V — surface speed of the work/tool, m/min.
  • Feed f — tool advance per revolution (mm/rev).
  • Depth of cut d — thickness of material removed in one pass.
  • MRR — material removal rate, mm³/min.
  • Turning / drilling / milling / shaping / grinding — the basic operations.

V = π D N / 1000  (m/min)  ;  turning MRR ≈ (π D N) × f × d  (mm³/min)
Worked exampleD = 50 mm at N = 400 rpm: V = π×50×400/1000 = 62.8 m/min. With f = 0.2 mm/rev, d = 2 mm: MRR = (π×50×400) × 0.2 × 2 = 62 832 × 0.4 ≈ 25 100 mm³/min.

Tool materials: high-speed steel (HSS), carbide, ceramic, diamond — harder (and costlier) materials allow higher speeds.

H5. CNC Machining

Key terms — know these words
  • NC / CNC — numerical / computer numerical control.
  • MCU — machine control unit — reads the program, drives the axes.
  • G-code — motion commands (G00 rapid, G01 linear feed, G02/G03 arcs).
  • M-code — machine functions (M03 spindle on, M05 stop, M06 tool change, M30 end).
  • Servo motor + encoder — drive and position feedback.
  • ATC — automatic tool changer.
  • Axes (X, Y, Z) — directions of table/tool motion; 3-axis, 5-axis machines.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

A CNC axis: the controller turns the program into commands, the drive and servo motor move the table through a ball screw, and an encoder reports the real position back so the controller can correct any error.

Workflow: CAD model → CAM (toolpaths) → G-code part program → CNC machine. A closed loop compares commanded and measured position (encoder) so errors are corrected. Advantages: accuracy and repeatability, complex shapes, less labour, lights-out production.

Worked exampleG21 G90 (mm, absolute); G00 X0 Y0 (rapid to origin); G01 X50 F100 (feed 50 mm along X at 100 mm/min) — takes 50/100 = 0.5 min = 30 s; M30 end.

H6. Additive Manufacturing (3-D Printing)

Key terms — know these words
  • Additive manufacturing — making a part by adding material layer by layer.
  • STL — triangulated surface file sent to the printer.
  • Slicing — cutting the model into layers and generating toolpath (G-code).
  • FDM / FFF — fused deposition modelling: melts and extrudes thermoplastic filament.
  • SLA — stereolithography: UV light cures liquid resin.
  • SLS / SLM — laser fuses powder (polymer / metal).
  • Layer height — thickness of each layer.
  • Support structure — temporary material supporting overhangs.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

FDM / FFF 3D printing: a heated nozzle melts thermoplastic filament and deposits it in a path; after each layer the platform moves down (or the nozzle up) by the layer height.

  1. CAD model → export STL.
  2. Slice into layers, choose orientation, supports, infill.
  3. Print layer by layer.
  4. Post-process: remove supports, sand, cure, heat-treat.
TechnologyMaterialFeature
FDM / FFFPLA, ABS filamentcheap, common, visible layer lines
SLAphotopolymer resinfine detail and smooth surface
SLSnylon powderstrong, no supports needed
SLM / DMLSmetal powderfunctional metal parts (aerospace, medical)
  • Advantages: complex geometry, no tooling, low waste, quick prototypes, customisation.
  • Limits: slow for volume, surface finish, anisotropic strength, size and material limits.
  • Applications: prototypes, tooling, medical implants, aerospace parts, customised products.
Worked exampleFDM: 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 60 mm/s → volumetric rate 0.4 × 0.2 × 60 = 4.8 mm³/s. A 30 cm³ (30 000 mm³) part takes 30 000/4.8 = 6250 s ≈ 1.7 h of pure printing.

H7. Thermal Engineering & the IC Engine

Key terms — know these words
  • Heat engine — device converting heat to work.
  • IC engine — engine burning fuel inside the cylinder.
  • TDC / BDC — top / bottom dead centre — extreme piston positions.
  • Stroke / bore — piston travel / cylinder diameter.
  • Compression ratio r — Vmax/Vmin.
  • SI / CI — spark-ignition (petrol) / compression-ignition (diesel).
  • Brake power BP — power at the shaft, 2πNT/60.
  • Thermal efficiency — work output ÷ heat input.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Four-stroke petrol (SI) engine: one power stroke every two crank revolutions (720°). Valves open only for suction (IN) and exhaust (EX); the shading shows fresh charge, compressed charge, burning gas and exhaust above the piston. Red arrow = piston direction.

  • Four-stroke: suction, compression, power, exhaust — 2 crank revolutions, one power stroke per cycle.
  • Two-stroke: the cycle is completed in one revolution (a power stroke every revolution) with ports instead of valves; simpler and lighter but less efficient and more polluting.
SI (petrol)CI (diesel)
Fuel intakeair + fuel mixtureair only; fuel injected at the end of compression
Ignitionspark plugheat of compression
Compression ratio6–1015–22
Ideal cycleOttoDiesel
Efficiencylowerhigher
swept volume = (π/4) D² L  ;  Otto efficiency η = 1 − 1 / rγ−1  ;  BP = 2π N T / 60
Worked example4-cylinder engine, bore 80 mm, stroke 90 mm: Vs = π/4 × 80² × 90 = 452 389 mm³ ≈ 0.452 L per cylinder → 1.81 L total. With r = 10, γ = 1.4: η = 1 − 10−0.4 = 1 − 0.398 = 0.60 (ideal). Torque 150 N·m at 3000 rpm: BP = 2π×3000×150/60 = 47.1 kW.

H8. Power Plants

Key terms — know these words
  • Rankine cycle — steam power cycle: boiler → turbine → condenser → pump.
  • Prime mover — turbine or engine driving the generator.
  • Penstock — pipe carrying water to a hydro turbine.
  • Reactor / moderator / control rods — nuclear: fission vessel / slows neutrons / absorbs neutrons.
  • Capacity factor — actual output ÷ maximum possible.
  • Overall efficiency — electrical output ÷ fuel energy input.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Steam (Rankine-cycle) power plant: the boiler makes high-pressure steam, the turbine expands it to drive the generator, the condenser turns the exhaust back into water and the pump returns it to the boiler.

PlantEnergy source → conversionNotes
Thermal (steam)coal/gas heat → steam → turbine → generator≈35–40% efficient; needs cooling water; emissions
Hydrowater potential energy → turbineP = ρ g Q H η; clean, flexible, site-limited
NuclearU-235 fission heat → steamno CO₂ in operation; waste and safety issues
Gas turbinefuel burnt in compressed air → turbinefast start, peaking plants; combined cycle ≈ 55–60%
Dieseldiesel engine + alternatorstandby and remote areas
Worked exampleHydro: Q = 10 m³/s, H = 50 m, η = 0.85: P = 1000×9.81×10×50×0.85 = 4.17 MW.
Thermal: 500 MW output at 38% overall efficiency needs 500/0.38 = 1316 MW of fuel heat; with coal of 20 MJ/kg that is 65.8 kg/s ≈ 237 t/h.

H9. Refrigeration & Air Conditioning

Key terms — know these words
  • Refrigeration — removing heat from a cold space and rejecting it to a warmer place.
  • Refrigerant — working fluid (R134a, R32…).
  • Compressor / condenser / expansion valve / evaporator — the four main components.
  • COP — coefficient of performance = QL/W.
  • Ton of refrigeration — 3.517 kW (211 kJ/min).
  • Air conditioning — controlling temperature, humidity, cleanliness and distribution of air.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Vapour-compression refrigeration cycle: compressor raises pressure; the condenser rejects heat; the expansion valve drops pressure and temperature; the evaporator absorbs heat from the cold space.

COP = refrigeration effect / work input = QL / W  ;  Carnot COP = TL / (TH − TL)   (kelvin)
Worked exampleAn evaporator absorbs 3.5 kW with a 1 kW compressor: COP = 3.5. Limit between −10 °C (263 K) and 35 °C (308 K): COPCarnot = 263/45 = 5.8; a real cycle is lower. A 1.5-ton AC removes 1.5 × 3.517 ≈ 5.3 kW of heat.

Air conditioning adds control of humidity (dehumidifying at the cold coil), filtering and air circulation; window, split and central types. Heat taken from the room plus the compressor work is rejected outdoors at the condenser.

H10. Fuel Cells & Sustainable Energy

Key terms — know these words
  • Fuel cell — electrochemical device converting fuel's chemical energy directly into electricity.
  • Anode / cathode / electrolyte (PEM) — fuel electrode / oxidant electrode / proton-conducting membrane.
  • Renewable energy — from sources replenished naturally.
  • Photovoltaic effect — light generating voltage in a semiconductor.
  • Betz limit — maximum 59.3% of wind power can be extracted.
  • Sustainability — meeting present needs without harming future generations.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Hydrogen PEM fuel cell: hydrogen splits at the anode, protons cross the membrane, electrons take the external circuit (the useful current) and all meet oxygen at the cathode to make water.

overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O + electricity + heat  ;  ideal cell voltage ≈ 1.23 V (≈ 0.6–0.8 V under load)
  • Efficiency: typically 40–60% electrical (higher with heat recovery) — not limited by the Carnot cycle; emits only water if hydrogen is used.
  • Types: PEMFC (vehicles), SOFC, alkaline, phosphoric-acid, molten-carbonate.
  • Uses: fuel-cell vehicles, backup power, portable power.
TechnologyPrinciple
Solar PVphotovoltaic effect in silicon cells → DC (then inverter)
Solar thermalsunlight heats a fluid → steam or hot water
WindP = ½ ρ A v³ Cp
Biomassburning/digesting organic matter

Worked exampleA stack of 100 cells each giving 0.7 V under load: 70 V (series). Wind: rotor radius 40 m (A = 5027 m²), v = 12 m/s, Cp = 0.4: P = 0.5×1.225×5027×12³×0.4 = 2.1 MW (note the v³ — doubling wind speed gives 8× power).

H11. Mechanisms: Four-Bar & Hexapod

Key terms — know these words
  • Link — rigid body joining others.
  • Kinematic pair — joint between two links (revolute, prismatic).
  • Kinematic chain / mechanism — assembly of links; mechanism = chain with one link fixed.
  • Degrees of freedom (DOF) — independent motions.
  • Grashof's law — s + l ≤ p + q for a link to rotate fully.
  • Crank / rocker / coupler — fully rotating / oscillating / floating link.
  • Inversion — a different mechanism obtained by fixing another link.
  • Hexapod (Stewart platform) — parallel mechanism with six linear actuators.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

A four-bar mechanism: four rigid links, four revolute pairs, one degree of freedom (3(n−1) − 2j = 3·3 − 2·4 = 1). With these lengths Grashof's condition holds and it is a crank-rocker.

planar DOF (Grübler): F = 3(n − 1) − 2j   (n links, j lower pairs)  ;  four-bar: F = 3·3 − 2·4 = 1
Fixed linkMechanism (Grashof chain)
link adjacent to the shortestcrank-rocker — crank rotates fully, rocker oscillates
the shortest linkdouble-crank (drag link) — both cranks rotate
link opposite to the shortestdouble-rocker — both oscillate (shortest link = coupler rotates)

If s + l > p + q (non-Grashof) no link can rotate fully and every inversion is a triple/double-rocker. Common four-bar uses: windscreen wipers (crank-rocker), locomotive coupling rods (double crank), and the slider-crank (IC engine, pumps) as a related chain.

Worked exampleLinks 20, 40, 50, 60: s + l = 20 + 60 = 80 ≤ p + q = 40 + 50 = 90 → Grashof. Fix the link next to the 20 → crank-rocker; fix the 20 → double-crank; fix the link opposite the 20 → double-rocker.

Hexapod (Stewart platform): a parallel mechanism in which six variable-length legs connect a fixed base to a moving platform — used in flight simulators, machine-tool positioning tables and precision alignment.

A hexapod moves the platform in all 6 DOF by changing six leg lengths; parallel structure gives high stiffness and precision — flight simulators, positioning stages, telescopes, machining.

  • Actuator / sensor / controller — motor, hydraulic or pneumatic drive / encoder, proximity, force, vision / the computer.

H14. Pneumatic & Hydraulic Actuators

Key terms — know these words
  • Actuator — device converting fluid or electrical energy into motion.
  • Pascal's law — pressure in a confined fluid acts equally in all directions.
  • Pneumatic — uses compressed air (≈ 6–8 bar).
  • Hydraulic — uses oil under high pressure (to ≈ 200–350 bar).
  • Cylinder — linear actuator: piston in a barrel.
  • Valve (DCV) — directs/controls flow.
  • FRL unit — filter, regulator, lubricator for compressed air.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Pascal's law in a hydraulic press: pressure applied to a confined liquid is transmitted equally in all directions, so a small force on a small piston balances a large force on a large piston.

p = F/A  ;  F₂ = F₁ (A₂/A₁)  ;  cylinder force F = p × A
PneumaticHydraulic
Fluidair (compressible)oil (incompressible)
Pressurelow (≈ 6–8 bar)high (≈ 100–350 bar)
Forcemoderatevery large
Speedfastslower, smooth, accurate positioning
Cleanlinessclean, exhaust to airoil leaks possible
Componentscompressor, FRL, valves, cylinderspump, reservoir, valves, cylinders/motors
Usesfactory automation, pick-and-place, toolspresses, excavators, brakes, lifts
Worked exampleHydraulic press: F₁ = 50 N on A₁ = 10 cm², A₂ = 100 cm²: F₂ = 50 × 100/10 = 500 N. Pneumatic cylinder: bore 50 mm (A = 1963 mm²) at 6 bar (0.6 N/mm²): F = 0.6 × 1963 ≈ 1180 N (less on the return stroke because of the rod area).
