A1. Ohm's Law & Circuit Elements

Key terms — know these words
  • Charge (Q) — the basic electrical quantity, measured in coulombs (C).
  • Current (I) — rate of flow of charge, I = dQ/dt; unit ampere (A = C/s). Conventional direction = the way positive charge would move (from + to −).
  • Voltage / potential difference (V) — energy per unit charge between two points; unit volt (V = J/C).
  • Resistance (R) — opposition to current flow; unit ohm (Ω). For a wire, R = ρL/A (resistivity × length ÷ area).
  • Conductance (G) — reciprocal of resistance, G = 1/R; unit siemens (S). Makes parallel-circuit sums easier.
  • Power (P) — rate of energy conversion, P = VI = I²R = V²/R; unit watt (W).
  • Energy (W) — power × time; unit joule (J). Electricity bills use the kilowatt-hour (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ).
  • Ideal voltage source — keeps its terminal voltage fixed whatever current it supplies (internal resistance = 0).
  • Ideal current source — forces a fixed current whatever voltage appears across it (internal resistance = ∞).
  • Independent / dependent source — independent: value set by itself. Dependent (controlled): value set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit (drawn as a diamond).
  • Active / passive element — active elements (sources) can deliver energy; passive elements (R, L, C) only absorb, dissipate or store it.
  • Linear element — an element whose V–I graph is a straight line through the origin (double V, double I).
  • Short circuit / open circuit — short: R = 0, V = 0, any current. Open: R = ∞, I = 0, any voltage.
  • Passive sign convention — if current enters the + terminal of an element, then P = VI > 0 means the element absorbs power; P < 0 means it delivers power.
Definition For a resistor, the voltage across it and the current through it are directly proportional:
V = I·R
where V is in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms (Ω). Equivalently, I = V/R and R = V/I.

A single-loop circuit: the source drives current I clockwise (leaving its + plate) through R. Ohm's law links V, I and R.

Fixing a common misconception Students often think "more resistance always means less current, full stop" — true only if V is fixed. If a circuit has multiple resistors, changing one resistor changes the current distribution everywhere (Section A3), not just through itself. Ohm's law is a local law for one element; predicting the whole circuit needs Kirchhoff's laws (A2).
Worked example A 12 V battery drives a 4 Ω resistor. Find the current and the power dissipated.
I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A
P = VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W (check: P = I²R = 3² × 4 = 36 W ✓ — two independent formulas, same answer)
The "compute two ways, check they agree" habit above is the same trick you'll use to verify Thevenin equivalents (A7) and to check power balance in superposition (A9).

Fixing a common misconception Short vs open circuit: people mix these up. A short lets current flow freely with zero voltage across it (like a wire). An open circuit lets zero current flow with any voltage across it (like a switch turned off). A source is never 'shorted' safely unless it has internal resistance — a shorted ideal voltage source would demand infinite current.
Worked example — sign convention A 12 V battery drives 3 A out of its + terminal. Battery: current leaves the + terminal, so P = −VI = −36 W (it delivers 36 W). The resistor it drives: current enters its + terminal, so P = +36 W (it absorbs 36 W). Total power in the circuit = −36 + 36 = 0 — energy is conserved.

A2. Kirchhoff's Laws

Key terms — know these words
  • Node — a point where two or more circuit elements join.
  • Essential node (junction) — a node where three or more branches meet — the only nodes that need a KCL equation.
  • Branch — a single element (or a series string of elements) connecting two nodes; it carries one current.
  • Loop — any closed path that visits each node at most once.
  • Mesh — a loop that contains no other loop inside it (a 'window' of the circuit).
  • Voltage rise / drop — going through a source from − to + is a rise; going through a resistor along the current is a drop.
  • Planar circuit — a circuit that can be drawn on paper with no wires crossing — needed for mesh analysis.
Definition KCL (Current Law): the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it — charge cannot pile up at a point.
Σ Iin = Σ Iout
KVL (Voltage Law): the sum of voltage rises around any closed loop equals the sum of voltage drops.
Σ Vrises = Σ Vdrops (equivalently, Σ V = 0 around the loop)
Why this is true KCL is conservation of charge applied to a single point: charge is neither created nor stored at an ideal node, so whatever flows in must flow out.
KVL is conservation of energy applied to a charge that travels once around a loop and returns to its start: since voltage is potential energy per unit charge, and the charge is back where it began, the net change in potential energy over the round trip must be zero.

Left: KCL at a node — the arrows into the dot balance the arrow out. Right: KVL round the dashed loop — the source's rise equals the two resistors' drops.

Worked example In the KVL loop above, V = 20 V, R1 = 2 Ω, R2 = 3 Ω. Since it's a single loop, the same current I flows through everything, so:
20 = I(2) + I(3) = 5I ⟹ I = 4 A
Drop across R1 = 8 V, across R2 = 12 V. Check: 8 + 12 = 20 V ✓ (rises = drops).
KVL written once per loop is exactly the equation you write n times in Mesh-Current Analysis (A5); KCL written once per node is exactly what you write in Node-Voltage Analysis (A4). Everything from here to A9 is KCL/KVL done systematically for bigger circuits.

Steps for applying KVL to a loop

  1. Pick a direction (clockwise is conventional) and label current directions on each resistor.
  2. Walk once round the loop. Going through a source from − to + is a rise (+); through a resistor with the current is a drop (−).
  3. Write: Σ rises − Σ drops = 0.
  4. If a solved current comes out negative, it simply flows opposite to the way you drew it.
Worked example — KCL at two nodes A node has 7 A entering along one wire, and leaves as 3 A and Ix. KCL: 7 = 3 + Ix ⟹ Ix = 4 A. If Ix then splits at the next node into 1 A and Iy, Iy = 4 − 1 = 3 A.

A3. Series, Parallel & Dividers

Key terms — know these words
  • Equivalent resistance (Req) — the single resistor that draws the same current as the whole network at the same voltage.
  • Series connection — elements strung end-to-end so they carry the same current.
  • Parallel connection — elements joined across the same two nodes so they share the same voltage.
  • Voltage divider — series resistors splitting a supply voltage in proportion to their resistances.
  • Current divider — parallel resistors splitting a supply current in inverse proportion to their resistances.
  • Series–parallel network — a mix of both; reduce it step by step, innermost combination first.
Definition Series (same current through each element): Req = R1 + R2 + ⋯
Parallel (same voltage across each element): 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ⋯ (two only: Req = R1R2/(R1+R2))

Series elements share one current path end to end; parallel elements share both end nodes, so they share the same voltage.

Why this is true Series: current has only one path, so by KCL the same I flows through every element in the chain. Summing V=IR drops around the loop by KVL gives Vtotal = I(R1+R2+…) = I·Req.
Parallel: both elements touch the same pair of nodes, so by definition of voltage (potential difference between two points) they see the same V. By KCL, the source current splits: I = V/R1 + V/R2 = V(1/R1 + 1/R2) = V/Req.
Divider formulas Voltage divider (series R1, R2 across V): VR2 = V · R2/(R1+R2)
Current divider (parallel R1, R2 fed by I): IR2 = I · R1/(R1+R2) — note it's the other resistor on top.
Fixing a common misconception Students often copy the voltage-divider formula into the current-divider formula and put R2 on top for IR2 as well. It's flipped: in a current divider, the branch with the smaller resistance takes the larger share of current, so IR2's share must be weighted by the other resistor, R1.
Worked example R1 = 6 Ω and R2 = 3 Ω are in parallel, fed by a 9 A source. Find the current through each.
Req = (6×3)/(6+3) = 2 Ω, so V = I·Req = 9×2 = 18 V across both.
IR1 = 18/6 = 3 A, IR2 = 18/3 = 6 A. Check: 3 + 6 = 9 A ✓ (KCL).
Via the divider shortcut: IR2 = 9 × 6/(6+3) = 6 A ✓ — same answer without solving for V first.
Req reduction is the first move in almost every Thevenin problem (A7): reduce everything except the load to one resistor, exactly as done here.

Useful shortcuts

  • n equal resistors in parallel: Req = R/n. Two 10 Ω in parallel give 5 Ω.
  • Conductance form: Geq = G1 + G2 + … for parallel (conductances add just like series resistances do).
  • Parallel is always smaller than the smallest branch — a quick sanity check on your answer.
  • Power divides too: in series the larger R dissipates more (P = I²R); in parallel the smaller R dissipates more (P = V²/R).
Worked example — series–parallel reduction A 12 V source feeds 2 Ω in series with (6 Ω ∥ 3 Ω).
Step 1: 6 ∥ 3 = 18/9 = 2 Ω. Step 2: Rtotal = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω. Step 3: I = 12/4 = 3 A.
Voltage across the parallel pair = 3 × 2 = 6 V ⟹ 6 Ω carries 1 A, 3 Ω carries 2 A (sum = 3 A ✓). Check the divider: I3Ω = 3 × 6/(6+3) = 2 A ✓.

A4. Node-Voltage Analysis

Key terms — know these words
  • Reference node (ground) — the node chosen as 0 V; every other node voltage is measured relative to it.
  • Node voltage — the voltage of a node with respect to the reference node.
  • Supernode — two non-reference nodes joined by a voltage source; treated as one node for KCL, plus one extra equation from the source.
  • Conductance form — writing branch current as G × (voltage difference) instead of (voltage difference)/R; gives a symmetric equation matrix.
Definition Pick one node as the reference (0 V, "ground"). Assign an unknown voltage to every other node. At each unknown node, write one KCL equation using Ohm's law to express every branch current in terms of node voltages. Solve the resulting simultaneous equations.

Two unknown nodes A and B. The 6 A source injects current into A; the bottom rail is the 0 V reference.

Worked example Write KCL at each node (current leaving a node through a resistor to a lower-potential node is (Vhere−Vthere)/R):
Node A: 6 = VA/R1 + (VA−VB)/R2 = VA/4 + (VA−VB)/4
Node B: 0 = (VB−VA)/R2 + VB/R3 = (VB−VA)/4 + VB/4
From the B-equation: VB = VA/2. Substituting into the A-equation: 6 = VA/4 + (VA−VA/2)/4 = 3VA/8 ⟹ VA = 16 V, VB = 8 V.
Check (KCL at A): current out through R1 = 16/4 = 4 A; through R2 = (16−8)/4 = 2 A. Total out = 6 A = current in ✓.
Keep VA=16 V in mind — the same numbers reappear, from a totally different method, as the Thevenin voltage in A7 and the superposition example in A9. That agreement is not a coincidence; it's the same physical circuit.

Steps

  1. Mark all nodes; choose the one with the most branches as the reference (0 V).
  2. Label the remaining node voltages V1, V2, …
  3. At each unknown node write KCL: Σ (currents leaving) = 0, with each resistor current = (Vnode − Vneighbour)/R.
  4. Solve the n − 1 linear equations. Then find any branch current with Ohm's law.

Matrix (conductance) form — spot the pattern

For the A4 example (three 4 Ω resistors, G = 0.25 S each) the equations collapse to:

[ 0.5 −0.25 ; −0.25 0.5 ] · [VA ; VB] = [ 6 ; 0 ]

Diagonal = sum of conductances touching that node; off-diagonal = minus the conductance between the two nodes; right-hand side = current sources injected. You can write this by inspection — a real time-saver in exams.

Supernode — the only tricky case If a voltage source sits between two non-reference nodes, you cannot write a current through it (an ideal voltage source can carry any current). So merge the two nodes into a supernode: write one KCL for the pair, and add one extra equation V1 − V2 = Vsource.

A5. Mesh-Current Analysis

Key terms — know these words
  • Mesh current — an imaginary current circulating round one mesh; real branch currents are built from these.
  • Shared (common) branch — a branch belonging to two meshes; its real current = difference of the two mesh currents.
  • Supermesh — two meshes sharing a current source; the source's branch is left out and one KVL is written round the combined outer loop.
  • Clockwise convention — choosing every mesh current clockwise makes all the shared-resistor terms negative — a built-in error check.
Definition Assign a circulating current to each independent loop ("mesh") of a planar circuit, all in the same rotational sense (say, clockwise). Write one KVL equation per mesh; a branch shared by two meshes carries the difference of their two mesh currents. Solve simultaneously, then recover real branch currents from the mesh currents.

Two-mesh circuit. Mesh 1 (left) holds V₁, R₁ and the shared branch Rs; mesh 2 (right) holds V₂, R₂ and the same Rs. Both mesh currents are drawn clockwise, and both sources push current clockwise.

Worked example V1=16 V, R1=4 Ω, Rs=2 Ω (shared), R2=6 Ω, V2=2 V, both sources driving their mesh clockwise.
Mesh 1: V1 = I1(R1+Rs) − I2·Rs ⟹ 16 = 6I1 − 2I2
Mesh 2: V2 = I2(R2+Rs) − I1·Rs ⟹ 2 = 8I2 − 2I1
From the first equation: I2 = 3I1 − 8. Substitute into the second:
2 = 8(3I1−8) − 2I1 = 22I1 − 64 ⟹ I1 = 3 A, I2 = 1 A.
Actual current through the shared resistor Rs = I1 − I2 = 2 A (from mesh-1 side toward mesh-2 side).
Notice the pattern in the equations: each mesh's own resistors get a + sign, the shared resistor gets a −Iother·Rshared term. This is structurally identical to the node-voltage equations of A4 — swap "current source" ↔ "voltage source" and "resistor to ground" ↔ "resistor around the mesh," and it's the same bookkeeping. Two roads, same destination.

Steps

  1. Count meshes (windows). Draw a clockwise mesh current in each.
  2. Write KVL round each mesh: (sum of R in the mesh) × own current − (shared R) × neighbour current = (source rise driving that mesh).
  3. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  4. Real current in a shared branch = difference of the two mesh currents (with sign showing direction).
Supermesh — the only tricky case If a current source is a shared branch between two meshes, you can't write KVL across it (unknown voltage). Skip it: write one KVL round the big outer loop of the two meshes, and add the constraint I1 − I2 = Isource.
SituationChoose
Many current sources, few nodesNode-voltage
Many voltage sources, few meshesMesh-current
Non-planar circuitNode-voltage (mesh analysis needs planar)

A6. Source Transformation

Key terms — know these words
  • Ideal source — no internal resistance; the source transformation does not apply to an ideal source on its own.
  • Practical source — an ideal source plus its internal resistance (in series for a voltage source, in parallel for a current source).
  • Internal resistance — the resistance inside a real source that makes its terminal voltage sag under load.
  • Equivalent sources — two sources with identical open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current at the terminals.
Definition A real voltage source Vs with series resistance R is externally indistinguishable from a real current source Is = Vs/R with the same R in parallel, and vice versa (Is with parallel R ↔ Vs=IsR with series R).

12 V in series with 4 Ω looks, from terminals a–b, exactly like a 3 A source (12/4) in parallel with the same 4 Ω.

Why this is true Two black boxes are "externally indistinguishable" if they produce the same V–I relationship at the terminals. Left box: open-circuit voltage = 12 V (no current, no drop across R); short-circuit current = 12/4 = 3 A. Right box: open-circuit voltage = 3 A × 4 Ω = 12 V (all current forced through R since the external path is open); short-circuit current = 3 A (all current bypasses R through the short). Same two extreme points ⟹ same straight-line V–I relationship ⟹ identical behaviour for any load attached at a–b.
This V=IR ↔ I=V/R swap is exactly what makes Thevenin ⟷ Norton equivalents (next section) interchangeable — a Thevenin source is nothing but a voltage source, and a Norton source is its current-source transform.
Two traps 1. Direction/polarity: the current-source arrow must point toward the + terminal of the equivalent voltage source (the terminal the arrow leaves from is the + side).
2. Ideal sources: a voltage source with no series resistance (or a current source with no parallel resistance) cannot be transformed. Also, a resistor in parallel with a voltage source, or in series with a current source, does not affect what the other elements see, so it can be dropped when analysing the rest of the circuit.
Worked example — going the other way A 2 A current source in parallel with 5 Ω ⟹ voltage source V = I·R = 2 × 5 = 10 V in series with the same 5 Ω. Check: open-circuit voltage of the original = 2 × 5 = 10 V ✓; short-circuit current of the new one = 10/5 = 2 A ✓.

Why bother? Transform repeatedly to turn a ladder of mixed sources into one source with one resistor — often faster than writing any equation at all.

A7. Thevenin's & Norton's Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Linear network — a network whose elements obey superposition/proportionality; Thevenin's theorem holds only for these.
  • Terminals a–b — the two points where the load will connect and from which we 'look into' the network.
  • Open-circuit voltage (Voc) — voltage across a–b with nothing connected; equals Vth.
  • Short-circuit current (Isc) — current when a–b are shorted together; equals IN.
  • Thevenin resistance (Rth) — resistance seen at a–b with independent sources killed; also Rth = Voc/Isc.
  • Norton equivalent — current source IN in parallel with RN = Rth.
  • Killing a source — voltage source → replace by a short; current source → replace by an open circuit.
Definition Any linear resistive network, viewed from two terminals a–b, behaves exactly like a single source in series (or parallel) with a single resistance:
Thevenin: Vth in series with Rth   ⟺   Norton: IN=Vth/Rth in parallel with RN=Rth
Vth = open-circuit voltage at a–b. Rth = resistance seen at a–b with all independent sources killed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).

The 24 V / 6 Ω / 12 Ω network (load removed) collapses, as seen from a–b, to a 16 V source in series with 4 Ω.

Worked example Find Vth: with a–b open, no current flows anywhere except around the single loop 24 V–6 Ω–12 Ω: I = 24/(6+12) = 4/3 A. Voltage across the 12 Ω (this is Vab since nothing else drops between a and b) = (4/3)(12) = 16 V = Vth.
Find Rth: kill the 24 V source (replace with a short). Looking into a–b now sees 6 Ω and 12 Ω in parallel: Rth = (6×12)/(6+12) = 4 Ω.
Norton form: IN = Vth/Rth = 16/4 = 4 A, RN = 4 Ω.
Vth=16 V is the exact same number as node A's voltage in the A4 example — no surprise, since Thevenin's theorem is just node/mesh analysis packaged for one pair of terminals. Keep Vth=16 V, Rth=4 Ω on hand: A8 attaches a load here to find maximum power, and A9 re-derives this 16 V by superposition.

Procedure

  1. Remove the load; label terminals a–b.
  2. Vth: calculate the open-circuit voltage across a–b (any method).
  3. Rth: kill independent sources and find the resistance looking into a–b — or compute Rth = Voc/Isc.
  4. Draw Vth in series with Rth, reconnect the load, and solve the simple one-loop circuit.
Why the second Rth method works The Thevenin equivalent has an open-circuit voltage Vth and, when shorted, delivers Isc = Vth/Rth. Rearranging gives Rth = Voc/Isc. Useful when the network contains dependent sources: they must not be killed, so use Voc/Isc (or apply a test source) instead.
Worked example — using it to change the load With Vth = 16 V and Rth = 4 Ω (from above), what does a 12 Ω load draw?
I = 16/(4+12) = 1 A, Vload = 12 V. For a 4 Ω load: 2 A; for 28 Ω: 0.5 A. That is the power of Thevenin — solve the network once, then any load is a one-line calculation.

A8. Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Load (RL) — the element receiving power from the source network.
  • Matched load — RL = Rth; the condition for maximum power to the load.
  • Efficiency (η) — η = power in load ÷ total power supplied = RL/(Rth + RL).
  • Pmax — Vth²/(4Rth) — the peak of the power-vs-RL curve.
Definition A source with Thevenin resistance Rth delivers maximum power to a load RL when
RL = Rth
and that maximum power is Pmax = Vth² / (4Rth).
Why this is true (derivation) Current in the loop: I = Vth/(Rth+RL). Power in the load:
P = I²RL = Vth² · RL / (Rth+RL)²
Maximise P over RL by setting dP/dRL=0. Using the quotient rule on RL/(Rth+RL)²:
dP/dRL ∝ (Rth+RL)² − RL·2(Rth+RL) = (Rth+RL)[(Rth+RL) − 2RL] = (Rth+RL)(Rth−RL)
This is zero (and changes sign from + to −, confirming a maximum) exactly when RL=Rth. Substituting back: Pmax = Vth²·Rth/(2Rth)² = Vth²/(4Rth).

Power delivered to RL (Vth = 16 V, Rth = 4 Ω): it rises, peaks exactly at RL = Rth, then falls — the calculus in the box above proves the peak sits there.

Fixing a common misconception "Maximum power transfer" does not mean maximum efficiency. At RL=Rth, exactly half the total power is dissipated in Rth itself — efficiency is only 50%. Power systems engineers actually want RL ≫ Rth for high efficiency (low loss in supply resistance); matched loading is what you want in signal/communication circuits, where getting the largest possible power to a small receiver matters more than wasting some in the source.
Worked example Continuing the A7 circuit (Vth=16 V, Rth=4 Ω): attach RL=4 Ω.
I = 16/(4+4) = 2 A, PL = I²RL = 4×4 = 16 W.
Check against the formula: Pmax = 16²/(4×4) = 256/16 = 16 W ✓.
Efficiency vs power The current is I = Vth/(Rth + RL), so
η = Pload/Ptotal = I²RL / [I²(Rth + RL)] = RL/(Rth + RL)
At RL = Rth, η = 1/2 = 50%. As RL → ∞, η → 100% but the power delivered drops to zero (almost no current). There's the trade-off.

A9. Superposition Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Independent source — a source whose value does not depend on anything else in the circuit.
  • Response — the voltage or current you are solving for.
  • Superposition — total response = algebraic sum of responses to each independent source acting alone.
  • Dependent source (caution) — stays in the circuit during superposition; only independent sources are killed.
Definition In a linear circuit with multiple independent sources, the response (any voltage or current) equals the sum of the responses caused by each source acting alone, with all other independent sources killed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).
Why this is true Every equation Ohm's/Kirchhoff's laws produce for a resistive circuit is linear in the source values (no squares or products of sources appear). For linear equations, the solution for a sum of inputs is exactly the sum of the solutions for each input separately — this is the defining property of linearity, and it's the same property that makes A4's node equations solvable by straightforward elimination in the first place.

The network with a 2 A source added at node A. Left: only the 24 V source acts. Right: the 24 V source is replaced by a short, so 6 Ω and 12 Ω sit in parallel. Add the node voltages: 16 + 8 = 24 V.

Worked example Add a 2 A current source injecting into node a of the A7 circuit (24 V, 6 Ω, 12 Ω). Find Va by superposition.
Step 1 — V1 acting alone (open the current source): this is exactly the A7 Thevenin calculation — Va1 = 16 V.
Step 2 — 2 A source acting alone (short the 24 V source): now 6 Ω and 12 Ω are both simply tied from node a to ground — they're in parallel, Req=(6×12)/18=4 Ω. All 2 A flows into this 4 Ω: Va2 = 2×4 = 8 V.
Total: Va = Va1 + Va2 = 16 + 8 = 24 V.
Independent check (direct node analysis with both sources active): (Va−24)/6 + Va/12 − 2 = 0 ⟹ 3Va = 72 ⟹ Va = 24 V ✓ — matches exactly.
MethodBest suited to…What you solve
Node-Voltagecircuits with many parallel branches / current sourcesn−1 equations for n nodes
Mesh-Currentplanar circuits with many series loops / voltage sourcesone equation per independent loop
Thevenin/Nortonfinding behaviour at one pair of terminals, esp. varying loadVth, Rth once, reuse for any RL

Everything above assumed pure resistors and DC (unchanging) sources. Part B repeats this exact same logic — KVL, KCL, series/parallel, even a "divider" — but the numbers become phasors (complex numbers) because inductors and capacitors react differently to changing current than resistors do.

Steps

  1. Keep one independent source; kill all the others (short voltage sources, open current sources).
  2. Solve for the required voltage/current — label it with a subscript for that source.
  3. Repeat for each source.
  4. Add the individual responses algebraically (watch signs and reference directions).
Fixing a common misconception Superposition applies to voltage and current, not to power. Power is quadratic (I²R), and (I1 + I2)² ≠ I1² + I2². Find the total current by superposition first, then compute the power.
Quick proof: with I1 = 3 A and I2 = 1 A through 2 Ω, adding the separate powers gives 18 + 2 = 20 W but the real power is (4)²·2 = 32 W.

Part B — AC Circuits Module 1, continued

B1. Generating an AC Waveform

Key terms — know these words
  • Waveform — the graph of a quantity against time.
  • Cycle — one complete set of positive and negative values of the wave.
  • Alternation — half a cycle (one positive or one negative loop).
  • Period (T) — time for one cycle; T = 1/f, in seconds.
  • Frequency (f) — cycles per second; unit hertz (Hz). Indian mains = 50 Hz.
  • Angular frequency (ω) — ω = 2πf, in rad/s. Mains: 2π × 50 ≈ 314 rad/s.
  • Instantaneous value — the value at one particular time, e.g. v(t) = Vm sin ωt.
  • Peak (maximum) value Vm — the amplitude — the largest instantaneous value.
  • Peak-to-peak value — 2Vm, from the positive crest to the negative crest.
  • Phase angle (φ) — the angular shift of a wave relative to a reference; v = Vm sin(ωt + φ).
Definition A coil rotating at constant angular speed ω inside a uniform magnetic field produces a sinusoidal EMF:
v(t) = Vm sin(ωt)
where Vm is the peak value and ω = 2πf (f = frequency in Hz).

One full rotation of the coil (360°) traces one full sine cycle: positive peak at 90°, zero at 180°, negative peak at 270°.

This same coil-in-a-field picture is exactly the DC generator principle used later (Module 2) — the only difference is a commutator "rectifies" this AC waveform into a one-directional (DC) output.
General form with phase
v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ)   ;   T = 1/f   ;   ω = 2πf
φ > 0 means the wave starts ahead (leads the reference sin ωt); φ < 0 means it lags.
Worked example — reading a waveform equation For v = 325 sin(314t + 30°): peak Vm = 325 V; ω = 314 rad/s ⟹ f = 314/2π = 50 Hz; T = 1/50 = 20 ms; phase = +30° (leads sin 314t by 30°); Vrms = 325/√2 ≈ 230 V; and at t = 0 the instantaneous value is 325 sin 30° = 162.5 V.

Lead and lag in one line

Two waves of the same frequency are compared by the angle between their zero-crossings or peaks. The one that reaches its peak earlier leads. Phase difference is only meaningful when frequencies are equal.

B2. RMS, Average, Form Factor & Peak Factor

Key terms — know these words
  • RMS (root mean square) — square it, average it over a period, take the root. Also called the effective value — the DC value that would heat a resistor equally.
  • Average (mean) value — for a symmetrical AC wave the full-cycle mean is 0, so 'average' means the mean of the rectified half-cycle.
  • Form factor — Vrms / Vavg. Sine wave: 1.11.
  • Peak factor (crest factor) — Vm / Vrms. Sine wave: 1.414.
  • Rectified wave — a wave with the negative half flipped (full-wave) or removed (half-wave).
  • Effective value — another name for RMS — it produces the same heating as the equal DC.
Fixing a common misconception "Average value of a sine wave is Vm/2" — false. Averaged over a full cycle, a sine wave's true average is exactly zero (equal area above and below the axis cancels). "Average value" in this topic always means the average of the rectified waveform, i.e. averaged over one half-cycle only.
Why Vrms = Vm/√2 (derivation) RMS = "root of the mean of the square." Square the waveform, average it over one period, then take the square root.
Vrms² = (1/T)∫0T Vm² sin²(ωt) dt
Use the identity sin²θ = (1−cos2θ)/2:
Vrms² = (Vm²/T)∫0T (1−cos2ωt)/2 dt = (Vm²/2T)[T − 0] = Vm²/2
(the cos2ωt term integrates to exactly zero over a whole number of its own periods). Taking the square root: Vrms = Vm/√2 ≈ 0.707 Vm.
Why Vavg = 2Vm/π (derivation) Average the waveform over one half-cycle (θ from 0 to π), where sinθ ≥ 0 throughout:
Vavg = (1/π)∫0π Vm sinθ dθ = (Vm/π)[−cosθ]0π = (Vm/π)(1−(−1)) = 2Vm/π ≈ 0.637 Vm

Three 'heights' of the same 100 V-peak sine wave (one half-cycle shown). RMS sits above the average because squaring weights the larger values more.

Definitions
Form Factor = Vrms/Vavg = (Vm/√2)/(2Vm/π) = π/(2√2) ≈ 1.11
Peak Factor (crest factor) = Vm/Vrms = √2 ≈ 1.414
Worked example A sinusoidal supply has Vm = 100 V. Find Vrms, Vavg, form factor, peak factor.
Vrms = 100/√2 = 70.7 V   Vavg = 2(100)/π = 63.7 V
FF = 70.7/63.7 = 1.11   PF = 100/70.7 = 1.414
Note: every household "230 V AC" or "120 V AC" rating is the RMS value — the actual peak in a 230 V mains supply is 230×√2 ≈ 325 V.
Why RMS is called the 'effective' value Heat in a resistor is P = i²R, so the average heating of an AC current is R × mean(i²). A DC current Idc gives Idc²R. Setting them equal: Idc = √mean(i²) = Irms. So a 5 A rms AC current heats a heater exactly like 5 A DC — that is why all AC ratings quote RMS.

Same recipe, other waveforms (peak = Vm)

The half-wave numbers (0.318 and 0.5) are the same ones that appear in the rectifier formulas Idc = Im/π and Irms = Im/2 later in the course.

Worked example — reading an instrument A moving-coil (average-responding) meter calibrated in RMS assumes form factor 1.11. If it reads 10 V on a sine wave, Vavg = 10/1.11 = 9.0 V. If the same meter is used on a square wave whose Vavg is actually 9.0 V, the true RMS is 9.0 × 1 = 9.0 V — but the meter still shows 10 V, an 11% error. Lesson: form factor depends on waveform shape.

B3. Phasors & Impedance

Key terms — know these words
  • Phasor — a complex number (magnitude + angle) representing a sinusoid at a known frequency.
  • Polar / rectangular form — V∠θ ↔ a + jb, where j = √−1 and a = V cos θ, b = V sin θ.
  • Impedance (Z) — V/I for AC circuits; Z = R + jX, unit ohm (Ω).
  • Reactance (X) — the imaginary part of impedance: XL = ωL (inductive, +), XC = 1/(ωC) (capacitive, −).
  • Inductor (L) / Capacitor (C) — energy-storage elements: L stores energy in a magnetic field (henry, H); C in an electric field (farad, F).
  • Leading / lagging — 'current lags voltage' means the current wave peaks later than the voltage wave.
  • Admittance (Y) — Y = 1/Z = G + jB, unit siemens (S); B is susceptance, G is conductance.
Definition A phasor is a complex number that represents a sinusoid's magnitude and phase, freezing the rotating picture of B1 at t=0. Impedance Z is the AC generalisation of resistance — the ratio of voltage phasor to current phasor:
Z = V/I = R + jX  (Ω),   |Z| = √(R²+X²),   φ = arctan(X/R)
Resistor: ZR=R (in phase). Inductor: ZL=jωL=jXL (voltage leads current by 90°). Capacitor: ZC=1/(jωC)=−jXC (voltage lags current by 90°).
Why phasors are allowed to replace calculus For a linear circuit driven by a single-frequency sinusoid, every voltage and current in steady state is a sinusoid at that same frequency — only magnitude and phase differ. Since d/dt(A sin(ωt+θ)) is again a sinusoid at frequency ω (just scaled by ω and shifted 90°), differentiation becomes multiplication by jω in the phasor domain. This turns differential equations for L and C into plain algebra — exactly the same KVL/KCL algebra as Part A, just with complex numbers standing in for the resistor-only calculation.

Phasor diagram of an inductive (RL) circuit: current is the reference; the voltage phasor sits ahead of it by the impedance angle φ.

Every diagram, formula and worked example from Part A carries over unchanged if you mentally replace every "R" with "Z" and every real number with a phasor — that's the entire content of the next section.

Converting between forms

Worked example — one impedance, both forms Z = 3 + j4 Ω = 5∠53.13° Ω. A current of 2∠0° A gives V = Z·I = 10∠53.13° V, i.e. the voltage leads the current by 53.13°. Divide back: V/I = 5∠53.13° ✓. For a 50 Hz supply, XL = 4 Ω means L = XL/ω = 4/314 = 12.7 mH.
Fixing a common misconception Reactance is not resistance: a pure inductor or capacitor dissipates no real power. Reactance limits current and shifts phase, but the energy is stored and returned each cycle, not turned into heat.

B4. Series RL, RC, RLC Circuits

Key terms — know these words
  • Series RLC circuit — R, L and C carrying the same current; total impedance Z = R + j(XL − XC).
  • Inductive circuit — XL > XC: net reactance positive, current lags.
  • Capacitive circuit — XC > XL: net reactance negative, current leads.
  • Resonance — XL = XC: reactances cancel, Z = R (minimum), current is maximum and in phase with V.
  • Resonant frequency (f0) — f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)).
  • Quality factor (Q) — at resonance Q = XL/R; a big Q means a sharp, narrow resonance peak and large L and C voltages.
  • Voltage triangle — VR, VX and V form a right triangle — the impedance triangle multiplied by I.
Definition
CircuitImpedance ZPhase angle φBehaviour
Series RLR + jXLtan⁻¹(XL/R), positivecurrent lags voltage
Series RCR − jXCtan⁻¹(XC/R), negativecurrent leads voltage
Series RLCR + j(XL−XC)tan⁻¹((XL−XC)/R)lag if XL>XC, lead if XC>XL, in phase if equal (resonance)

Series RL circuit and its impedance triangle — a 3-4-5 right triangle: resistance and reactance are the legs, impedance the hypotenuse.

Worked example A series RL circuit has R = 3 Ω, XL = 4 Ω, supplied by V = 50 V (rms). Find |Z|, φ, and I.
|Z| = √(3²+4²) = √25 = 5 Ω   φ = tan⁻¹(4/3) = 53.13° (current lags voltage)
I = V/|Z| = 50/5 = 10 A
Keep V=50 V, I=10 A, φ=53.13° — Section B5 uses this exact same circuit to compute real, reactive and apparent power.
Worked example — series RC R = 3 Ω, XC = 4 Ω, V = 50 V rms. Z = 3 − j4 ⟹ |Z| = 5 Ω, φ = −53.13° (current leads the voltage). I = 50/5 = 10 A. VR = 30 V, VC = 40 V, and these add as a right triangle: √(30² + 40²) = 50 V ✓ — not 30 + 40 = 70 V.
Worked example — series RLC R = 4 Ω, XL = 9 Ω, XC = 6 Ω, V = 100 V.
Net reactance X = 9 − 6 = 3 Ω (inductive). Z = 4 + j3 ⟹ |Z| = 5 Ω, φ = tan⁻¹(3/4) = 36.87° lagging. I = 100/5 = 20 A. cos φ = 0.8.
Individual voltages: VR = 80 V, VL = 180 V, VC = 120 V. Note VL and VC can each be larger than the supply — they partially cancel (180 − 120 = 60 V), and √(80² + 60²) = 100 V ✓.

Resonance in detail

Why f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)) XL = XC ⟹ ωL = 1/(ωC) ⟹ ω² = 1/(LC) ⟹ ω0 = 1/√(LC), so f0 = ω0/2π.
  • At resonance: Z = R (minimum), I = V/R (maximum), φ = 0, pf = 1.
  • VL and VC are equal and opposite, each = Q × V (can be many times the supply).
  • Below f0 the circuit is capacitive; above f0 it is inductive.

Current in a series RLC circuit versus frequency: a sharp peak at resonance, where XL = XC. The higher the Q, the narrower the peak.

B5. Power & Power Factor in AC Circuits

Key terms — know these words
  • Instantaneous power p(t) — p = v·i at each instant; it oscillates at twice the supply frequency.
  • Real (active) power P — the average power actually converted to heat/work; unit watt (W).
  • Reactive power Q — power swapped back and forth with L and C; unit volt-ampere-reactive (VAR). Positive for inductive loads.
  • Apparent power S — S = VrmsIrms; unit volt-ampere (VA). What cables and transformers must be rated for.
  • Power factor (pf) — cos φ = P/S, from 0 to 1.
  • Lagging / leading pf — lagging = inductive load (motors); leading = capacitive load.
  • Unity power factor — pf = 1: purely resistive; all the apparent power is real power.
  • Complex power — S = P + jQ = V·I* (I* is the complex conjugate of the current phasor).
  • Power-factor correction — adding capacitance in parallel with an inductive load to cancel Q and raise pf.
Definition
Apparent power  S = VI  (VA)   |   Real power  P = VI cosφ  (W)   |   Reactive power  Q = VI sinφ  (VAR)
S² = P² + Q²     Power factor = cosφ = P/S
Why real power involves cosφ Instantaneous power is p(t)=v(t)i(t). With v=Vmsin(ωt) and i=Imsin(ωt−φ), the product expands (via a standard trig identity) into a constant term plus a term oscillating at 2ω. Averaged over a full cycle, the oscillating term vanishes and only the constant term survives:
Pavg = (VmIm/2)cosφ = VrmsIrmscosφ
This is genuinely the only part of VI that does net work over a cycle — the rest sloshes energy back and forth into the inductor/capacitor's field and back, which is exactly what "reactive" power Q means.

The power triangle for the B4 circuit — the same 3-4-5 shape as the impedance triangle, because P, Q and S are I² times R, X and Z.

Worked example Continuing B4's circuit: V=50 V, I=10 A, φ=53.13° (cosφ=0.6, sinφ=0.8).
S = VI = 500 VA   P = 500×0.6 = 300 W   Q = 500×0.8 = 400 VAR
Check: P²+Q² = 300²+400² = 90000+160000 = 250000 = 500² = S² ✓
Fixing a common misconception A low power factor is not "wasted power" in the sense of heat — reactive power Q isn't dissipated, it's shuttled back and forth. But a low cosφ still costs money: for the same real power P, a lower cosφ demands a larger current I=P/(Vcosφ), which means thicker cables and bigger transformers just to deliver the same useful watts. That's why factories with inductive loads (motors) install capacitor banks to raise their power factor.
Every motor in Topic 2 (Electrical Machines) — DC or three-phase induction — has winding inductance, so every one of them draws a lagging power factor exactly like this RL example. The induction motor there is, electrically, just a big rotating RL/RLC load.

How each element handles power

Complex power gathers everything: S = P + jQ, with |S| = VI. Because inductive and capacitive Q have opposite signs, a capacitor can 'cancel' an inductor's Q — the basis of power-factor correction.

Worked example — power-factor correction A motor-like load draws P = 300 W at pf 0.6 lagging (from the B5 example: S = 500 VA, Q = 400 VAR). We want pf 0.8 lagging.
New angle: cos φ₂ = 0.8 ⟹ tan φ₂ = 0.75 ⟹ Q₂ = P tan φ₂ = 300 × 0.75 = 225 VAR.
Capacitor must supply QC = 400 − 225 = 175 VAR.
Effect on current: before, S = 500 VA ⟹ I = 10 A at 50 V. After, S₂ = P/cos φ₂ = 300/0.8 = 375 VA ⟹ I = 7.5 A. The supply now carries 25% less current for the same 300 W of useful power.
Fixing a common misconception Power in AC is not simply V × I with rms values — that is only the apparent power. Multiply by the power factor to get the real power that does the work. Also, add P's arithmetically and Q's algebraically; never add S's directly — S is the length of a vector.
