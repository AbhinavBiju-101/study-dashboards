A1. Ohm's Law & Circuit Elements

Key terms — know these words
  • Charge (Q) — the basic electrical quantity, measured in coulombs (C).
  • Current (I) — rate of flow of charge, I = dQ/dt; unit ampere (A = C/s). Conventional direction = the way positive charge would move (from + to −).
  • Voltage / potential difference (V) — energy per unit charge between two points; unit volt (V = J/C).
  • Resistance (R) — opposition to current flow; unit ohm (Ω). For a wire, R = ρL/A (resistivity × length ÷ area).
  • Conductance (G) — reciprocal of resistance, G = 1/R; unit siemens (S). Makes parallel-circuit sums easier.
  • Power (P) — rate of energy conversion, P = VI = I²R = V²/R; unit watt (W).
  • Energy (W) — power × time; unit joule (J). Electricity bills use the kilowatt-hour (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ).
  • Ideal voltage source — keeps its terminal voltage fixed whatever current it supplies (internal resistance = 0).
  • Ideal current source — forces a fixed current whatever voltage appears across it (internal resistance = ∞).
  • Independent / dependent source — independent: value set by itself. Dependent (controlled): value set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit (drawn as a diamond).
  • Active / passive element — active elements (sources) can deliver energy; passive elements (R, L, C) only absorb, dissipate or store it.
  • Linear element — an element whose V–I graph is a straight line through the origin (double V, double I).
  • Short circuit / open circuit — short: R = 0, V = 0, any current. Open: R = ∞, I = 0, any voltage.
  • Passive sign convention — if current enters the + terminal of an element, then P = VI > 0 means the element absorbs power; P < 0 means it delivers power.
Definition For a resistor, the voltage across it and the current through it are directly proportional:
V = I·R
where V is in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms (Ω). Equivalently, I = V/R and R = V/I.

A single-loop circuit: the source drives current I clockwise (leaving its + plate) through R. Ohm's law links V, I and R.

Fixing a common misconception Students often think "more resistance always means less current, full stop" — true only if V is fixed. If a circuit has multiple resistors, changing one resistor changes the current distribution everywhere (Section A3), not just through itself. Ohm's law is a local law for one element; predicting the whole circuit needs Kirchhoff's laws (A2).
Worked example A 12 V battery drives a 4 Ω resistor. Find the current and the power dissipated.
I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A
P = VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W (check: P = I²R = 3² × 4 = 36 W ✓ — two independent formulas, same answer)
The "compute two ways, check they agree" habit above is the same trick you'll use to verify Thevenin equivalents (A7) and to check power balance in superposition (A9).

An open circuit lets zero current flow with any voltage across it (like a switch turned off). A source is never 'shorted' safely unless it has internal resistance — a shorted ideal voltage source would demand infinite current.
Worked example — sign convention A 12 V battery drives 3 A out of its + terminal. Battery: current leaves the + terminal, so P = −VI = −36 W (it delivers 36 W). The resistor it drives: current enters its + terminal, so P = +36 W (it absorbs 36 W). Total power in the circuit = −36 + 36 = 0 — energy is conserved.

A2. Kirchhoff's Laws

Key terms — know these words
  • Node — a point where two or more circuit elements join.
  • Essential node (junction) — a node where three or more branches meet — the only nodes that need a KCL equation.
  • Branch — a single element (or a series string of elements) connecting two nodes; it carries one current.
  • Loop — any closed path that visits each node at most once.
  • Mesh — a loop that contains no other loop inside it (a 'window' of the circuit).
  • Voltage rise / drop — going through a source from − to + is a rise; going through a resistor along the current is a drop.
  • Planar circuit — a circuit that can be drawn on paper with no wires crossing — needed for mesh analysis.
Definition KCL (Current Law): the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it — charge cannot pile up at a point.
Σ Iin = Σ Iout
KVL (Voltage Law): the sum of voltage rises around any closed loop equals the sum of voltage drops.
Σ Vrises = Σ Vdrops (equivalently, Σ V = 0 around the loop)
Why this is true KCL is conservation of charge applied to a single point: charge is neither created nor stored at an ideal node, so whatever flows in must flow out.
KVL is conservation of energy applied to a charge that travels once around a loop and returns to its start: since voltage is potential energy per unit charge, and the charge is back where it began, the net change in potential energy over the round trip must be zero.

Left: KCL at a node — the arrows into the dot balance the arrow out. Right: KVL round the dashed loop — the source's rise equals the two resistors' drops.

Worked example In the KVL loop above, V = 20 V, R1 = 2 Ω, R2 = 3 Ω. Since it's a single loop, the same current I flows through everything, so:
20 = I(2) + I(3) = 5I ⟹ I = 4 A
Drop across R1 = 8 V, across R2 = 12 V. Check: 8 + 12 = 20 V ✓ (rises = drops).
KVL written once per loop is exactly the equation you write n times in Mesh-Current Analysis (A5); KCL written once per node is exactly what you write in Node-Voltage Analysis (A4). Everything from here to A9 is KCL/KVL done systematically for bigger circuits.

Steps for applying KVL to a loop

  1. Pick a direction (clockwise is conventional) and label current directions on each resistor.
  2. Walk once round the loop. Going through a source from − to + is a rise (+); through a resistor with the current is a drop (−).
  3. Write: Σ rises − Σ drops = 0.
  4. If a solved current comes out negative, it simply flows opposite to the way you drew it.
Worked example — KCL at two nodes A node has 7 A entering along one wire, and leaves as 3 A and Ix. KCL: 7 = 3 + Ix ⟹ Ix = 4 A. If Ix then splits at the next node into 1 A and Iy, Iy = 4 − 1 = 3 A.

A3. Series, Parallel & Dividers

Key terms — know these words
  • Equivalent resistance (Req) — the single resistor that draws the same current as the whole network at the same voltage.
  • Series connection — elements strung end-to-end so they carry the same current.
  • Parallel connection — elements joined across the same two nodes so they share the same voltage.
  • Voltage divider — series resistors splitting a supply voltage in proportion to their resistances.
  • Current divider — parallel resistors splitting a supply current in inverse proportion to their resistances.
  • Series–parallel network — a mix of both; reduce it step by step, innermost combination first.
Definition Series (same current through each element): Req = R1 + R2 + ⋯
Parallel (same voltage across each element): 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ⋯ (two only: Req = R1R2/(R1+R2))

Series elements share one current path end to end; parallel elements share both end nodes, so they share the same voltage.

Why this is true Series: current has only one path, so by KCL the same I flows through every element in the chain. Summing V=IR drops around the loop by KVL gives Vtotal = I(R1+R2+…) = I·Req.
Parallel: both elements touch the same pair of nodes, so by definition of voltage (potential difference between two points) they see the same V. By KCL, the source current splits: I = V/R1 + V/R2 = V(1/R1 + 1/R2) = V/Req.
Divider formulas Voltage divider (series R1, R2 across V): VR2 = V · R2/(R1+R2)
Current divider (parallel R1, R2 fed by I): IR2 = I · R1/(R1+R2) — note it's the other resistor on top.
Fixing a common misconception Students often copy the voltage-divider formula into the current-divider formula and put R2 on top for IR2 as well. It's flipped: in a current divider, the branch with the smaller resistance takes the larger share of current, so IR2's share must be weighted by the other resistor, R1.
Worked example R1 = 6 Ω and R2 = 3 Ω are in parallel, fed by a 9 A source. Find the current through each.
Req = (6×3)/(6+3) = 2 Ω, so V = I·Req = 9×2 = 18 V across both.
IR1 = 18/6 = 3 A, IR2 = 18/3 = 6 A. Check: 3 + 6 = 9 A ✓ (KCL).
Via the divider shortcut: IR2 = 9 × 6/(6+3) = 6 A ✓ — same answer without solving for V first.
Req reduction is the first move in almost every Thevenin problem (A7): reduce everything except the load to one resistor, exactly as done here.

Useful shortcuts

  • n equal resistors in parallel: Req = R/n. Two 10 Ω in parallel give 5 Ω.
  • Conductance form: Geq = G1 + G2 + … for parallel (conductances add just like series resistances do).
  • Parallel is always smaller than the smallest branch — a quick sanity check on your answer.
  • Power divides too: in series the larger R dissipates more (P = I²R); in parallel the smaller R dissipates more (P = V²/R).
Worked example — series–parallel reduction A 12 V source feeds 2 Ω in series with (6 Ω ∥ 3 Ω).
Step 1: 6 ∥ 3 = 18/9 = 2 Ω. Step 2: Rtotal = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω. Step 3: I = 12/4 = 3 A.
Voltage across the parallel pair = 3 × 2 = 6 V ⟹ 6 Ω carries 1 A, 3 Ω carries 2 A (sum = 3 A ✓). Check the divider: I3Ω = 3 × 6/(6+3) = 2 A ✓.

A4. Node-Voltage Analysis

Key terms — know these words
  • Reference node (ground) — the node chosen as 0 V; every other node voltage is measured relative to it.
  • Node voltage — the voltage of a node with respect to the reference node.
  • Supernode — two non-reference nodes joined by a voltage source; treated as one node for KCL, plus one extra equation from the source.
  • Conductance form — writing branch current as G × (voltage difference) instead of (voltage difference)/R; gives a symmetric equation matrix.
Definition Pick one node as the reference (0 V, "ground"). Assign an unknown voltage to every other node. At each unknown node, write one KCL equation using Ohm's law to express every branch current in terms of node voltages. Solve the resulting simultaneous equations.

Two unknown nodes A and B. The 6 A source injects current into A; the bottom rail is the 0 V reference.

Worked example Write KCL at each node (current leaving a node through a resistor to a lower-potential node is (Vhere−Vthere)/R):
Node A: 6 = VA/R1 + (VA−VB)/R2 = VA/4 + (VA−VB)/4
Node B: 0 = (VB−VA)/R2 + VB/R3 = (VB−VA)/4 + VB/4
From the B-equation: VB = VA/2. Substituting into the A-equation: 6 = VA/4 + (VA−VA/2)/4 = 3VA/8 ⟹ VA = 16 V, VB = 8 V.
Check (KCL at A): current out through R1 = 16/4 = 4 A; through R2 = (16−8)/4 = 2 A. Total out = 6 A = current in ✓.
Keep VA=16 V in mind — the same numbers reappear, from a totally different method, as the Thevenin voltage in A7 and the superposition example in A9. That agreement is not a coincidence; it's the same physical circuit.

Steps

  1. Mark all nodes; choose the one with the most branches as the reference (0 V).
  2. Label the remaining node voltages V1, V2, …
  3. At each unknown node write KCL: Σ (currents leaving) = 0, with each resistor current = (Vnode − Vneighbour)/R.
  4. Solve the n − 1 linear equations. Then find any branch current with Ohm's law.

Matrix (conductance) form — spot the pattern

For the A4 example (three 4 Ω resistors, G = 0.25 S each) the equations collapse to:

[ 0.5 −0.25 ; −0.25 0.5 ] · [VA ; VB] = [ 6 ; 0 ]

Diagonal = sum of conductances touching that node; off-diagonal = minus the conductance between the two nodes; right-hand side = current sources injected. You can write this by inspection — a real time-saver in exams.

Supernode — the only tricky case If a voltage source sits between two non-reference nodes, you cannot write a current through it (an ideal voltage source can carry any current). So merge the two nodes into a supernode: write one KCL for the pair, and add one extra equation V1 − V2 = Vsource.

A5. Mesh-Current Analysis

Key terms — know these words
  • Mesh current — an imaginary current circulating round one mesh; real branch currents are built from these.
  • Shared (common) branch — a branch belonging to two meshes; its real current = difference of the two mesh currents.
  • Supermesh — two meshes sharing a current source; the source's branch is left out and one KVL is written round the combined outer loop.
  • Clockwise convention — choosing every mesh current clockwise makes all the shared-resistor terms negative — a built-in error check.
Definition Assign a circulating current to each independent loop ("mesh") of a planar circuit, all in the same rotational sense (say, clockwise). Write one KVL equation per mesh; a branch shared by two meshes carries the difference of their two mesh currents. Solve simultaneously, then recover real branch currents from the mesh currents.

Two-mesh circuit. Mesh 1 (left) holds V₁, R₁ and the shared branch Rs; mesh 2 (right) holds V₂, R₂ and the same Rs. Both mesh currents are drawn clockwise, and both sources push current clockwise.

Worked example V1=16 V, R1=4 Ω, Rs=2 Ω (shared), R2=6 Ω, V2=2 V, both sources driving their mesh clockwise.
Mesh 1: V1 = I1(R1+Rs) − I2·Rs ⟹ 16 = 6I1 − 2I2
Mesh 2: V2 = I2(R2+Rs) − I1·Rs ⟹ 2 = 8I2 − 2I1
From the first equation: I2 = 3I1 − 8. Substitute into the second:
2 = 8(3I1−8) − 2I1 = 22I1 − 64 ⟹ I1 = 3 A, I2 = 1 A.
Actual current through the shared resistor Rs = I1 − I2 = 2 A (from mesh-1 side toward mesh-2 side).
Notice the pattern in the equations: each mesh's own resistors get a + sign, the shared resistor gets a −Iother·Rshared term. This is structurally identical to the node-voltage equations of A4 — swap "current source" ↔ "voltage source" and "resistor to ground" ↔ "resistor around the mesh," and it's the same bookkeeping. Two roads, same destination.

Steps

  1. Count meshes (windows). Draw a clockwise mesh current in each.
  2. Write KVL round each mesh: (sum of R in the mesh) × own current − (shared R) × neighbour current = (source rise driving that mesh).
  3. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  4. Real current in a shared branch = difference of the two mesh currents (with sign showing direction).
Supermesh — the only tricky case If a current source is a shared branch between two meshes, you can't write KVL across it (unknown voltage). Skip it: write one KVL round the big outer loop of the two meshes, and add the constraint I1 − I2 = Isource.
SituationChoose
Many current sources, few nodesNode-voltage
Many voltage sources, few meshesMesh-current
Non-planar circuitNode-voltage (mesh analysis needs planar)

A6. Source Transformation

Key terms — know these words
  • Ideal source — no internal resistance; the source transformation does not apply to an ideal source on its own.
  • Practical source — an ideal source plus its internal resistance (in series for a voltage source, in parallel for a current source).
  • Internal resistance — the resistance inside a real source that makes its terminal voltage sag under load.
  • Equivalent sources — two sources with identical open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current at the terminals.
Definition A real voltage source Vs with series resistance R is externally indistinguishable from a real current source Is = Vs/R with the same R in parallel, and vice versa (Is with parallel R ↔ Vs=IsR with series R).

12 V in series with 4 Ω looks, from terminals a–b, exactly like a 3 A source (12/4) in parallel with the same 4 Ω.

Why this is true Two black boxes are "externally indistinguishable" if they produce the same V–I relationship at the terminals. Left box: open-circuit voltage = 12 V (no current, no drop across R); short-circuit current = 12/4 = 3 A. Right box: open-circuit voltage = 3 A × 4 Ω = 12 V (all current forced through R since the external path is open); short-circuit current = 3 A (all current bypasses R through the short). Same two extreme points ⟹ same straight-line V–I relationship ⟹ identical behaviour for any load attached at a–b.
This V=IR ↔ I=V/R swap is exactly what makes Thevenin ⟷ Norton equivalents (next section) interchangeable — a Thevenin source is nothing but a voltage source, and a Norton source is its current-source transform.
Two traps 1. Direction/polarity: the current-source arrow must point toward the + terminal of the equivalent voltage source (the terminal the arrow leaves from is the + side).
2. Ideal sources: a voltage source with no series resistance (or a current source with no parallel resistance) cannot be transformed. Also, a resistor in parallel with a voltage source, or in series with a current source, does not affect what the other elements see, so it can be dropped when analysing the rest of the circuit.
Worked example — going the other way A 2 A current source in parallel with 5 Ω ⟹ voltage source V = I·R = 2 × 5 = 10 V in series with the same 5 Ω. Check: open-circuit voltage of the original = 2 × 5 = 10 V ✓; short-circuit current of the new one = 10/5 = 2 A ✓.

Why bother? Transform repeatedly to turn a ladder of mixed sources into one source with one resistor — often faster than writing any equation at all.

A7. Thevenin's & Norton's Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Linear network — a network whose elements obey superposition/proportionality; Thevenin's theorem holds only for these.
  • Terminals a–b — the two points where the load will connect and from which we 'look into' the network.
  • Open-circuit voltage (Voc) — voltage across a–b with nothing connected; equals Vth.
  • Short-circuit current (Isc) — current when a–b are shorted together; equals IN.
  • Thevenin resistance (Rth) — resistance seen at a–b with independent sources killed; also Rth = Voc/Isc.
  • Norton equivalent — current source IN in parallel with RN = Rth.
  • Killing a source — voltage source → replace by a short; current source → replace by an open circuit.
Definition Any linear resistive network, viewed from two terminals a–b, behaves exactly like a single source in series (or parallel) with a single resistance:
Thevenin: Vth in series with Rth   ⟺   Norton: IN=Vth/Rth in parallel with RN=Rth
Vth = open-circuit voltage at a–b. Rth = resistance seen at a–b with all independent sources killed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).

The 24 V / 6 Ω / 12 Ω network (load removed) collapses, as seen from a–b, to a 16 V source in series with 4 Ω.

Worked example Find Vth: with a–b open, no current flows anywhere except around the single loop 24 V–6 Ω–12 Ω: I = 24/(6+12) = 4/3 A. Voltage across the 12 Ω (this is Vab since nothing else drops between a and b) = (4/3)(12) = 16 V = Vth.
Find Rth: kill the 24 V source (replace with a short). Looking into a–b now sees 6 Ω and 12 Ω in parallel: Rth = (6×12)/(6+12) = 4 Ω.
Norton form: IN = Vth/Rth = 16/4 = 4 A, RN = 4 Ω.
Vth=16 V is the exact same number as node A's voltage in the A4 example — no surprise, since Thevenin's theorem is just node/mesh analysis packaged for one pair of terminals. Keep Vth=16 V, Rth=4 Ω on hand: A8 attaches a load here to find maximum power, and A9 re-derives this 16 V by superposition.

Procedure

  1. Remove the load; label terminals a–b.
  2. Vth: calculate the open-circuit voltage across a–b (any method).
  3. Rth: kill independent sources and find the resistance looking into a–b — or compute Rth = Voc/Isc.
  4. Draw Vth in series with Rth, reconnect the load, and solve the simple one-loop circuit.
Why the second Rth method works The Thevenin equivalent has an open-circuit voltage Vth and, when shorted, delivers Isc = Vth/Rth. Rearranging gives Rth = Voc/Isc. Useful when the network contains dependent sources: they must not be killed, so use Voc/Isc (or apply a test source) instead.
Worked example — using it to change the load With Vth = 16 V and Rth = 4 Ω (from above), what does a 12 Ω load draw?
I = 16/(4+12) = 1 A, Vload = 12 V. For a 4 Ω load: 2 A; for 28 Ω: 0.5 A. That is the power of Thevenin — solve the network once, then any load is a one-line calculation.

A8. Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Load (RL) — the element receiving power from the source network.
  • Matched load — RL = Rth; the condition for maximum power to the load.
  • Efficiency (η) — η = power in load ÷ total power supplied = RL/(Rth + RL).
  • Pmax — Vth²/(4Rth) — the peak of the power-vs-RL curve.
Definition A source with Thevenin resistance Rth delivers maximum power to a load RL when
RL = Rth
and that maximum power is Pmax = Vth² / (4Rth).
Why this is true (derivation) Current in the loop: I = Vth/(Rth+RL). Power in the load:
P = I²RL = Vth² · RL / (Rth+RL)²
Maximise P over RL by setting dP/dRL=0. Using the quotient rule on RL/(Rth+RL)²:
dP/dRL ∝ (Rth+RL)² − RL·2(Rth+RL) = (Rth+RL)[(Rth+RL) − 2RL] = (Rth+RL)(Rth−RL)
This is zero (and changes sign from + to −, confirming a maximum) exactly when RL=Rth. Substituting back: Pmax = Vth²·Rth/(2Rth)² = Vth²/(4Rth).

Power delivered to RL (Vth = 16 V, Rth = 4 Ω): it rises, peaks exactly at RL = Rth, then falls — the calculus in the box above proves the peak sits there.

Fixing a common misconception "Maximum power transfer" does not mean maximum efficiency. At RL=Rth, exactly half the total power is dissipated in Rth itself — efficiency is only 50%. Power systems engineers actually want RL ≫ Rth for high efficiency (low loss in supply resistance); matched loading is what you want in signal/communication circuits, where getting the largest possible power to a small receiver matters more than wasting some in the source.
Worked example Continuing the A7 circuit (Vth=16 V, Rth=4 Ω): attach RL=4 Ω.
I = 16/(4+4) = 2 A, PL = I²RL = 4×4 = 16 W.
Check against the formula: Pmax = 16²/(4×4) = 256/16 = 16 W ✓.
Efficiency vs power The current is I = Vth/(Rth + RL), so
η = Pload/Ptotal = I²RL / [I²(Rth + RL)] = RL/(Rth + RL)
At RL = Rth, η = 1/2 = 50%. As RL → ∞, η → 100% but the power delivered drops to zero (almost no current). There's the trade-off.

A9. Superposition Theorem

Key terms — know these words
  • Independent source — a source whose value does not depend on anything else in the circuit.
  • Response — the voltage or current you are solving for.
  • Superposition — total response = algebraic sum of responses to each independent source acting alone.
  • Dependent source (caution) — stays in the circuit during superposition; only independent sources are killed.
Definition In a linear circuit with multiple independent sources, the response (any voltage or current) equals the sum of the responses caused by each source acting alone, with all other independent sources killed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).
Why this is true Every equation Ohm's/Kirchhoff's laws produce for a resistive circuit is linear in the source values (no squares or products of sources appear). For linear equations, the solution for a sum of inputs is exactly the sum of the solutions for each input separately — this is the defining property of linearity, and it's the same property that makes A4's node equations solvable by straightforward elimination in the first place.

The network with a 2 A source added at node A. Left: only the 24 V source acts. Right: the 24 V source is replaced by a short, so 6 Ω and 12 Ω sit in parallel. Add the node voltages: 16 + 8 = 24 V.

Worked example Add a 2 A current source injecting into node a of the A7 circuit (24 V, 6 Ω, 12 Ω). Find Va by superposition.
Step 1 — V1 acting alone (open the current source): this is exactly the A7 Thevenin calculation — Va1 = 16 V.
Step 2 — 2 A source acting alone (short the 24 V source): now 6 Ω and 12 Ω are both simply tied from node a to ground — they're in parallel, Req=(6×12)/18=4 Ω. All 2 A flows into this 4 Ω: Va2 = 2×4 = 8 V.
Total: Va = Va1 + Va2 = 16 + 8 = 24 V.
Independent check (direct node analysis with both sources active): (Va−24)/6 + Va/12 − 2 = 0 ⟹ 3Va = 72 ⟹ Va = 24 V ✓ — matches exactly.

Everything above assumed pure resistors and DC (unchanging) sources. Part B repeats this exact same logic — KVL, KCL, series/parallel, even a "divider" — but the numbers become phasors (complex numbers) because inductors and capacitors react differently to changing current than resistors do.

Steps

  1. Keep one independent source; kill all the others (short voltage sources, open current sources).
  2. Solve for the required voltage/current — label it with a subscript for that source.
  3. Repeat for each source.
  4. Add the individual responses algebraically (watch signs and reference directions).
Fixing a common misconception Superposition applies to voltage and current, not to power. Power is quadratic (I²R), and (I1 + I2)² ≠ I1² + I2². Find the total current by superposition first, then compute the power.
Quick proof: with I1 = 3 A and I2 = 1 A through 2 Ω, adding the separate powers gives 18 + 2 = 20 W but the real power is (4)²·2 = 32 W.

B1. Generating an AC Waveform

Key terms — know these words
  • Waveform — the graph of a quantity against time.
  • Cycle — one complete set of positive and negative values of the wave.
  • Alternation — half a cycle (one positive or one negative loop).
  • Period (T) — time for one cycle; T = 1/f, in seconds.
  • Frequency (f) — cycles per second; unit hertz (Hz). Indian mains = 50 Hz.
  • Angular frequency (ω) — ω = 2πf, in rad/s. Mains: 2π × 50 ≈ 314 rad/s.
  • Instantaneous value — the value at one particular time, e.g. v(t) = Vm sin ωt.
  • Peak (maximum) value Vm — the amplitude — the largest instantaneous value.
  • Peak-to-peak value — 2Vm, from the positive crest to the negative crest.
  • Phase angle (φ) — the angular shift of a wave relative to a reference; v = Vm sin(ωt + φ).
Definition A coil rotating at constant angular speed ω inside a uniform magnetic field produces a sinusoidal EMF:
v(t) = Vm sin(ωt)
where Vm is the peak value and ω = 2πf (f = frequency in Hz).

One full rotation of the coil (360°) traces one full sine cycle: positive peak at 90°, zero at 180°, negative peak at 270°.

This same coil-in-a-field picture is exactly the DC generator principle used later (Module 2) — the only difference is a commutator "rectifies" this AC waveform into a one-directional (DC) output.
General form with phase
v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ)   ;   T = 1/f   ;   ω = 2πf
φ > 0 means the wave starts ahead (leads the reference sin ωt); φ < 0 means it lags.
Worked example — reading a waveform equation For v = 325 sin(314t + 30°): peak Vm = 325 V; ω = 314 rad/s ⟹ f = 314/2π = 50 Hz; T = 1/50 = 20 ms; phase = +30° (leads sin 314t by 30°); Vrms = 325/√2 ≈ 230 V; and at t = 0 the instantaneous value is 325 sin 30° = 162.5 V.

Lead and lag in one line

Two waves of the same frequency are compared by the angle between their zero-crossings or peaks. The one that reaches its peak earlier leads. Phase difference is only meaningful when frequencies are equal.

B2. RMS, Average, Form Factor & Peak Factor

Key terms — know these words
  • RMS (root mean square) — square it, average it over a period, take the root. Also called the effective value — the DC value that would heat a resistor equally.
  • Average (mean) value — for a symmetrical AC wave the full-cycle mean is 0, so 'average' means the mean of the rectified half-cycle.
  • Form factor — Vrms / Vavg. Sine wave: 1.11.
  • Peak factor (crest factor) — Vm / Vrms. Sine wave: 1.414.
  • Rectified wave — a wave with the negative half flipped (full-wave) or removed (half-wave).
  • Effective value — another name for RMS — it produces the same heating as the equal DC.
Fixing a common misconception "Average value of a sine wave is Vm/2" — false. Averaged over a full cycle, a sine wave's true average is exactly zero (equal area above and below the axis cancels). "Average value" in this topic always means the average of the rectified waveform, i.e. averaged over one half-cycle only.
Why Vrms = Vm/√2 (derivation) RMS = "root of the mean of the square." Square the waveform, average it over one period, then take the square root.
Vrms² = (1/T)∫0T Vm² sin²(ωt) dt
Use the identity sin²θ = (1−cos2θ)/2:
Vrms² = (Vm²/T)∫0T (1−cos2ωt)/2 dt = (Vm²/2T)[T − 0] = Vm²/2
(the cos2ωt term integrates to exactly zero over a whole number of its own periods). Taking the square root: Vrms = Vm/√2 ≈ 0.707 Vm.
Why Vavg = 2Vm/π (derivation) Average the waveform over one half-cycle (θ from 0 to π), where sinθ ≥ 0 throughout:
Vavg = (1/π)∫0π Vm sinθ dθ = (Vm/π)[−cosθ]0π = (Vm/π)(1−(−1)) = 2Vm/π ≈ 0.637 Vm

Three 'heights' of the same 100 V-peak sine wave (one half-cycle shown). RMS sits above the average because squaring weights the larger values more.

Definitions

Peak Factor (crest factor) = Vm/Vrms = √2 ≈ 1.414
Worked example A sinusoidal supply has Vm = 100 V. Find Vrms, Vavg, form factor, peak factor.
Vrms = 100/√2 = 70.7 V   Vavg = 2(100)/π = 63.7 V

Why RMS is called the 'effective' value Heat in a resistor is P = i²R, so the average heating of an AC current is R × mean(i²). A DC current Idc gives Idc²R. Setting them equal: Idc = √mean(i²) = Irms. So a 5 A rms AC current heats a heater exactly like 5 A DC — that is why all AC ratings quote RMS.

B3. Phasors & Impedance

Key terms — know these words
  • Phasor — a complex number (magnitude + angle) representing a sinusoid at a known frequency.
  • Polar / rectangular form — V∠θ ↔ a + jb, where j = √−1 and a = V cos θ, b = V sin θ.
  • Impedance (Z) — V/I for AC circuits; Z = R + jX, unit ohm (Ω).
  • Reactance (X) — the imaginary part of impedance: XL = ωL (inductive, +), XC = 1/(ωC) (capacitive, −).
  • Inductor (L) / Capacitor (C) — energy-storage elements: L stores energy in a magnetic field (henry, H); C in an electric field (farad, F).
  • Leading / lagging — 'current lags voltage' means the current wave peaks later than the voltage wave.
  • Admittance (Y) — Y = 1/Z = G + jB, unit siemens (S); B is susceptance, G is conductance.
Definition A phasor is a complex number that represents a sinusoid's magnitude and phase, freezing the rotating picture of B1 at t=0. Impedance Z is the AC generalisation of resistance — the ratio of voltage phasor to current phasor:
Z = V/I = R + jX  (Ω),   |Z| = √(R²+X²),   φ = arctan(X/R)
Resistor: ZR=R (in phase). Inductor: ZL=jωL=jXL (voltage leads current by 90°). Capacitor: ZC=1/(jωC)=−jXC (voltage lags current by 90°).
Why phasors are allowed to replace calculus For a linear circuit driven by a single-frequency sinusoid, every voltage and current in steady state is a sinusoid at that same frequency — only magnitude and phase differ. Since d/dt(A sin(ωt+θ)) is again a sinusoid at frequency ω (just scaled by ω and shifted 90°), differentiation becomes multiplication by jω in the phasor domain. This turns differential equations for L and C into plain algebra — exactly the same KVL/KCL algebra as Part A, just with complex numbers standing in for the resistor-only calculation.

B4. Series RL, RC, RLC Circuits

Key terms — know these words
  • Series RLC circuit — R, L and C carrying the same current; total impedance Z = R + j(XL − XC).
  • Inductive circuit — XL > XC: net reactance positive, current lags.
  • Capacitive circuit — XC > XL: net reactance negative, current leads.
  • Resonance — XL = XC: reactances cancel, Z = R (minimum), current is maximum and in phase with V.
  • Resonant frequency (f0) — f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)).
  • Quality factor (Q) — at resonance Q = XL/R; a big Q means a sharp, narrow resonance peak and large L and C voltages.
  • Voltage triangle — VR, VX and V form a right triangle — the impedance triangle multiplied by I.

Resonance in detail

Why f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)) XL = XC ⟹ ωL = 1/(ωC) ⟹ ω² = 1/(LC) ⟹ ω0 = 1/√(LC), so f0 = ω0/2π.
  • At resonance: Z = R (minimum), I = V/R (maximum), φ = 0, pf = 1.
  • VL and VC are equal and opposite, each = Q × V (can be many times the supply).
  • Below f0 the circuit is capacitive; above f0 it is inductive.

B5. Power & Power Factor in AC Circuits

Key terms — know these words
  • Instantaneous power p(t) — p = v·i at each instant; it oscillates at twice the supply frequency.
  • Real (active) power P — the average power actually converted to heat/work; unit watt (W).
  • Reactive power Q — power swapped back and forth with L and C; unit volt-ampere-reactive (VAR). Positive for inductive loads.
  • Apparent power S — S = VrmsIrms; unit volt-ampere (VA). What cables and transformers must be rated for.
  • Power factor (pf) — cos φ = P/S, from 0 to 1.
  • Lagging / leading pf — lagging = inductive load (motors); leading = capacitive load.
  • Unity power factor — pf = 1: purely resistive; all the apparent power is real power.
  • Complex power — S = P + jQ = V·I* (I* is the complex conjugate of the current phasor).
  • Power-factor correction — adding capacitance in parallel with an inductive load to cancel Q and raise pf.
Definition
Apparent power  S = VI  (VA)   |   Real power  P = VI cosφ  (W)   |   Reactive power  Q = VI sinφ  (VAR)
S² = P² + Q²     Power factor = cosφ = P/S
Why real power involves cosφ Instantaneous power is p(t)=v(t)i(t). With v=Vmsin(ωt) and i=Imsin(ωt−φ), the product expands (via a standard trig identity) into a constant term plus a term oscillating at 2ω. Averaged over a full cycle, the oscillating term vanishes and only the constant term survives:
Pavg = (VmIm/2)cosφ = VrmsIrmscosφ
This is genuinely the only part of VI that does net work over a cycle — the rest sloshes energy back and forth into the inductor/capacitor's field and back, which is exactly what "reactive" power Q means.

The power triangle for the B4 circuit — the same 3-4-5 shape as the impedance triangle, because P, Q and S are I² times R, X and Z.

Worked example Continuing B4's circuit: V=50 V, I=10 A, φ=53.13° (cosφ=0.6, sinφ=0.8).
S = VI = 500 VA   P = 500×0.6 = 300 W   Q = 500×0.8 = 400 VAR
Check: P²+Q² = 300²+400² = 90000+160000 = 250000 = 500² = S² ✓
Fixing a common misconception A low power factor is not "wasted power" in the sense of heat — reactive power Q isn't dissipated, it's shuttled back and forth. But a low cosφ still costs money: for the same real power P, a lower cosφ demands a larger current I=P/(Vcosφ), which means thicker cables and bigger transformers just to deliver the same useful watts. That's why factories with inductive loads (motors) install capacitor banks to raise their power factor.
Every motor in Part C (Electrical Machines) — DC or three-phase induction — has winding inductance, so every one of them draws a lagging power factor exactly like this RL example. The induction motor there is, electrically, just a big rotating RL/RLC load.

C1. DC Motor: Construction

Key terms — know these words
  • Yoke — the outer frame (cast/rolled steel): supports the poles, protects the machine and carries the magnetic flux between poles.
  • Field system — the stationary part that produces the main flux: an even number of pole cores bolted to the yoke, with field winding on each.
  • Salient pole — a pole that projects inward towards the armature.
  • Pole core / pole shoe — the core carries the field coil; the curved shoe spreads the flux over the armature, supports the coil and lowers reluctance.
  • Field winding — the coils on the poles that, when energised, create alternate N and S poles.
  • Armature — the rotating part: core + winding; it carries the current that interacts with the field to make torque.
  • Armature core — slotted cylinder of insulated soft-steel laminations mounted on the shaft.
  • Armature winding — insulated conductors placed in the slots and suitably interconnected — lap winding or wave winding.
  • Commutator — a mechanical rectifier/inverter of wedge-shaped copper segments insulated by mica; connects the rotating armature to the fixed supply.
  • Brushes / brush holder — carbon blocks pressed on the commutator by springs; they carry current between the supply and the armature.
  • Air gap — the small gap between pole shoe and armature across which the flux passes.
  • Lamination — thin insulated steel sheet; stacking them breaks up the paths for eddy currents.
  • Eddy current — circulating currents induced inside the iron by changing flux; they only waste heat, so they are minimised.
  • Spider — a spoked frame on which laminations are mounted in large machines instead of being keyed directly to the shaft.
Definition A DC motor has four essential parts: the yoke (outer magnetic frame), the field system (poles + field winding, produces the magnetic flux), the armature (rotating core + winding, carries current and experiences force), and the commutator + brushes (mechanically reverse the current in each armature coil every half-turn).

Cross-section of a two-pole DC machine: the yoke carries flux between the N and S poles; conductors in the armature slots carry current straight along the shaft, in opposite directions under the two poles.

The commutator here is doing the reverse job of the diode bridge you'd see in a rectifier — both take an alternating quantity and make it one-directional. The DC generator (same machine, opposite energy flow) uses exactly this commutator to turn the AC EMF from B1's rotating-coil picture into DC.

C2. DC Motor: Working Principle & Back EMF

Key terms — know these words
  • Torque (T) — the turning effort of the shaft; T ∝ Φ·Ia.
  • Fleming's left-hand rule — for MOTORS: thuMb = Motion (force), First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current.
  • Fleming's right-hand rule — for GENERATORS: thuMb = Motion, First finger = Field, seCond finger = induced EMF/current.
  • Back EMF (counter EMF), Eb — the EMF induced in the armature of a running motor; it opposes the supply voltage.
  • Armature current (Ia) and resistance (Ra) — the current in, and resistance of, the armature winding.
  • Commutation — the reversal of current in an armature coil as it passes from under one pole to the next.
  • Motoring action — electrical input → mechanical output (supply pushes current against Eb).
  • Generating action — mechanical input → electrical output (E drives current out to the load).
  • Starter — a series resistor (or electronic controller) that limits the very large starting current.
  • Speed equation — N ∝ Eb/Φ — speed rises with back-EMF and falls with flux.
Definition — force on a current-carrying conductor
F = BIL sinθ  (N)
where B is flux density (T), I is conductor current (A), L is conductor length in the field (m), θ the angle between conductor and field (usually 90°, so F=BIL). Direction: Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motors): thuMb = Motion/force, First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current.

Why the motor also generates a back EMF The instant the armature conductor starts moving (because of the force above), it is itself a conductor moving through a magnetic field — which is precisely the generator condition of B1/C1. By Lenz's law this induced EMF must oppose the very current that created the motion, so it is called back EMF, Eb, and it grows with speed N and flux Φ (Eb ∝ ΦN). The armature circuit equation is:
V = Eb + IaRa
This is nothing but Ohm's law / KVL from Part A applied to the armature loop: supply voltage = back EMF (a voltage source opposing V) + the IaRa drop across armature resistance.
Fixing a common misconception "Back EMF wastes energy" — no. Back EMF is exactly how the motor converts electrical power into mechanical power: the electrical power associated with it, EbIa, is the mechanical power developed (minus small losses). Only the Ia²Ra term is a genuine (heat) loss.
Worked example A DC motor has V=220 V, Ra=1 Ω, and draws Ia=20 A. Find Eb and the mechanical power developed.
Eb = V − IaRa = 220 − 20(1) = 200 V
Mechanical power = EbIa = 200×20 = 4000 W = 4 kW
Input power = VIa = 220×20 = 4400 W. Armature copper loss = Ia²Ra = 400 W. Check: 4000+400=4400 W ✓.

Working principle, step by step

  1. Connect the DC supply: the field winding is energised and creates alternate N and S poles.
  2. The armature conductors carry current — in one direction under the N pole, in the opposite direction under the S pole.
  3. Each current-carrying conductor sits in a magnetic field, so a force F = BIL acts on it (Fleming's left-hand rule). For the layout shown in the slides, this tends to move each conductor anticlockwise.
  4. The forces on all conductors add to give a driving torque that turns the armature.
  5. As a conductor moves from under one pole to the next, the commutator reverses its current — and the new pole is of opposite polarity, so the force direction stays the same.
  6. Result: continuous rotation in one direction.

Torque and speed relations

T ∝ Φ · Ia   |   Eb ∝ Φ · N   |   N ∝ Eb/Φ = (V − IaRa)/Φ

Weaken the flux → the motor speeds up; raise the supply voltage → the motor speeds up. These two 'knobs' are how DC-motor speed is controlled.

Why a DC motor needs a starter At the instant of switching on, the armature is at rest, so N = 0 and Eb = 0. The armature circuit equation V = Eb + IaRa then gives Ia = V/Ra. Since Ra is tiny (a fraction of an ohm), that current is enormous. E.g. 220 V and Ra = 0.5 Ω gives 440 A versus a normal 20 A. A starter resistor limits it and is cut out as Eb builds up.
Worked example — speed change with load Motor: V = 220 V, Ra = 0.5 Ω, Ia = 20 A, N = 1000 rpm, flux constant.
Eb1 = 220 − 20 × 0.5 = 210 V.
Load is reduced and Ia falls to 10 A: Eb2 = 220 − 10 × 0.5 = 215 V.
Since N ∝ Eb (Φ constant): N2 = 1000 × 215/210 = 1024 rpm. Speed rises slightly at light load — this small change is what 'good speed regulation' means.

With many coils and many commutator segments, the ripples smooth out to nearly steady DC.

A single-loop generator: the loop's EMF alternates every half-turn; the commutator swaps the brush connections at each zero crossing so the load sees a one-directional, pulsating output.

Key terms — know these words

  • Self-excited — field winding fed from the machine's own armature supply (shunt, series, compound).

C4. Transformer: Construction

Key terms — know these words
  • Primary / secondary winding — the winding connected to the source / the winding connected to the load.
  • Core — the laminated silicon-steel path that carries the mutual flux.
  • Limb / yoke (transformer) — limb: the part of the core carrying a winding; yoke: the part that joins the limbs.
  • Mutual inductance — the property by which a changing current in one coil induces an EMF in a neighbouring coil.
  • Turns ratio — N1/N2, the number of primary turns to secondary turns.
  • Step-up / step-down — step-up: V2 > V1 (N2 > N1). Step-down: V2 < V1.
  • Core-type / shell-type — windings around the limbs of a two-limb core / windings on the centre limb of a three-limb core.
  • Cylindrical (concentric) winding — LV coil next to the core, HV coil concentric over it — used in core-type.
  • Sandwich (disc) winding — LV and HV sections stacked alternately — used in shell-type.
  • Leakage flux — flux that links only one winding; it is minimised by winding layout.
  • Hysteresis / eddy-current loss — core losses; silicon steel cuts the first, lamination cuts the second.
  • Excitation (magnetising) current — the small no-load primary current needed to set up the flux.
Definition A transformer has a laminated silicon-steel core (thin, insulated sheets — reduces eddy-current loss) carrying two windings, primary and secondary, linked by the same magnetic flux but with no electrical connection between them. Core type: windings surround two limbs of a rectangular core. Shell type: both windings sit on the central limb of a three-limb core.

Core-type construction: the alternating flux Φ set up by the primary circulates round the core and links the secondary winding on the opposite limb.

Working principle (mutual induction)

  1. An alternating voltage V1 is applied to the primary; a small alternating current flows and sets up an alternating flux Φ in the core.
  2. This flux links both windings, so by Faraday's law it induces EMF E1 in the primary and E2 in the secondary.
  3. The EMFs are proportional to the numbers of turns: E2/E1 = N2/N1.
  4. Connect a load across the secondary and a current I2 flows; power passes from the primary circuit to the secondary circuit magnetically, with no electrical connection and no change in frequency.
Ideal-transformer relations
V1/V2 = N1/N2 = I2/I1   (V1I1 = V2I2)
A transformer that steps voltage up steps current down by the same ratio; power in = power out (for an ideal one). If N1 > N2 it is step-down; if N2 > N1 it is step-up.

Why put LV next to the core? The low-voltage winding needs less insulation from the grounded core than the high-voltage one. Putting LV closest to the core and wrapping HV over it means only the LV coil needs to be insulated from the core, and the HV winding is insulated by the LV winding and its own insulation — saving space and cost.
Worked example — ideal transformer currents A 2200 V / 220 V, 10 kVA transformer. Turns ratio = 2200/220 = 10 (step-down). Rated primary current = 10 000/2200 = 4.55 A; rated secondary current = 10 000/220 = 45.5 A — ten times larger, matching I2/I1 = N1/N2 = 10.

Losses and applications

  • Core (iron) loss: hysteresis (reduced by silicon steel) + eddy current (reduced by laminations); roughly constant regardless of load.
  • Copper loss: I²R in the windings; grows with load.
  • Uses: stepping voltage up/down for power transmission and distribution; supplying suitable voltages to electronic devices (TV etc.) and low-voltage electronic circuits; stepping up voltage in home inverter systems.
Fixing a common misconception A transformer does not work on DC. With a steady primary current the flux is constant, dΦ/dt = 0, and no EMF is induced in the secondary. Worse, the winding resistance alone limits DC current, which can burn the primary out. Only changing flux induces voltage.

C5. Transformer: EMF Equation

Key terms — know these words
  • Flux (Φ, Φm) — magnetic flux in webers (Wb); Φm is its peak value.
  • Faraday's law — induced EMF = rate of change of flux linkage: e = −N dΦ/dt.
  • RMS EMF (E) — E = 4.44 f Φm N — the effective value of the induced EMF.
  • Form factor — 1.11 for a sine wave; note 4 × 1.11 = 4.44, the constant in the EMF equation.
  • Phase of EMF — e lags the flux by 90°.
  • EMF per turn — E/N = 4.44 f Φm — the same in both windings.
Derivation Assume a sinusoidal mutual flux Φ = Φm sin(ωt). By Faraday's law, the EMF induced in a winding of N turns is:
e = −N dΦ/dt = −NΦmω cos(ωt)
The magnitude of this EMF peaks at Em = NΦmω = NΦm(2πf). Just as in B2, the RMS value of a sinusoid is its peak divided by √2:
E = Em/√2 = 2πfΦmN/√2 = 4.44 f Φm N
Applying this to both windings: E1=4.44fΦmN1, E2=4.44fΦmN2. Since the same Φm and f appear in both, dividing gives the turns-ratio rule:
E1/E2 = N1/N2
The "4.44" is nothing mysterious — it's 2π/√2 (≈4.44), the exact same √2 that converted peak to RMS back in B2. Anywhere you see 4.44 in a machines formula, a sinusoidal-flux EMF derivation like this one is hiding behind it.
Worked example A transformer has N1=1000 turns, N2=250 turns, is supplied at 50 Hz, and the core flux is Φm=0.02 Wb. Find E1, E2, and state whether it's step-up or step-down.
E1 = 4.44×50×0.02×1000 = 4440 V
E2 = 4.44×50×0.02×250 = 1110 V
Check via turns ratio: E1/E2 = 1000/250 = 4, and 4440/1110 = 4 ✓. Since N2<N1 (E2<E1), this is a step-down transformer.
Where does 4.44 come from — a second way For a sinusoidal flux the average EMF over a quarter cycle is 4 f Φm N (flux swings from 0 to Φm in T/4 = 1/(4f)). The RMS value = form factor × average = 1.11 × 4fΦmN = 4.44 fΦmN. It agrees with the derivation in the box above (2π/√2 = 4.44), and it shows why the form factor from the AC-circuits topic matters here.
Phase relations Because e = −N dΦ/dt and Φ = Φm sin ωt, the EMF is −NΦmω cos ωt = NΦmω sin(ωt − 90°). So the induced EMF lags the flux by 90°, and E1 and E2 are in phase with each other (the same flux induces both). The primary draws a small magnetising current in phase with the flux.
Worked example — find the turns A 220 V, 50 Hz primary; peak core flux Φm = 0.01 Wb. Neglect winding drop so E1 ≈ 220 V.
N1 = E1/(4.44 f Φm) = 220/(4.44 × 50 × 0.01) = 220/2.22 ≈ 99 ≈ 100 turns.
For a 22 V secondary: N2 = N1 × 22/220 = 10 turns.
What happens at a lower frequency? For a given voltage, Φm = E/(4.44 f N). If the frequency drops (say a 50 Hz transformer on a 25 Hz supply), Φm would double, pushing the core into saturation and causing very large magnetising current. That is why transformers are rated for a specific frequency, and why a 50 Hz design can't be freely used at lower frequency.

C6. Three-Phase Induction Motor

Key terms — know these words
  • Stator — the stationary part: steel frame + slotted silicon-steel core + three-phase winding.
  • Rotor — the rotating part, either squirrel-cage or wound.
  • Rotating magnetic field (RMF) — a field of constant magnitude that rotates in space, produced by a balanced three-phase supply.
  • Synchronous speed (Ns) — the speed of the RMF, Ns = 120f/P rpm.
  • Slip (s) — s = (Ns − Nr)/Ns; typically 2–5% at full load.
  • Poles (P) — number of magnetic poles wound into the stator; more poles = slower speed.
  • Squirrel-cage rotor — rotor bars (copper/aluminium) short-circuited by end rings.
  • End rings — the rings that short-circuit the rotor bars at both ends.
  • Slip rings — insulated rings on the shaft of a wound rotor that connect the rotor winding to external resistors through brushes.
  • Wound (slip-ring) rotor — rotor with a three-phase winding (star-connected) brought out to slip rings.
  • Induction (asynchronous) motor — a motor whose rotor current is induced by the stator field, and which never quite reaches synchronous speed.
  • Inrush (starting) current — the very large current drawn when the motor is first switched on, often 5–7× rated.
Definition The stator carries three windings spaced 120° apart in space, fed by three currents 120° apart in time (a balanced 3-phase supply). Their combined magnetomotive force produces a magnetic field of constant magnitude that rotates at synchronous speed:
Ns = 120f/P  (rpm),   P = number of poles
This rotating field cuts the stationary rotor bars, inducing currents in them (transformer action — no electrical connection needed, hence "induction" motor); those currents, in the rotating field, experience a force (Fleming's left-hand rule again) that drags the rotor around.

Stator construction

  • A cylindrical frame of fabricated steel encloses a hollow core of thin silicon-steel laminations (silicon steel and lamination together reduce hysteresis and eddy-current loss).
  • Evenly spaced slots on the inner surface hold the stator winding.
  • Three windings, connected in star or delta, form a balanced three-phase winding, wound for a definite number of poles.
  • More poles means lower speed (Ns = 120f/P): 2 poles → 3000 rpm, 4 → 1500, 6 → 1000, 8 → 750 (at 50 Hz).

Why the field rotates

The three winding currents are 120° apart in time. Watch what they do at a few instants (Im = peak current):

Three balanced currents 120° apart. Each phase peaks in turn (A, then B, then C), and at any instant the three sum to zero.

How the rotor is driven

  1. The rotating field sweeps past the stationary rotor conductors and induces EMF in them (Faraday's law).
  2. The rotor circuit is closed (short-circuited bars, or external resistors for a wound rotor), so rotor currents flow.
  3. These currents lie in the stator's magnetic field, so each experiences a force (F = BIL) — together giving torque in the direction of the field's rotation (Lenz's law: the rotor 'chases' the change).
  4. The rotor speeds up but can never catch the field: at Nr = Ns there would be no relative motion, no induced EMF, no current and no torque. Hence slip.
Nr = Ns(1 − s)   |   rotor-current frequency fr = s·f
Worked example — 6-pole motor 50 Hz supply, 6 poles: Ns = 120 × 50/6 = 1000 rpm. At 3% slip, Nr = 1000 × 0.97 = 970 rpm, and the rotor current frequency = 0.03 × 50 = 1.5 Hz. At start (s = 1) the rotor frequency equals the supply frequency, 50 Hz.
Fixing a common misconception 'Induction motors run at synchronous speed' — no, that is a synchronous motor. An induction motor is called asynchronous because it always runs a few percent slower. Also, to reverse an induction motor you swap any two of the three supply leads — that reverses the direction the field rotates.

C7. Single-Phase Induction Motor

Key terms — know these words
  • Pulsating field — the field of one winding on single-phase AC: fixed axis, magnitude varying sinusoidally.
  • Forward / backward flux (φf, φb) — the two equal, oppositely rotating halves of a pulsating field.
  • Main winding — low resistance, high reactance winding that stays in circuit.
  • Auxiliary (starting) winding — second winding, 90° away in space, used to make a rotating field at start.
  • Centrifugal switch — disconnects the starting winding at about 70–80% of synchronous speed.
  • Capacitor-start / CSCR / PSC — ways of making the auxiliary current lead the main current.
  • Shaded-pole — copper rings on part of each pole delay the flux there, giving a weak rotating effect.

Used where only single-phase supply exists: fans, hair-dryers, washing machines, vacuum cleaners, mixers, refrigerators, pumps. Rating is fractional kW. The rotor is a squirrel cage and the stator has a main winding and (except in shaded-pole types) an auxiliary winding in space quadrature; Ns = 120f/P as before.

Why it is not self-startingA single winding on AC produces a field that merely pulsates along one axis. That pulsating field equals two fields φf and φb of half amplitude rotating in opposite directions. At standstill they produce equal and opposite torques, so the net starting torque is zero — the motor hums but does not turn. Once pushed in either direction, the rotor sees one component more than the other and keeps accelerating. So a starting aid is required.

C8. BLDC Motor (Brushless DC)

Key terms — know these words
  • BLDC — brushless DC motor: DC-supplied, electronically commutated.
  • Permanent-magnet rotor — rotor carries the magnets; no windings, no brushes.

C9. Stepper Motor

Key terms — know these words
  • Step angle — the rotation per pulse; typically 0.9°–1.8° (200–400 steps/rev).
  • Open-loop control — pulses are counted; no position feedback.
  • PM / VR / hybrid — permanent-magnet, variable-reluctance and hybrid stepper types.
  • Phase — one stator winding set that is energised as a unit.
  • Missed step — a step lost because of overload; undetectable in open loop.
  • Holding torque — torque at standstill with the winding energised.

A stator with several windings is energised in a programmed sequence by an electronic driver; the permanent-magnet (or toothed soft-iron) rotor aligns with the energised pole. Each pulse gives one step; rotor angle ∝ number of pulses, speed ∝ pulse frequency.

Part D — Power Electronics & Electric Drives concepts

D1. Concepts of Power Electronics

Key terms — know these words
  • Power electronics — use of semiconductor switches to convert and control electrical power.
  • Converter — a circuit changing the form of electrical energy (AC/DC, voltage level, frequency).
  • Rectifier / Inverter / Chopper — AC→DC / DC→AC / DC→DC converters.
  • SCR (thyristor), MOSFET, IGBT — common switching devices; the BJT and diode are also used.
  • PWM — pulse-width modulation: varying the ON fraction (duty cycle) to control the average output.
  • Duty cycle D — ON time ÷ period.
  • Harmonics — unwanted extra frequencies produced by switching.

Power electronics manages and converts electrical energy using semiconductor devices. The key idea is to run the devices as switches (fully ON or fully OFF), because an ideal switch dissipates almost no power: ON, it has ≈ 0 V across it; OFF, it carries ≈ 0 A. This is why converters are efficient.

A power-electronic system: the converter shapes energy from the source for the load, while sensor and control circuit close a feedback loop around it.

The four basic converter families of power electronics, named by what goes in and what comes out.

Chopper (DC–DC step-down): Vout = D · Vin   ,   D = ton / T
Worked exampleFrom a 48 V battery a chopper with D = 0.25 gives 0.25 × 48 = 12 V average. A linear regulator dropping 48 V to 12 V at 2 A would waste (48−12)×2 = 72 W of the 96 W drawn (η = 25%), whereas a switching converter loses only a few watts (η ≈ 90%+). The 'DC–DC buck converter speed control of a car wiper motor' lab uses exactly this.

Control methods: PWM adjusts the width of switching pulses. Side effect: switching creates harmonic distortion in currents and voltages, which filters and good design must manage.

D2. Industrial Applications of Electrical Drives

Key terms — know these words
  • Electric drive — system that converts electrical energy to controlled mechanical motion.
  • VFD (variable-frequency drive) — converter that varies the frequency and voltage fed to an AC motor to control its speed.
  • V/f control — keeping voltage ∝ frequency so the motor flux stays constant.
  • Regenerative braking — the motor acts as a generator, returning energy to the supply/battery.
  • Affinity laws — for fans and pumps: flow ∝ N, pressure ∝ N², power ∝ N³.
  • Feedback loop — sensor → control unit → converter, so the drive follows the reference.

Block diagram of an electric drive: the control unit compares the command with sensed motor/load quantities and tells the converter what to deliver to the motor.

A VFD rectifies the supply, then inverts it to AC of adjustable frequency and voltage; since Ns = 120f/P, changing f changes the motor speed smoothly. Keeping V/f constant keeps the flux (and torque capability) constant.

Worked example4-pole motor, 400 V at 50 Hz: Ns = 1500 rpm. At 30 Hz: Ns = 120×30/4 = 900 rpm and V = 400×30/50 = 240 V (V/f constant).
Energy saving: a 10 kW fan run at 80% speed needs 10 × 0.8³ = 5.12 kW — about half the power for 20% less flow, which is why VFDs are used on pumps and fans instead of throttling.
  • Manufacturing and automation: conveyors, pumps, fans — speed matched to the process, saving energy and giving better control.
  • Robotics: precise, dynamic speed and position control of joints.
  • Electric vehicles: battery DC → AC for the motor; regenerative braking recovers energy.
  • Others: lifts, escalators, industrial mixers, cranes.

Part E — Electrical Safety & Earthing safety

E1. Earthing

Key terms — know these words
  • Earthing — connecting exposed metal parts of an installation to the general mass of earth through a low-resistance conductor.
  • Fault current — abnormal current from a leak or short circuit.
  • Earth electrode — plate, rod or pipe buried in the ground.
  • Touch voltage — the voltage a person touching a faulty metal body would experience.
  • Equipotential — all metal parts held at nearly the same potential, so no dangerous voltage difference.
  • Protective device — fuse, MCB, ELCB/RCCB that disconnects the supply on a fault.
DefinitionThe principle of earthing is to provide a low-resistance path to earth for fault currents. If a live wire leaks or shorts to a metal case, the current then runs safely into the ground, harming nobody, and the large current operates the protective device.

A live wire touches the metal body. Left: the body becomes live and the person offers the only path to earth. Right: a low-resistance earth wire carries the fault current instead, and the protective device disconnects the supply.

Worked exampleWithout earthing, a metal body at 230 V stays live until someone touches it; a body of 1 kΩ then carries 230 mA — in the fatal range (E2). With a 1 Ω earth wire in parallel with the person (1000 Ω), the current divider (A3) sends 1000/1001 ≈ 99.9% of the fault current through the earth wire; the large fault current blows the fuse / trips the MCB.

E2. Electrical Hazards & Safety

Key terms — know these words
  • Electric shock — current passing through the body.
  • Burns — heating of tissue by current or by an arc.
  • Arc (arc flash) — a sudden discharge through air that releases intense heat and light.
  • Fibrillation — uncontrolled twitching of the heart that stops circulation.
  • Insulation — non-conducting covering that keeps live parts from contact.
  • Fuse / MCB — devices that interrupt a circuit on overcurrent.
  • PPE — personal protective equipment: insulating gloves, shoes, mats, face shield.
  • Shock
  • Burns
  • Arc
  • Explosions
  • Fires

Common causes of electrocution: touching overhead lines; maintenance on live equipment; damaged equipment (extension leads, plugs, sockets); equipment affected by rain or water.

Effect of AC current on the human body (bands are not to scale). Currents of only tens of milliamperes through the chest are dangerous — far below what a fuse is designed to notice.

Worked exampleBody resistance depends on skin condition: dry ≈ 100 kΩ, wet or sweaty only ≈ 1 kΩ. At 230 V: dry skin → 2.3 mA (a tingle), wet skin → 230 mA (fatal). The same supply is harmless or deadly depending on the circuit through the body — which is why water and electricity are such a dangerous combination.

Preventing electrical accidents

  • Insulation on all live conductors.
  • Electrical protective devices — fuses, circuit breakers, earth-leakage devices (RCCB/ELCB) that cut the supply in milliseconds.
  • Guarding — live parts enclosed so accidental contact is impossible.
  • Earthing & grounding (E1).
  • Personal protective equipment — gloves, insulated shoes and mats, tools.
Fixing a common misconceptionA fuse or MCB protects the wiring and equipment from overload and short circuit; it does not reliably protect a person, because a fatal 100 mA is far below its rating. Personal protection needs an earth-leakage device (which trips at about 30 mA).

P1. PN Junction Diode

Key terms — know these words
  • Semiconductor — material between conductor and insulator (Si, Ge); conductivity set by doping.
  • P-type / N-type — doped with acceptor / donor impurity; majority carriers holes / electrons.
  • Majority / minority carriers — the dominant carrier type / the other type in a doped region.
  • Diffusion — carriers moving from high to low concentration across the junction.
  • Depletion layer — region near the junction emptied of free carriers, holding fixed ions.
  • Barrier potential (V₀) — ≈ 0.7 V (Si), 0.3 V (Ge).
  • Forward / reverse bias — + on P and − on N / the opposite.
  • Saturation current (Is) — tiny reverse current due to minority carriers.
  • Knee (cut-in) voltage — forward voltage where current starts to rise rapidly.

A junction diode has one half doped p-type (mobile holes) and the other n-type (mobile electrons). Holes diffuse into the n-side and electrons into the p-side; the region A near the junction on the p-side acquires an excess of negative charge and region B on the n-side a surplus of positive charge. These charges repel further migration and create a potential barrier.

A PN junction at equilibrium: diffusion leaves fixed negative ions on the P side and fixed positive ions on the N side. The resulting field (and potential barrier) stops further diffusion.

Forward bias (+ on P) pushes carriers toward the junction and shrinks the depletion layer, so current flows. Reverse bias (+ on N) pulls carriers away and widens the layer, so only a tiny leakage current flows.

Forward bias: drift of holes toward the junction in the p-region and of electrons the other way in the n-region; they combine near the junction while the battery keeps supplying new carriers — current is hole flow in the p-region, electron flow in the n-region, and a mix near the junction. Reverse bias: holes and electrons are pulled away, widening the depletion layer, which behaves as an insulator; only a small current due to minority carriers (thermally generated electron–hole pairs, life ≈ 50 µs in Si, 100 µs in Ge) flows — the saturation current.

V–I characteristic of a junction diode: almost nothing flows until the forward knee (≈ 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium); in reverse only a tiny leakage flows until breakdown.

P2. Zener Diode

Key terms — know these words
  • Zener diode — diode designed for use in reverse breakdown (4–75 V typical).
  • Breakdown voltage VZ — the nearly constant voltage across the diode after breakdown (reference voltage).
  • Zener / avalanche effect — the two breakdown mechanisms (Zener below ≈5 V, avalanche above).
  • IZ(min) / IZ(max) — minimum current to stay in regulation / maximum before power limit.
  • PZ — maximum power rating of the Zener.

Special junction diode — voltage-regulator diode: reverse breakdown voltage in the range of about 4 V to 75 V depending on the type. Forward-biased it behaves as a normal diode. As the reverse voltage rises, a value is reached where the current rises very rapidly because the increased velocity of the carriers causes ionisation. The voltage across the diode after breakdown is the reference voltage and stays practically constant over a wide range of current provided the maximum junction temperature is not exceeded. It is heavily doped, which gives a thin depletion layer and a sharp, low breakdown voltage.

Zener diode V–I curve: forward it behaves like an ordinary diode; in reverse the voltage is pinned near VZ over a wide range of current, which is what makes it a voltage reference.

P3. Voltage Regulator (Zener shunt regulator)

Key terms — know these words
  • Regulation — keeping the output voltage constant.
  • Line regulation — stability when the input voltage changes.
  • Load regulation — stability when the load current changes.
  • Rs — series (current-limiting) resistor.
  • Shunt regulator — regulating element in parallel with the load.

Rs is in series with the Zener to limit the current, Vs being across the combination; the stabilised output is taken across the Zener (Vout = VZ) and the load RL is in parallel with it. There is a minimum Zener current for which regulation is effective, so the Zener current must always stay above it; the upper limit depends on the power rating. With no load (IL = 0) all the circuit current passes through the Zener, which then dissipates its maximum power.

Zener shunt regulator: the series resistor Rs drops the excess voltage, the reverse-biased Zener holds the output near VZ, and RL is in parallel with the Zener. IS = IZ + IL.

IS = (Vin − VZ)/Rs = IZ + IL  ;  Rs ≤ (Vin,min − VZ) / (IZ,min + IL,max)
Worked exampleVin = 12 V, VZ = 5.1 V, IL up to 20 mA, IZ,min = 5 mA: Rs ≤ (12 − 5.1)/(25 mA) = 276 Ω → choose 270 Ω. At no load: I = 6.9/270 = 25.6 mA, PZ = 5.1 × 25.6 mA = 130 mW — safe for a 500 mW Zener. Power in Rs = (25.6 mA)² × 270 = 177 mW (use a ¼ W resistor or larger).

P4. Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

Key terms — know these words
  • BJT — transistor whose operation depends on both electron and hole carriers; current-controlled.
  • Emitter / Base / Collector — heavily doped injector / thin lightly doped control layer / collector.
  • NPN / PNP — n-p-n and p-n-p layer orders.
  • α (alpha) — ΔIC/ΔIE for common base (≈ 0.95–0.99).
  • β (beta) — ΔIC/ΔIB for common emitter (20–200).
  • Cut-off / active / saturation — OFF / amplifying / fully ON regions.

A BJT has three regions and two junctions: emitter, base, collector. In normal (active) operation the emitter–base junction is forward-biased and the collector–base junction reverse-biased. The arrow on the emitter shows conventional emitter-current direction: out of an NPN, in to a PNP.

P5. MOSFET

Key terms — know these words
  • MOSFET — metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor; also called IGFET.
  • Source / Gate / Drain / Substrate — the terminals and the body (joined to source internally).
  • Enhancement mode — channel absent at VGS = 0; gate voltage creates it.
  • Depletion mode — channel present at VGS = 0 (ON by default); gate voltage depletes it.
  • Threshold voltage VT — gate voltage at which the channel forms.
  • Ohmic / saturation / cut-off — regions of the ID–VDS characteristic.
  • Transconductance gm — ΔID/ΔVGS.

The metal gate is insulated from the semiconductor by a SiO₂ layer, so the input impedance is extremely high and the device is voltage-controlled (VGS controls ID), unlike the current-controlled BJT. N-channel enhancement: source and drain are heavily doped N on a P substrate; majority carriers are electrons; source and drain are physically separate until the gate voltage forms a channel. P-channel: the opposite doping, majority carriers holes. In depletion types a thin strip of channel material already joins source and drain.

N-channel enhancement MOSFET: the gate is insulated by SiO₂, so no gate current flows. A positive gate voltage above the threshold pulls electrons under the oxide and forms the channel between N⁺ source and drain.

MOSFET output characteristics (VT = 2 V): below VT nothing flows (cut-off); for small VDS the device acts like a voltage-controlled resistor (ohmic); beyond VDS = VGS − VT the current flattens (saturation).

  • Cut-off: no current; MOSFET is OFF.
  • Ohmic region: drain current rises with VDS.
  • Saturation: drain current constant for further VDS (beyond pinch-off, VDS ≥ VGS − VT).
  • Depletion mode: ON by default; negative gate voltage = depletion, positive = enhancement.
  • Enhancement mode: positive gate voltage makes it conduct.

P6. Rectifiers

Key terms — know these words
  • Rectification — obtaining unidirectional current/voltage from alternating.
  • Half-wave / full-wave — conducts on one / both half-cycles.
  • Centre tap — midpoint connection of the transformer secondary.
  • PIV — peak inverse voltage a diode must withstand.
  • Ripple — AC component left on the DC output; ripple factor γ = Iac,rms/Idc.
  • Rectification efficiency η — DC output power ÷ AC input power.

A diode lets current flow in one direction only, so a load in series with it conducts on alternate half-cycles — the load current (and voltage) is unidirectional, i.e. 'direct' but pulsating.

Half-wave rectifier: the diode conducts only on positive half-cycles, so the load sees pulses with an average of Vm/π and the ripple is at the supply frequency.

Half-wave: Idc = Im/π = 0.318 Im  ;  Irms = Im/2 = 0.5 Im  ;  ηmax = 0.405 (40.5%)

Centre-tapped full-wave rectifier: D₁ conducts when A is positive, D₂ when B is positive; each takes half the secondary, so the load current always flows the same way and the ripple frequency is 2f.

Centre-tap full-wave: C is the centre tap of the secondary; when A is positive w.r.t. C, D₁ conducts and D₂ is off; when the polarities reverse D₂ conducts and D₁ is off. Each diode conducts on alternate half-cycles, passing current through the load in the same direction.

Full-wave: Idc = 2Im/π = 0.637 Im  ;  Irms = Im/√2 = 0.707 Im  ;  η = 8/π² = 0.81 (81%)

Bridge rectifier: when the top input is positive, current runs through D₁, the load (right to left) and D₄; on the other half-cycle through D₂, the same load direction, and D₃. Two diodes conduct at a time, so about 1.4 V is lost.

Bridge: the centre-tap circuit uses each half of the secondary only half the time. In the bridge, when A is positive D₁ and D₃ (slide labelling) conduct, when B is positive D₂ and D₄ conduct; the load current direction is the same and the same full-wave expressions apply. No centre tap is needed.

P7. Operational Amplifiers

Key terms — know these words
  • Op-amp — high-gain DC-coupled differential amplifier IC.
  • Inverting / non-inverting input — the − / + terminals; output is out of / in phase with the signal on them.
  • Open-loop gain — gain without feedback; typically 10⁵.
  • Negative feedback — feeding a fraction of the output back to the − input to stabilise gain.
  • Virtual earth (virtual short) — with feedback, v+ ≈ v−; so for the inverting amp the − input sits at ≈ 0 V.
  • Closed-loop gain Av — gain with feedback; set by resistors.
  • Saturation — output clipped near the supply rails.

Properties of the common IC op-amp: an inverting and a non-inverting input; very high input impedance (assumed infinite) at both; low output impedance; very large voltage gain without feedback (typically 10⁵) that is constant over a wide frequency range; little drift (output zero when input is zero); good stability. Uses go beyond linear amplification into digital logic systems.

Inverting amplifier: negative feedback through Rf forces the inverting input to ≈ 0 V (virtual earth), so the current vi/R₁ must flow through Rf. Output = −(Rf/R₁) × input.

Derivation — inverting amplifierInput current is zero and the − input is held at ≈ 0 V, so the current through R₁ is vi/R₁; all of it must flow through Rf, so vo = −Rf(vi/R₁). Hence Av = −Rf/R₁.

Non-inverting amplifier: the input drives the + terminal; Rf and R₁ form a divider that feeds back a fraction of vo to the − terminal. Gain is always ≥ 1 and the output is in phase with the input.

Non-inverting: v− = vi (virtual short)  ⟹  vi = vo R₁/(R₁+Rf)  ⟹  Av = 1 + Rf/R₁

Summing amplifier: all input currents add at the virtual-earth node and flow through Rf, so the output is the (negative) weighted sum. With RA = RB = RC = Rf it is simply −(vA + vB + vC).

Worked exampleInverting: R₁ = 10 kΩ, Rf = 100 kΩ, vi = 0.2 V → Av = −10, vo = −2 V.
Non-inverting: Rf = 90 kΩ, R₁ = 10 kΩ → Av = 10; vi = 0.5 V → 5 V. A voltage follower is Rf = 0, R₁ = ∞ → gain 1.
Summing: RA = RB = RC = Rf, inputs 1 V, 2 V, 3 V → vo = −(1+2+3) = −6 V.
With ±12 V supplies, a gain of 10 and a 2 V input would try to give 20 V, so the output saturates near ±11 V.

P8. Electromagnetic Spectrum

Key terms — know these words
  • EM wave — oscillating E and B fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel; needs no medium.
  • Wavelength λ — distance of one wave cycle.
  • Frequency f — cycles per second (Hz).
  • c — speed of light, 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum.
  • Photon energy — E = hf, proportional to frequency.
  • Band — range of the spectrum with similar behaviour/use.

The electromagnetic spectrum. All of it travels at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum; c = fλ links frequency and wavelength.

  • Radio waves: longest wavelength, lowest frequency; broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwaves: ovens, radar, satellite.
  • Infrared: felt as heat; thermal imaging, remote controls.
  • Visible light: the narrow band the eye sees, red to violet.
  • Ultraviolet: shorter wavelength, higher energy than visible; can cause sunburn.
  • X-rays: pass through soft tissue; medical imaging.
  • Gamma rays: shortest wavelength, highest frequency; radioactive sources.

P9. Elements of a Communication System

Key terms — know these words
  • Source — origin of the information.
  • Transducer — converts between a physical quantity and an electrical signal (microphone ↔ speaker).
  • Transmitter — converts the information into a signal suited to the channel (modulates, amplifies).
  • Channel — the wired or wireless medium.
  • Noise — unwanted signals that distort the message.
  • Receiver — reverses the transmitter's process.
  • Destination — final recipient.
  • Modulation — impressing the message on a high-frequency carrier.
  • SNR — signal-to-noise ratio, in dB = 10 log₁₀(Ps/Pn).

Block diagram of a communication system: the message is converted to an electrical signal, shaped by the transmitter, sent over a channel that adds noise, then recovered by the receiver and converted back.

  • Source: origin of the information.
  • Input transducer: converts the source's information into an electrical signal (e.g. a microphone for voice).
  • Transmitter: converts the information into a signal suitable for transmission.
  • Channel: medium (wired or wireless) the signal travels through; noise enters here and can distort the message.
  • Receiver: reverses the transmitter's process, recovering a form the destination understands.
  • Output transducer: electrical signal back to a perceivable form (a speaker).
  • Feedback: signal from receiver to source indicating success of the communication.
Why modulate?An antenna needs a length of about λ/4. A 3 kHz audio tone has λ = c/f = 3×10⁸/3000 = 100 km (λ/4 = 25 km!) — impractical. Modulating a 1 MHz carrier (λ = 300 m, λ/4 = 75 m) is far more practical; modulation also allows many stations to share the air (different carriers) and improves noise immunity.
Worked exampleSignal power 2 mW, noise power 0.02 mW: SNR = 100 = 10 log₁₀100 = 20 dB.

Types of modulation: amplitude (AM), frequency (FM), phase (PM) for analog messages; digital schemes for data. Duplex modes: simplex (one way), half-duplex (both ways, one at a time), full-duplex (both at once).

P11. Fundamentals of Satellite Communication

Key terms — know these words
  • Satellite — an orbiting repeater that relays signals between ground stations.
  • Uplink / downlink — ground-to-satellite / satellite-to-ground links.
  • Transponder — receiver-transmitter combination on the satellite that receives, amplifies, shifts frequency and retransmits.
  • TWT amplifier — travelling-wave-tube power amplifier.
  • LEO / MEO / GEO — low (≈160–2000 km), medium and geostationary (≈35 786 km) Earth orbit.
  • Ground control station — monitors and maintains the satellite's position and health.

Principle: a ground station transmits (uplink) to the satellite, which, acting as a repeater, receives, amplifies and retransmits the signal back to Earth (downlink). Components: transmitting earth station; satellite with transponders; receiving earth station; ground control station.

Satellite link: the ground station sends the uplink at a higher frequency; the satellite amplifies it, shifts it down in frequency and returns it as the downlink.

Satellite transponder chain (C-band): the 6 GHz uplink is filtered, amplified, mixed with a 2.2 GHz oscillator to shift it down to about 4 GHz, amplified again by a travelling-wave-tube amplifier and sent back down.

Uplink frequencies are higher than downlink frequencies to prevent interference (C-band: 5.9–6.4 GHz up, 3.7–4.2 GHz down). In the transponder: 6 GHz − 2.2 GHz local oscillator ≈ 3.8 GHz, inside the downlink band.

P12. Fundamentals of Radar

Key terms — know these words
  • Radar — Radio Detection And Ranging.
  • Echo — reflected portion of the transmitted wave.
  • Duplexer — switch letting one antenna transmit and receive.
  • Doppler effect — frequency shift of the echo due to target motion; gives velocity.
  • PRF / PRI — pulse repetition frequency / interval.
  • Unambiguous range — c·PRI/2 — longest range without confusing echoes.

A radar transmits electromagnetic energy and receives the echo reflected off an object. Transmission: a transmitter sends high-power pulses (or continuous waves). Reflection: part of the signal returns. Reception: a sensitive receiver, often sharing the antenna through a duplexer, picks up the weak echo. Processing: a computer extracts the object's characteristics.

Pulse radar: a short pulse goes out, reflects off the target and returns after time t; since it travelled out and back, the range is R = ct/2.

range R = c t / 2   ;   direction = antenna orientation at echo   ;   Doppler fd = 2 v f₀ / c
Worked exampleEcho returns after t = 200 µs: R = 3×10⁸ × 200×10⁻⁶ / 2 = 30 km. A 10 GHz radar sees a target closing at 30 m/s: fd = 2×30×10¹⁰/3×10⁸ = 2000 Hz.

Applications: air-traffic control, weather, speed guns, ships, wind sensing, automotive collision avoidance.

G1. Engineering Drawing: Importance & Scope

Key terms — know these words
  • Engineering drawing — a graphical language giving the shape, size, material and finish of an object so it can be made.
  • BIS — Bureau of Indian Standards; SP 46 is its drawing code, first-angle projection since 1991.
  • Machine / assembly / production drawing — part drawings, how parts fit, and manufacturing details.
  • Pictorial view — a 3-D-looking view (isometric, perspective).
  • Orthographic view — flat views (front, top, side) that show true shapes.
  • CAD — computer-aided design/drafting.

Words alone cannot describe a shape exactly. An engineering drawing communicates shape, size, material, tolerances and surface finish in a standard form that a designer in one place and a machinist in another read identically. It is the link between design and manufacture and the legal record of a design. A standard (BIS) fixes the symbols, line types, lettering and layout so that the drawing means the same to everyone.

  • Scope: mechanical, civil (building drawings), electrical and electronic layouts, piping, shipbuilding, aircraft.
  • Before CAD (1950–80): rooms full of draughtsmen on drawing boards; today: 3-D CAD models from which drawings, analysis and CNC/3-D-printing files are generated.
  • CAD is used for: product design, simulation/analysis, manufacturing drawings, architecture, animation and visualisation, and rapid prototyping.
Learn the standards (G2–G5) and the views (G6–G10) by hand first — CAD (G13) automates the drawing, but the exam asks you to draw, dimension and read views correctly.

G3. Types of Lines

Key terms — know these words
  • Visible outline — thick continuous line (type A).
  • Hidden line — dashed thin line (F) for edges not visible.
  • Centre line — long-dash-dot thin line (G).
  • Dimension / extension / leader — thin continuous lines (B).
  • Cutting plane line — chain thin with thick ends and at changes of direction (H).
  • Hatching — thin parallel lines (B) on cut surfaces.
  • Break line — freehand wavy (C) or zig-zag (D).

BIS line types used in engineering drawing. In orthographic views the commonest are A (visible), F (hidden), G (centre lines), B (dimensions) and H (cutting planes).

In orthographic projection the lines normally met are A (visible outlines/edges), F (hidden), G (centre lines, symmetry, trajectories, pitch circles), B (dimension, extension, projection, short centre, leader and reference lines, hatching) and H (cutting planes). Use HB pencils for thick and 2H for thin lines.

Which line wins?When lines coincide the usual order of preference is: visible outline first, then hidden line, then centre line, then cutting-plane line, then dimension/projection lines — draw the one that takes priority.

G4. Lettering & Dimensioning

Key terms — know these words
  • Lettering — writing of words and numerals on a drawing — plain, single-stroke, uniform.
  • Extension (projection) line — thin line showing the extent of a dimension.
  • Dimension line — thin line with arrowheads between extension lines.
  • Leader line — thin line from a feature to a note or size.
  • Aligned / unidirectional system — figures placed parallel to the dimension line (readable from bottom or right) / all figures horizontal.
  • Datum — reference surface from which dimensions are measured.
  • Fully defined — all geometry dimensioned, nothing missing or redundant.

Lettering must be neat, uniform and legible. Capital letters in single-stroke style are used; recommended heights are roughly 6–8 mm for main titles, 3–6 mm for sub-titles and 3–5 mm for notes and dimension figures.

Elements of dimensioning: extension (projection) lines start just clear of the outline and run slightly past the dimension line; the dimension line ends in arrowheads; figures read from the bottom or the right; diameters carry Ø and use a leader.

Systems: in the aligned system figures are written parallel to the dimension line and read from the bottom or from the right; in the unidirectional system all figures read from the bottom. Dimension lines carry arrowheads (about 3 mm long, 1 mm wide); extension lines start about 1 mm from the outline and extend about 3 mm beyond the dimension line.

Why parallel dimensioning is preferred for inspection: every feature is positioned from the same datum surface, so one hole's error does not shift the others.

  • Chain (continuous): each dimension relates to the previous one; tolerances accumulate.
  • Parallel / progressive: all dimensions from one datum; errors do not accumulate; preferred for functional features.
  • Combined: a mix of both.

G5. Dimensioning Rules & Checking Errors

Key terms — know these words
  • Overall dimension — the total length, width or height of the object.
  • Redundant (duplicate) dimension — a size given twice — not allowed.
  • Ø / R / SR / □ — diameter, radius, spherical radius and square symbols.
  • Chamfer — bevelled edge, dimensioned as angle × length.
  • Functional dimension — one needed for the function of the part.
  • Dimension lines are outside the views where possible; avoid dimensioning inside the outline.
  • Give each dimension once, on the view where the feature is most clearly seen; no duplicates and no unnecessary closed chains.
  • Place smaller dimensions nearer the view and larger ones outside; keep about 8–10 mm between parallel dimension lines.
  • Never use an outline, centre line or extension line as a dimension line; do not cross dimension lines with other lines.
  • Dimension a circle with Ø (leader line), an arc with R; give holes by their centre position (from two edges) and size.
  • Write plain numbers, no unit (the unit is stated once — mm); no trailing decimals such as '.00'.
  • Dimensions are those of the finished part.
Worked exampleCAT-1 Q1 (10 marks): a plate 110 × 70 with a 40-wide slot, two Ø20 holes and a 20 × 40 post is dimensioned with the overall length twice (110 top and bottom), some figures inside the outline, values written as '110.00', and centre lines used as dimension lines.
Corrected version (the key): one overall length 110 (bottom) and one overall height 70 (left); positions from the left/bottom edges — 25 to the first hole's centre, 40 slot width, 20 post width, 20 from the top, 15 and 40 from the right; Ø20 shown with leaders for both holes; all dimension lines outside the views; no '.00'. List the errors first, then redraw.
Marks are given for (i) naming each error, (ii) a clean redrawn view with correct line types, (iii) every dimension given once, outside, with arrowheads, extension lines and the Ø symbol.

G6. Orthographic Projection: Principles, First & Third Angle

Key terms — know these words
  • Projection — representing a 3-D object on a plane by projectors from its points.
  • Parallel / perspective projection — projectors parallel / converging to the eye.
  • Orthographic projection — projectors parallel to each other and perpendicular to the plane.
  • VP / HP / PP — vertical, horizontal and profile (side) planes.
  • Front view (FV) / top view (TV) / side view — elevation / plan / end elevation.
  • Quadrant — one of the four regions formed by HP and VP.
  • XY line — line of intersection of HP and VP (reference line).

In orthographic projection the object is viewed along lines of sight that are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the plane of projection, so each view shows true shapes and sizes. Normally the front view (from the front), top view and a side view are used; together they define the object completely.

The four quadrants. First angle: the object lies between the observer and the vertical plane (the plane is behind the object and treated as opaque). Third angle: the plane lies between the observer and the object (transparent).

G7. Drawing Orthographic Views: Method & Worked Examples

Key terms — know these words
  • Pictorial view — the 3-D picture given in the question.
  • Direction of view (X) — arrow showing which face is the front view.
  • Hidden detail — edges seen only through the object, shown dashed.
  • Projector (construction line) — thin line carrying a point from one view to another.
  • Landscape layout — sheet orientation required for the views.
  1. Choose the front-view direction (the X arrow) so that the front view shows the most shape.
  2. Measure the overall length, height, depth; plan the layout so all three views fit with gaps for dimensions.
  3. Draw thin boxes of overall size for each view (first angle: FV top-left, TV below it, LSV to the right of FV).
  4. Add the details one feature at a time, carrying points between views with thin projectors (height between FV and LSV; width between FV and TV).
  5. Show hidden detail dashed; add centre lines for holes (a hole is a circle in the view along its axis, dashed lines in the others).
  6. Darken the outlines (HB), then dimension each feature once.

Three views of a stepped block (first angle). The step appears as the L-shaped outline in the front view, as an edge in the top view and as a horizontal line in the left-side view. No hidden lines are needed because every edge is visible from the viewing side.

G9. Isometric Projection

Key terms — know these words
  • Isometric — equal measure — all three axes equally foreshortened.
  • Isometric axes — one vertical, two at 30° to the horizontal; 120° apart.
  • Isometric scale — scale of shortened lengths; 0.816 of true length.
  • Isometric drawing — drawn with true lengths (scale 1) — easier, 22% larger.
  • Isometric projection — drawn with the isometric scale.
  • Isometric / non-isometric lines — lines parallel to the axes / all others.
  • Four-centre method — arcs construction of the isometric ellipse inside a rhombus.

Orthographic views are accurate but need spatial imagination; an isometric view presents the object in 3-D in one picture. The cube is turned so that its three visible edges make equal angles with the picture plane, so they are drawn 120° apart.

Isometric axes drawn on a cube. All three faces are foreshortened equally, which is why it is called ‘iso-metric’ (equal measure).

Why 0.816?The cube's diagonal is in the vertical plane; a true edge BP and its isometric image BA satisfy BA/BO = 1/cos 30° = 2/√3 and BP/BO = 1/cos 45° = √2. Hence BA/BP = (2/√3)(1/√2) = √2/√3 = 0.816 (≈ 9/11).

Constructing the isometric scale: mark true lengths on the 45° line, drop verticals onto the 30° line, and read the shortened (isometric) lengths there. Every length along an isometric axis is multiplied by about 0.816.

G10. Freehand Sketching

Key terms — know these words
  • Freehand sketch — drawn without instruments, quickly, with correct proportions.
  • Proportion — the correct relative sizes of features.
  • Construction (thin) lines — light guide lines later darkened.
  • Visualisation — the ability to picture an object in 3-D.
  • Overall size box — thin rectangle of length × height for each view.
  • Why sketch: quick idea generation; flexible; effective communication; encourages creativity; supports problem solving; useful in early design stages; improves visualisation; needs no special tools.

Freehand sketching technique from the lecture: always mark the end points, look at the end point (not the pencil tip), and build circles and ellipses from centre lines with radius marks.

  • Vertical lines are drawn from top to bottom (long lines with overlapping strokes); horizontal lines from left to right; the eye must be on the end point.
  • Inclined lines are drawn upward from left to right or downward from right to left.
  • Always mark the end points first.
  • Circle: draw centre lines, mark points at the radius on them (add diagonals for accuracy), then sweep through the marks; an ellipse is built the same way.

  1. Decide the views to be drawn.
  2. Draw boxes with thin lines of the overall length, width and height of the object.
  3. Add the details; dark outlines for visible edges, dashed for hidden edges.
  4. Dimension.

G11. Perspective Projection

Key terms — know these words
  • Perspective — the image formed on a picture plane by visual rays from the eye to the object.
  • PP (picture plane) — transparent vertical plane on which the image is formed.
  • GP (ground plane) — horizontal plane on which the object stands.
  • GL (ground line) — intersection of PP and GP.
  • Station point (S) — position of the observer's eye.
  • Horizon line (HL) — horizontal line at eye level on the PP.
  • Centre of vision (C) — foot of the axis of vision on the PP.
  • Visual ray — line from the eye to a point of the object.
  • Vanishing point — where receding parallel lines appear to meet.

In parallel (orthographic/isometric) projection the projectors are parallel; in perspective they converge on the eye, so parts farther away look smaller, as in a photograph. The object is placed behind the transparent picture plane and the image is formed where the visual rays pierce the PP.

Side view of the perspective set-up (visual-ray method): the observer at S looks through the transparent picture plane PP at an object standing on the ground plane GP; where each visual ray cuts the PP is a point of the perspective image.

  • One-point perspective: one face parallel to the PP; two-point: object turned so two sets of horizontals vanish at two points on the horizon; three-point: also a vanishing point for verticals.

Visual-ray method (square pyramid example)

Data: pyramid base 20, axis height 40, base 25 behind the PP; station point 50 above GP, 40 in front of PP and 40 to the left of the axis.

  1. Draw the PP and GL; mark the plan (top view) of the pyramid at its distance behind the PP, and the station point's plan.
  2. Join the station point's plan to each plan point (visual rays); mark where each ray cuts the PP line.
  3. Project these cut points down to the elevation, drawing the elevation of the object and of the station point above GL.
  4. In elevation, join the station point to each point of the object; the intersection of each of these lines with the projector from step 3 is that point's perspective.
  5. Join the perspective points in order, hidden edges dashed.
Fixing a common misconceptionThe result depends on the station point: moving the observer closer to the object increases the foreshortening and the convergence.

G13. Introduction to CAD

Key terms — know these words
  • CAD — computer-aided design — creating and modifying drawings/models on a computer.
  • Sketch & constraint — 2-D profile with geometric (parallel, tangent) and dimensional constraints.
  • Feature — extrude, revolve, cut, fillet, chamfer, shell.

H1. Metal Casting

Key terms — know these words
  • Casting — shaping metal by pouring it molten into a mould cavity and letting it solidify.
  • Pattern — replica of the part (made larger) used to make the mould cavity.
  • Mould / cope / drag — sand cavity; upper / lower box.
  • Core — insert forming internal cavities.
  • Sprue / runner / gate / riser — vertical channel / horizontal channel / entry to cavity / reservoir that feeds the shrinking casting.
  • Parting line — joint face between cope and drag.
  • Fettling — cleaning: cutting gates and risers, grinding.
  • Shrinkage / machining / draft allowance — extra size or taper built into the pattern.
Source noteYour syllabus lists this topic but I did not have your lecturer's slides for Module 4, so this is written from the standard textbook treatment (plus the CAT-1 casting question and key). Send the slides and I will align terms and examples to them.

Sand-casting mould (cross-section): metal runs down the sprue, along the runner and gate into the cavity; the riser feeds extra molten metal as the casting shrinks.

  1. Pattern making (with allowances).
  2. Moulding: sand rammed round the pattern in cope and drag; pattern removed; cores set; mould closed.
  3. Melting the metal and pouring through the sprue.
  4. Solidification and cooling (riser feeds shrinkage).
  5. Shake-out, fettling, inspection and heat treatment/machining if needed.

Pattern allowances — CAT-1 Q5 (answer in the key)

Pattern allowances (CAT-1 Q5): the pattern is made larger than the finished component — for shrinkage on cooling and extra stock to machine off — and its vertical faces are tapered (draft) so it can be withdrawn from the sand without damaging the mould.

  • Shrinkage allowance: the pattern is made larger than the casting because the metal contracts as it cools from the liquid to the solid state; the value depends on the material and its contraction.
  • Machining allowance: as-cast surfaces are rough; extra stock is added so that it can be machined off to reach the required size and finish.
  • Draft allowance: vertical faces of the pattern (⟂ the parting line) are given a slight taper so the pattern can be withdrawn without damaging the mould.
Worked examplePart (b) of the question — what if shrinkage and draft are neglected?
No shrinkage allowance → the cast part ends up smaller than the target, so it malfunctions in service or will not fit the assembly. No draft → the pattern jams when drawn from the sand, damaging mould surfaces and causing defects in the casting.
Numerical: cast-iron shrinkage ≈ 1% (10 mm per metre): a 500 mm casting needs a pattern of 500 × 1.01 = 505 mm (before machining allowance).

Common defects: blowholes/porosity (trapped gas), shrinkage cavities (poor feeding), misruns and cold shuts (metal too cold), sand inclusions, hot tears.

Definition [lecture]Bio-inspired design develops concepts, approaches and technologies that build and control "the way nature does": biological process → concept → proof of concept → technological design. It targets engineering and societal problems.

I3. Termite-mound ventilation in green buildings

Claim [lecture]Mounds keep internal temperature (the lecture quotes about 13–31 °C) and humidity stable despite outside swings, by passive design: tunnels and chambers, a central flue/chimney, thick walls. HVAC use is the problem: energy-hungry and CO2-heavy.
Derivation of the stack effectIdeal gas: ρ = p/(RT), so warm air is lighter. Two air columns of height h (outside cool, inside warm) differ in weight, giving Δp = g h (ρout − ρin). This unbalanced pressure pushes cool air in low and warm air out high, with no fan.
Worked exampleh = 10 m, outside 293 K, inside 303 K, ρ ≈ 353/T. ρout = 1.205, ρin = 1.165, difference 0.040 kg/m³. Δp = 9.81 × 10 × 0.040 ≈ 3.9 Pa. Doubling h doubles Δp, which is why mounds and solar chimneys are tall.
Thermal mass check: delay across a wall scales as τ ∼ L²/α. For α ≈ 5×10−7 m²/s, L = 0.2 m gives ≈ 22 h, so a midday heat peak arrives about a day later, the night-time "recharge" the lecture describes (order-of-magnitude estimate).

I6. Neural networks based on the human brain

The biological neuron [lecture]The neuron is the basic unit of the nervous system. Dendrites receive signals (inputs). The soma sums them and applies a threshold (processing, like a CPU). The axon carries the fired signal to terminals, which meet the next neuron's dendrites across a synapse. System loop: sensing organ → central nervous system → effector (actuator), with feedback.
Chemical vs electrical synapses [lecture]Over 95% of transmission is chemical: neurotransmitters cross the synaptic gap, delay > ~5 ms, supports complex tasks and lasting learning. Electrical: ion exchange, delay < 0.1 ms, bidirectional, used for fast reflexes (more common in snakes). Trade-off: speed vs ability to carry out learning.
Fixing a misconceptionThe lecture itself notes the brain's higher-order dendrites split repeatedly (primary, secondary, tertiary). An artificial neuron does not split inputs; instead you feed extra "features" such as x₁², x₁x₂, sin x₁. So the network is brain-inspired, not a copy (this is §1's how-vs-why distinction again).
Worked example (lecture numbers, completed)Inputs 0.1, 0.2, 0.6 with weights 1, 2, 1 (the third is implied by the lecturer's sum of 1.1). Products: 0.1, 0.4, 0.6. Sum = 1.1. Add bias 0.5: z = 1.6. Then apply f(z): sigmoid(1.6) ≈ 0.83; tanh(1.6) ≈ 0.92; ReLU(1.6) = 1.6; linear = 1.6. Training means adjusting the weights each iteration (epoch) so predictions approach the true output.

Evaluating and tuning

Error metrics: MAE = (1/n)Σ|y−ŷ|; MSE = (1/n)Σ(y−ŷ)²; RMSE = √MSE; R² = 1 − Σ(y−ŷ)²/Σ(y−ȳ)². A perfect model has R² = 1 and RMSE = 0. Squaring penalises big errors more (error −5 gives 5 under MAE but 25 under MSE). When several models give R² ≈ 1, RMSE separates them. Hyperparameters (neurons per layer, number of layers, learning rate, activation, features) are set before training; weights are learned during it. Tuning (e.g. with the Adam optimiser) hunts for low-RMSE regions: too few neurons in L1/L2 gives a high-RMSE peak, and a third layer was dispensable in the lecturer's study.

  • Define biomimetics, biomimicry and bionics, and give one example of each without notes.
  • Name three things that are not bio-inspired design and say why.
  • Match eight animals or plants to their products (kingfisher, boxfish, tadpole, snake, ear bones, beehive, burdock, skin).
  • Recite the four stages of wound healing and say which one the e-skin copies.
  • Derive Δp = gh(ρout−ρin) and compute it for h = 20 m, 5 K difference (about 4 Pa).
  • Explain how thermal mass delays a day's heat to night, and give two limits of termite-style ventilation.
  • Map head, antenna, wings, abdomen of an insect to drone parts.
  • Compute Re for a 2 mm insect at 0.5 m/s (about 67) and explain why it flaps rather than glides.
  • Explain why a gradient field gives force but a rotating field gives rotation in magnetic micro-robots.
  • Compute a neuron output by hand with bias (the 1.6 example) and apply sigmoid.
  • Say when to choose a wide, deep or narrow network, and classification or regression.
  • Compute MAE and RMSE for errors (−5, 3, 1) and explain which punishes the −5 more (MAE 3, RMSE ≈ 3.4).
