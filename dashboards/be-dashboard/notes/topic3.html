G1. Engineering Drawing: Importance & Scope

Key terms — know these words
  • Engineering drawing — a graphical language giving the shape, size, material and finish of an object so it can be made.
  • BIS — Bureau of Indian Standards; SP 46 is its drawing code, first-angle projection since 1991.
  • Machine / assembly / production drawing — part drawings, how parts fit, and manufacturing details.
  • Pictorial view — a 3-D-looking view (isometric, perspective).
  • Orthographic view — flat views (front, top, side) that show true shapes.
  • CAD — computer-aided design/drafting.

Words alone cannot describe a shape exactly. An engineering drawing communicates shape, size, material, tolerances and surface finish in a standard form that a designer in one place and a machinist in another read identically. It is the link between design and manufacture and the legal record of a design. A standard (BIS) fixes the symbols, line types, lettering and layout so that the drawing means the same to everyone.

  • Scope: mechanical, civil (building drawings), electrical and electronic layouts, piping, shipbuilding, aircraft.
  • Before CAD (1950–80): rooms full of draughtsmen on drawing boards; today: 3-D CAD models from which drawings, analysis and CNC/3-D-printing files are generated.
  • CAD is used for: product design, simulation/analysis, manufacturing drawings, architecture, animation and visualisation, and rapid prototyping.
Learn the standards (G2–G5) and the views (G6–G10) by hand first — CAD (G13) automates the drawing, but the exam asks you to draw, dimension and read views correctly.

G2. Drawing Sheets, Scales & Title Block

Key terms — know these words
  • A-series — sheet sizes in which every size is half of the previous; sides in ratio 1 : √2.
  • A0 — 841 × 1189 mm, area 1 m².
  • Title block — box at the bottom right giving drawing identity and details.
  • Scale — ratio of drawing size to actual size (1:2 reduces, 2:1 enlarges, 1:1 full size).
  • Revision — recorded change to a drawing.

BIS drawing-sheet sizes (A-series). A0 has an area of 1 m²; folding a sheet across its longer side gives the next size.

Title block (drawn in the bottom right corner of the sheet): it identifies the drawing, its owner, scale and projection symbol, and records who designed, drew, checked and approved it.

  • Key functions of the title block: identifies the drawing (title, number, revision); shows ownership (company, designer, approver); gives scale and projection method; tracks revisions and dates; lists material and technical details; ensures standardisation; helps organisation (sheet numbers, references).
Worked exampleA 1.5 m long object drawn at scale 1:20 is 1500/20 = 75 mm on paper. A 30 mm part drawn at 2:1 appears 60 mm long — but the dimension figure still reads 30.
Fixing a common misconceptionThe dimension figure on a drawing is the actual size, never the measured length on paper; the scale only affects how big the drawn line is.

G3. Types of Lines

Key terms — know these words
  • Visible outline — thick continuous line (type A).
  • Hidden line — dashed thin line (F) for edges not visible.
  • Centre line — long-dash-dot thin line (G).
  • Dimension / extension / leader — thin continuous lines (B).
  • Cutting plane line — chain thin with thick ends and at changes of direction (H).
  • Hatching — thin parallel lines (B) on cut surfaces.
  • Break line — freehand wavy (C) or zig-zag (D).

BIS line types used in engineering drawing. In orthographic views the commonest are A (visible), F (hidden), G (centre lines), B (dimensions) and H (cutting planes).

In orthographic projection the lines normally met are A (visible outlines/edges), F (hidden), G (centre lines, symmetry, trajectories, pitch circles), B (dimension, extension, projection, short centre, leader and reference lines, hatching) and H (cutting planes). Use HB pencils for thick and 2H for thin lines.

Which line wins?When lines coincide the usual order of preference is: visible outline first, then hidden line, then centre line, then cutting-plane line, then dimension/projection lines — draw the one that takes priority.

G4. Lettering & Dimensioning

Key terms — know these words
  • Lettering — writing of words and numerals on a drawing — plain, single-stroke, uniform.
  • Extension (projection) line — thin line showing the extent of a dimension.
  • Dimension line — thin line with arrowheads between extension lines.
  • Leader line — thin line from a feature to a note or size.
  • Aligned / unidirectional system — figures placed parallel to the dimension line (readable from bottom or right) / all figures horizontal.
  • Datum — reference surface from which dimensions are measured.
  • Fully defined — all geometry dimensioned, nothing missing or redundant.

Lettering must be neat, uniform and legible. Capital letters in single-stroke style are used; recommended heights are roughly 6–8 mm for main titles, 3–6 mm for sub-titles and 3–5 mm for notes and dimension figures.

Elements of dimensioning: extension (projection) lines start just clear of the outline and run slightly past the dimension line; the dimension line ends in arrowheads; figures read from the bottom or the right; diameters carry Ø and use a leader.

Systems: in the aligned system figures are written parallel to the dimension line and read from the bottom or from the right; in the unidirectional system all figures read from the bottom. Dimension lines carry arrowheads (about 3 mm long, 1 mm wide); extension lines start about 1 mm from the outline and extend about 3 mm beyond the dimension line.

Why parallel dimensioning is preferred for inspection: every feature is positioned from the same datum surface, so one hole's error does not shift the others.

  • Chain (continuous): each dimension relates to the previous one; tolerances accumulate.
  • Parallel / progressive: all dimensions from one datum; errors do not accumulate; preferred for functional features.
  • Combined: a mix of both.

G5. Dimensioning Rules & Checking Errors

Key terms — know these words
  • Overall dimension — the total length, width or height of the object.
  • Redundant (duplicate) dimension — a size given twice — not allowed.
  • Ø / R / SR / □ — diameter, radius, spherical radius and square symbols.
  • Chamfer — bevelled edge, dimensioned as angle × length.
  • Functional dimension — one needed for the function of the part.
  • Dimension lines are outside the views where possible; avoid dimensioning inside the outline.
  • Give each dimension once, on the view where the feature is most clearly seen; no duplicates and no unnecessary closed chains.
  • Place smaller dimensions nearer the view and larger ones outside; keep about 8–10 mm between parallel dimension lines.
  • Never use an outline, centre line or extension line as a dimension line; do not cross dimension lines with other lines.
  • Dimension a circle with Ø (leader line), an arc with R; give holes by their centre position (from two edges) and size.
  • Write plain numbers, no unit (the unit is stated once — mm); no trailing decimals such as '.00'.
  • Dimensions are those of the finished part.
Worked exampleCAT-1 Q1 (10 marks): a plate 110 × 70 with a 40-wide slot, two Ø20 holes and a 20 × 40 post is dimensioned with the overall length twice (110 top and bottom), some figures inside the outline, values written as '110.00', and centre lines used as dimension lines.
Corrected version (the key): one overall length 110 (bottom) and one overall height 70 (left); positions from the left/bottom edges — 25 to the first hole's centre, 40 slot width, 20 post width, 20 from the top, 15 and 40 from the right; Ø20 shown with leaders for both holes; all dimension lines outside the views; no '.00'. List the errors first, then redraw.
Marks are given for (i) naming each error, (ii) a clean redrawn view with correct line types, (iii) every dimension given once, outside, with arrowheads, extension lines and the Ø symbol.

G6. Orthographic Projection: Principles, First & Third Angle

Key terms — know these words
  • Projection — representing a 3-D object on a plane by projectors from its points.
  • Parallel / perspective projection — projectors parallel / converging to the eye.
  • Orthographic projection — projectors parallel to each other and perpendicular to the plane.
  • VP / HP / PP — vertical, horizontal and profile (side) planes.
  • Front view (FV) / top view (TV) / side view — elevation / plan / end elevation.
  • Quadrant — one of the four regions formed by HP and VP.
  • XY line — line of intersection of HP and VP (reference line).

In orthographic projection the object is viewed along lines of sight that are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the plane of projection, so each view shows true shapes and sizes. Normally the front view (from the front), top view and a side view are used; together they define the object completely.

The four quadrants. First angle: the object lies between the observer and the vertical plane (the plane is behind the object and treated as opaque). Third angle: the plane lies between the observer and the object (transparent).

Layout of views and the identifying symbol. First angle (BIS standard in India): front view on top, top view BELOW it, left-side view to the RIGHT of it. Third angle (USA and others): top view ABOVE, right-side view to the right.

G7. Drawing Orthographic Views: Method & Worked Examples

Key terms — know these words
  • Pictorial view — the 3-D picture given in the question.
  • Direction of view (X) — arrow showing which face is the front view.
  • Hidden detail — edges seen only through the object, shown dashed.
  • Projector (construction line) — thin line carrying a point from one view to another.
  • Landscape layout — sheet orientation required for the views.
  1. Choose the front-view direction (the X arrow) so that the front view shows the most shape.
  2. Measure the overall length, height, depth; plan the layout so all three views fit with gaps for dimensions.
  3. Draw thin boxes of overall size for each view (first angle: FV top-left, TV below it, LSV to the right of FV).
  4. Add the details one feature at a time, carrying points between views with thin projectors (height between FV and LSV; width between FV and TV).
  5. Show hidden detail dashed; add centre lines for holes (a hole is a circle in the view along its axis, dashed lines in the others).
  6. Darken the outlines (HB), then dimension each feature once.

Three views of a stepped block (first angle). The step appears as the L-shaped outline in the front view, as an edge in the top view and as a horizontal line in the left-side view. No hidden lines are needed because every edge is visible from the viewing side.

Worked exampleCAT-1 Q3 (10 marks): a base 75 × 48 × 10 carries a raised block with a Ø22 hole and a triangular rib on one end. Using first angle: (a) front view looking along X — base rectangle 75 × 10 with the block and its Ø22 circle above; (b) top view — 75 × 48 base with the block and rib; (c) left-side view — shows the base, the block and the sloping rib, with the hole as dashed lines. The key shows the hole's axis as centre lines and its hidden edges dashed in TV and LSV.

Pyramid question (CAT-1 Q2): see G8. Practice approach: sketch the views freehand first (G10) to check proportions, then draw to scale.

For 'identify the missing view' questions, use the same projector rules in reverse: widths must match between FV and TV, heights between FV and LSV.

G8. Projection of Solids

Key terms — know these words
  • Polyhedron — solid bounded by plane faces (prism, pyramid, cube).
  • Solid of revolution — generated by rotating a figure (cylinder, cone, sphere).
  • Axis — line joining the centres of the two ends (bases).
  • Base / apex — the face on which a solid rests / the top point of a pyramid or cone.
  • Simple position — axis perpendicular or parallel to a reference plane.
  • Generator — slant line of a cone/cylinder surface.

Solids: prisms (square, hexagonal …), pyramids, cylinders, cones, spheres. Simple positions: axis ⟂ HP (resting on its base), axis ⟂ VP, axis ∥ to both (resting on a face or generator). Rule: when the axis is perpendicular to a plane, the view on that plane shows the true shape of the base.

G9. Isometric Projection

Key terms — know these words
  • Isometric — equal measure — all three axes equally foreshortened.
  • Isometric axes — one vertical, two at 30° to the horizontal; 120° apart.
  • Isometric scale — scale of shortened lengths; 0.816 of true length.
  • Isometric drawing — drawn with true lengths (scale 1) — easier, 22% larger.
  • Isometric projection — drawn with the isometric scale.
  • Isometric / non-isometric lines — lines parallel to the axes / all others.
  • Four-centre method — arcs construction of the isometric ellipse inside a rhombus.

Orthographic views are accurate but need spatial imagination; an isometric view presents the object in 3-D in one picture. The cube is turned so that its three visible edges make equal angles with the picture plane, so they are drawn 120° apart.

Isometric axes drawn on a cube. All three faces are foreshortened equally, which is why it is called ‘iso-metric’ (equal measure).

Why 0.816?The cube's diagonal is in the vertical plane; a true edge BP and its isometric image BA satisfy BA/BO = 1/cos 30° = 2/√3 and BP/BO = 1/cos 45° = √2. Hence BA/BP = (2/√3)(1/√2) = √2/√3 = 0.816 (≈ 9/11).

Constructing the isometric scale: mark true lengths on the 45° line, drop verticals onto the 30° line, and read the shortened (isometric) lengths there. Every length along an isometric axis is multiplied by about 0.816.

Isometric view of the same stepped block (axes at 30°, true lengths). Compare it with the three orthographic views: the step, the riser face and the top surfaces are all visible in one picture.

Isometric of a circle (four-centre method)

  1. Draw a rhombus of side equal to the circle's diameter on the 30° axes.
  2. Mark the midpoints of each side.
  3. Join them to the opposite acute corners to locate the centres; draw the four arcs inside the rhombus.
  4. The result is the isometric ellipse.
Worked exampleCAT-1 Q4: vertical cylinder Ø50, axis 60. Draw a rhombus of side 50 (30° lines) for the top, make the ellipse (major axis ≈ 1.22 × 50 = 61 mm, minor ≈ 0.71 × 50 = 35 mm in an isometric drawing), repeat at the bottom 60 below along the vertical, and join the two ellipses by vertical tangents. In true isometric projection the ellipse's major axis equals the true diameter (50) and the minor is 50/√3 = 29.

Isometric view of a vertical cylinder (Ø50 × 60), as asked in CAT-1 Q4: two identical ellipses joined by vertical tangents; only the lower half of the bottom ellipse is visible.

Orthographic → isometric (from the freehand lecture): construct a box of the overall length, width and height on the 30° axes, then cut away or add the features measured along the axes; transfer non-isometric (sloping) edges by locating their end points.

G10. Freehand Sketching

Key terms — know these words
  • Freehand sketch — drawn without instruments, quickly, with correct proportions.
  • Proportion — the correct relative sizes of features.
  • Construction (thin) lines — light guide lines later darkened.
  • Visualisation — the ability to picture an object in 3-D.
  • Overall size box — thin rectangle of length × height for each view.
  • Why sketch: quick idea generation; flexible; effective communication; encourages creativity; supports problem solving; useful in early design stages; improves visualisation; needs no special tools.

Freehand sketching technique from the lecture: always mark the end points, look at the end point (not the pencil tip), and build circles and ellipses from centre lines with radius marks.

  • Vertical lines are drawn from top to bottom (long lines with overlapping strokes); horizontal lines from left to right; the eye must be on the end point.
  • Inclined lines are drawn upward from left to right or downward from right to left.
  • Always mark the end points first.
  • Circle: draw centre lines, mark points at the radius on them (add diagonals for accuracy), then sweep through the marks; an ellipse is built the same way.

Guidelines for orthographic sketches

  1. Decide the views to be drawn.
  2. Draw boxes with thin lines of the overall length, width and height of the object.
  3. Add the details; dark outlines for visible edges, dashed for hidden edges.
  4. Dimension.
Worked exampleObject with overall 70 (length) × 30 (height) × 50 (depth): boxes — FV 70 × 30, TV 70 × 50 below it, right-side view 50 × 30 to the left of the front view (third angle). Then add the step (15 mm high, 40 wide), the 20 slot, and so on. A second example (slot 16 wide × 10 deep through a 60 × 40 × 40 block) is sketched the same way: FV with the slot notch, TV with three strips, LSV with two dashed lines for the hidden slot.

Simple machine components to practise: bolt and nut, washer, V-block, bearing block, shaft with keyway, bracket — sketch the proportions first, then add holes, fillets and centre lines.

G11. Perspective Projection

Key terms — know these words
  • Perspective — the image formed on a picture plane by visual rays from the eye to the object.
  • PP (picture plane) — transparent vertical plane on which the image is formed.
  • GP (ground plane) — horizontal plane on which the object stands.
  • GL (ground line) — intersection of PP and GP.
  • Station point (S) — position of the observer's eye.
  • Horizon line (HL) — horizontal line at eye level on the PP.
  • Centre of vision (C) — foot of the axis of vision on the PP.
  • Visual ray — line from the eye to a point of the object.
  • Vanishing point — where receding parallel lines appear to meet.

In parallel (orthographic/isometric) projection the projectors are parallel; in perspective they converge on the eye, so parts farther away look smaller, as in a photograph. The object is placed behind the transparent picture plane and the image is formed where the visual rays pierce the PP.

Side view of the perspective set-up (visual-ray method): the observer at S looks through the transparent picture plane PP at an object standing on the ground plane GP; where each visual ray cuts the PP is a point of the perspective image.

  • One-point perspective: one face parallel to the PP; two-point: object turned so two sets of horizontals vanish at two points on the horizon; three-point: also a vanishing point for verticals.

Visual-ray method (square pyramid example)

Data: pyramid base 20, axis height 40, base 25 behind the PP; station point 50 above GP, 40 in front of PP and 40 to the left of the axis.

  1. Draw the PP and GL; mark the plan (top view) of the pyramid at its distance behind the PP, and the station point's plan.
  2. Join the station point's plan to each plan point (visual rays); mark where each ray cuts the PP line.
  3. Project these cut points down to the elevation, drawing the elevation of the object and of the station point above GL.
  4. In elevation, join the station point to each point of the object; the intersection of each of these lines with the projector from step 3 is that point's perspective.
  5. Join the perspective points in order, hidden edges dashed.
Fixing a common misconceptionThe result depends on the station point: moving the observer closer to the object increases the foreshortening and the convergence.

G12. Introduction to Building Drawing (Floor Plan)

Key terms — know these words
  • Plan — horizontal section through the building about 1 m above floor level.
  • Elevation — outside view of a face of the building.
  • Section — vertical cut showing heights and construction.
  • PCC / RCC — plain / reinforced cement concrete.
  • Plinth — part of the wall between ground and floor level.
  • Lintel — beam above a door or window opening.
  • Parapet — low wall at the edge of a roof.
  • Weathering course — waterproof layer on a roof.
  • Habitable room — room for living, sleeping, etc.

A building drawing shows what to build: a plan (rooms, walls, doors, windows), elevations and a section (foundation, wall, floor, roof heights). Drawings follow BIS conventions (your lecture cites IS 962:1967; the syllabus mentions SP 7, the National Building Code) with standard symbols and material hatching for brick, stone, concrete and so on. Scales: 1:100 for plans; 1:50 or larger for sections and details.

  • Parts of a building: foundation (PCC footings), plinth, superstructure (walls), lintel, roof slab (RCC), parapet, weathering course, floor finish, steps.
  • Typical thicknesses in the lecture pump-house example: parapet 200, weathering course 100, RCC roof slab 120, PCC (1:4:8) flooring 120, cement-mortar plaster about 20.
  • Material notation: cement mortar ratios (e.g. CM 1:5, 1:3) and concrete mixes (e.g. 1:4:8, 1:2:4) are written on the section.

A simple one-bedroom residential plan: external walls thick, partition walls thinner, door swings as quarter-circles, windows as double lines in the wall. Dimensions in mm as in a real plan; room sizes meet the usual minimum-area rules.

  • External walls 230 mm, partitions 115 mm; doors shown with the swing arc; windows as double lines in the wall.
  • Typical minimum sizes (National Building Code, check the latest): habitable room ≈ 9.5 m² (width ≥ 2.4 m); kitchen ≈ 4.5 m² (width ≥ 1.8 m); bathroom ≈ 1.8 m².
  • Lines: thick for sectioned walls, thin for furniture and dimension lines; room names and sizes written inside.
Worked examplePlot 10 m × 12 m = 120 m²; built-up area of the plan above = 6.8 × 6.0 = 40.8 m² → 34% of the plot. Living 3.6 × 3.6 = 13 m² and bedroom 3.2 × 3.6 = 11.5 m² both exceed the 9.5 m² minimum; kitchen 3.6 × 2.4 = 8.6 m² exceeds 4.5 m².

G13. Introduction to CAD

Key terms — know these words
  • CAD — computer-aided design — creating and modifying drawings/models on a computer.
  • Sketch & constraint — 2-D profile with geometric (parallel, tangent) and dimensional constraints.
  • Feature — extrude, revolve, cut, fillet, chamfer, shell.
  • Parametric model — model whose dimensions drive the geometry.
  • Layer — transparent overlay organising drawing content.
  • DWG / DXF / STEP / STL — drawing, exchange, neutral solid and 3-D-printing mesh file types.
  • CAM — computer-aided manufacturing.

In CAD software you sketch a 2-D profile with basic tools (line, circle, arc), add geometric and dimensional constraints, then build a 3-D solid with features — extrude (push a profile to a depth), revolve (spin it about an axis), cut, fillet (round an edge), chamfer (bevel an edge) and shell (hollow out). The 2-D engineering drawing — multiple views, dimensions, annotations — is then generated from the 3-D model, so a change in the model updates the drawing. (This is the content of the SolidWorks lab experiments.)

  • Advantages: accuracy, easy editing, storage and sharing, standard parts libraries, simulation, direct link to CNC and 3-D printing.
  • Common packages: AutoCAD (2-D drafting), SolidWorks, CATIA, Fusion 360, FreeCAD (3-D).
Worked exampleCoordinate input. Absolute: type 50,30 → point (50, 30). Relative polar: @50<30 → a line 50 long at 30°: Δx = 50 cos 30° = 43.3, Δy = 50 sin 30° = 25.
CAD files feed Module 4: an STL file goes to the 3-D-printer slicer (H6) and the CAM toolpath goes to the CNC machine (H5).
