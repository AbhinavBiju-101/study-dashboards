P1. PN Junction Diode

Key terms — know these words
  • Semiconductor — material between conductor and insulator (Si, Ge); conductivity set by doping.
  • P-type / N-type — doped with acceptor / donor impurity; majority carriers holes / electrons.
  • Majority / minority carriers — the dominant carrier type / the other type in a doped region.
  • Diffusion — carriers moving from high to low concentration across the junction.
  • Depletion layer — region near the junction emptied of free carriers, holding fixed ions.
  • Barrier potential (V₀) — ≈ 0.7 V (Si), 0.3 V (Ge).
  • Forward / reverse bias — + on P and − on N / the opposite.
  • Saturation current (Is) — tiny reverse current due to minority carriers.
  • Knee (cut-in) voltage — forward voltage where current starts to rise rapidly.

A junction diode has one half doped p-type (mobile holes) and the other n-type (mobile electrons). Holes diffuse into the n-side and electrons into the p-side; the region A near the junction on the p-side acquires an excess of negative charge and region B on the n-side a surplus of positive charge. These charges repel further migration and create a potential barrier.

A PN junction at equilibrium: diffusion leaves fixed negative ions on the P side and fixed positive ions on the N side. The resulting field (and potential barrier) stops further diffusion.

Forward bias (+ on P) pushes carriers toward the junction and shrinks the depletion layer, so current flows. Reverse bias (+ on N) pulls carriers away and widens the layer, so only a tiny leakage current flows.

Forward bias: drift of holes toward the junction in the p-region and of electrons the other way in the n-region; they combine near the junction while the battery keeps supplying new carriers — current is hole flow in the p-region, electron flow in the n-region, and a mix near the junction. Reverse bias: holes and electrons are pulled away, widening the depletion layer, which behaves as an insulator; only a small current due to minority carriers (thermally generated electron–hole pairs, life ≈ 50 µs in Si, 100 µs in Ge) flows — the saturation current.

V–I characteristic of a junction diode: almost nothing flows until the forward knee (≈ 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium); in reverse only a tiny leakage flows until breakdown.

P2. Zener Diode

Key terms — know these words
  • Zener diode — diode designed for use in reverse breakdown (4–75 V typical).
  • Breakdown voltage VZ — the nearly constant voltage across the diode after breakdown (reference voltage).
  • Zener / avalanche effect — the two breakdown mechanisms (Zener below ≈5 V, avalanche above).
  • IZ(min) / IZ(max) — minimum current to stay in regulation / maximum before power limit.
  • PZ — maximum power rating of the Zener.

Special junction diode — voltage-regulator diode: reverse breakdown voltage in the range of about 4 V to 75 V depending on the type. Forward-biased it behaves as a normal diode. As the reverse voltage rises, a value is reached where the current rises very rapidly because the increased velocity of the carriers causes ionisation. The voltage across the diode after breakdown is the reference voltage and stays practically constant over a wide range of current provided the maximum junction temperature is not exceeded. It is heavily doped, which gives a thin depletion layer and a sharp, low breakdown voltage.

Zener diode V–I curve: forward it behaves like an ordinary diode; in reverse the voltage is pinned near VZ over a wide range of current, which is what makes it a voltage reference.

Worked exampleA 5.1 V, 500 mW Zener: maximum current IZ(max) = P/VZ = 0.5/5.1 ≈ 98 mA. In use it must stay between IZ(min) (a few mA) and this limit.
Fixing a common misconceptionA Zener always works reverse-biased; a resistor is essential to limit the current — without it, the sharp breakdown current would destroy the device.

P3. Voltage Regulator (Zener shunt regulator)

Key terms — know these words
  • Regulation — keeping the output voltage constant.
  • Line regulation — stability when the input voltage changes.
  • Load regulation — stability when the load current changes.
  • Rs — series (current-limiting) resistor.
  • Shunt regulator — regulating element in parallel with the load.

Rs is in series with the Zener to limit the current, Vs being across the combination; the stabilised output is taken across the Zener (Vout = VZ) and the load RL is in parallel with it. There is a minimum Zener current for which regulation is effective, so the Zener current must always stay above it; the upper limit depends on the power rating. With no load (IL = 0) all the circuit current passes through the Zener, which then dissipates its maximum power.

Zener shunt regulator: the series resistor Rs drops the excess voltage, the reverse-biased Zener holds the output near VZ, and RL is in parallel with the Zener. IS = IZ + IL.

IS = (Vin − VZ)/Rs = IZ + IL  ;  Rs ≤ (Vin,min − VZ) / (IZ,min + IL,max)
Worked exampleVin = 12 V, VZ = 5.1 V, IL up to 20 mA, IZ,min = 5 mA: Rs ≤ (12 − 5.1)/(25 mA) = 276 Ω → choose 270 Ω. At no load: I = 6.9/270 = 25.6 mA, PZ = 5.1 × 25.6 mA = 130 mW — safe for a 500 mW Zener. Power in Rs = (25.6 mA)² × 270 = 177 mW (use a ¼ W resistor or larger).

P4. Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

Key terms — know these words
  • BJT — transistor whose operation depends on both electron and hole carriers; current-controlled.
  • Emitter / Base / Collector — heavily doped injector / thin lightly doped control layer / collector.
  • NPN / PNP — n-p-n and p-n-p layer orders.
  • α (alpha) — ΔIC/ΔIE for common base (≈ 0.95–0.99).
  • β (beta) — ΔIC/ΔIB for common emitter (20–200).
  • Cut-off / active / saturation — OFF / amplifying / fully ON regions.

A BJT has three regions and two junctions: emitter, base, collector. In normal (active) operation the emitter–base junction is forward-biased and the collector–base junction reverse-biased. The arrow on the emitter shows conventional emitter-current direction: out of an NPN, in to a PNP.

BJT symbols with conventional current directions. The emitter arrow always shows the direction of conventional emitter current: out of an NPN, into a PNP.

IE = IB + IC  ;  α = ΔIC/ΔIE  ;  β = ΔIC/ΔIB  ;  α = β/(1+β)  ;  β = α/(1−α)

Common-emitter output characteristics (β = 100): each curve is one base current. Left edge = saturation (switch ON), flat middle = active region (amplifier), IB = 0 = cut-off (switch OFF).

P5. MOSFET

Key terms — know these words
  • MOSFET — metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor; also called IGFET.
  • Source / Gate / Drain / Substrate — the terminals and the body (joined to source internally).
  • Enhancement mode — channel absent at VGS = 0; gate voltage creates it.
  • Depletion mode — channel present at VGS = 0 (ON by default); gate voltage depletes it.
  • Threshold voltage VT — gate voltage at which the channel forms.
  • Ohmic / saturation / cut-off — regions of the ID–VDS characteristic.
  • Transconductance gm — ΔID/ΔVGS.

The metal gate is insulated from the semiconductor by a SiO₂ layer, so the input impedance is extremely high and the device is voltage-controlled (VGS controls ID), unlike the current-controlled BJT. N-channel enhancement: source and drain are heavily doped N on a P substrate; majority carriers are electrons; source and drain are physically separate until the gate voltage forms a channel. P-channel: the opposite doping, majority carriers holes. In depletion types a thin strip of channel material already joins source and drain.

N-channel enhancement MOSFET: the gate is insulated by SiO₂, so no gate current flows. A positive gate voltage above the threshold pulls electrons under the oxide and forms the channel between N⁺ source and drain.

MOSFET output characteristics (VT = 2 V): below VT nothing flows (cut-off); for small VDS the device acts like a voltage-controlled resistor (ohmic); beyond VDS = VGS − VT the current flattens (saturation).

  • Cut-off: no current; MOSFET is OFF.
  • Ohmic region: drain current rises with VDS.
  • Saturation: drain current constant for further VDS (beyond pinch-off, VDS ≥ VGS − VT).
  • Depletion mode: ON by default; negative gate voltage = depletion, positive = enhancement.
  • Enhancement mode: positive gate voltage makes it conduct.

P6. Rectifiers

Key terms — know these words
  • Rectification — obtaining unidirectional current/voltage from alternating.
  • Half-wave / full-wave — conducts on one / both half-cycles.
  • Centre tap — midpoint connection of the transformer secondary.
  • PIV — peak inverse voltage a diode must withstand.
  • Ripple — AC component left on the DC output; ripple factor γ = Iac,rms/Idc.
  • Rectification efficiency η — DC output power ÷ AC input power.

A diode lets current flow in one direction only, so a load in series with it conducts on alternate half-cycles — the load current (and voltage) is unidirectional, i.e. 'direct' but pulsating.

Half-wave rectifier: the diode conducts only on positive half-cycles, so the load sees pulses with an average of Vm/π and the ripple is at the supply frequency.

Half-wave: Idc = Im/π = 0.318 Im  ;  Irms = Im/2 = 0.5 Im  ;  ηmax = 0.405 (40.5%)

Centre-tapped full-wave rectifier: D₁ conducts when A is positive, D₂ when B is positive; each takes half the secondary, so the load current always flows the same way and the ripple frequency is 2f.

Centre-tap full-wave: C is the centre tap of the secondary; when A is positive w.r.t. C, D₁ conducts and D₂ is off; when the polarities reverse D₂ conducts and D₁ is off. Each diode conducts on alternate half-cycles, passing current through the load in the same direction.

Full-wave: Idc = 2Im/π = 0.637 Im  ;  Irms = Im/√2 = 0.707 Im  ;  η = 8/π² = 0.81 (81%)

Bridge rectifier: when the top input is positive, current runs through D₁, the load (right to left) and D₄; on the other half-cycle through D₂, the same load direction, and D₃. Two diodes conduct at a time, so about 1.4 V is lost.

Bridge: the centre-tap circuit uses each half of the secondary only half the time. In the bridge, when A is positive D₁ and D₃ (slide labelling) conduct, when B is positive D₂ and D₄ conduct; the load current direction is the same and the same full-wave expressions apply. No centre tap is needed.

P7. Operational Amplifiers

Key terms — know these words
  • Op-amp — high-gain DC-coupled differential amplifier IC.
  • Inverting / non-inverting input — the − / + terminals; output is out of / in phase with the signal on them.
  • Open-loop gain — gain without feedback; typically 10⁵.
  • Negative feedback — feeding a fraction of the output back to the − input to stabilise gain.
  • Virtual earth (virtual short) — with feedback, v+ ≈ v−; so for the inverting amp the − input sits at ≈ 0 V.
  • Closed-loop gain Av — gain with feedback; set by resistors.
  • Saturation — output clipped near the supply rails.

Properties of the common IC op-amp: an inverting and a non-inverting input; very high input impedance (assumed infinite) at both; low output impedance; very large voltage gain without feedback (typically 10⁵) that is constant over a wide frequency range; little drift (output zero when input is zero); good stability. Uses go beyond linear amplification into digital logic systems.

Inverting amplifier: negative feedback through Rf forces the inverting input to ≈ 0 V (virtual earth), so the current vi/R₁ must flow through Rf. Output = −(Rf/R₁) × input.

Derivation — inverting amplifierInput current is zero and the − input is held at ≈ 0 V, so the current through R₁ is vi/R₁; all of it must flow through Rf, so vo = −Rf(vi/R₁). Hence Av = −Rf/R₁.

Non-inverting amplifier: the input drives the + terminal; Rf and R₁ form a divider that feeds back a fraction of vo to the − terminal. Gain is always ≥ 1 and the output is in phase with the input.

Non-inverting: v− = vi (virtual short)  ⟹  vi = vo R₁/(R₁+Rf)  ⟹  Av = 1 + Rf/R₁

Summing amplifier: all input currents add at the virtual-earth node and flow through Rf, so the output is the (negative) weighted sum. With RA = RB = RC = Rf it is simply −(vA + vB + vC).

Worked exampleInverting: R₁ = 10 kΩ, Rf = 100 kΩ, vi = 0.2 V → Av = −10, vo = −2 V.
Non-inverting: Rf = 90 kΩ, R₁ = 10 kΩ → Av = 10; vi = 0.5 V → 5 V. A voltage follower is Rf = 0, R₁ = ∞ → gain 1.
Summing: RA = RB = RC = Rf, inputs 1 V, 2 V, 3 V → vo = −(1+2+3) = −6 V.
With ±12 V supplies, a gain of 10 and a 2 V input would try to give 20 V, so the output saturates near ±11 V.

P8. Electromagnetic Spectrum

Key terms — know these words
  • EM wave — oscillating E and B fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel; needs no medium.
  • Wavelength λ — distance of one wave cycle.
  • Frequency f — cycles per second (Hz).
  • c — speed of light, 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum.
  • Photon energy — E = hf, proportional to frequency.
  • Band — range of the spectrum with similar behaviour/use.

The electromagnetic spectrum. All of it travels at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum; c = fλ links frequency and wavelength.

  • Radio waves: longest wavelength, lowest frequency; broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwaves: ovens, radar, satellite.
  • Infrared: felt as heat; thermal imaging, remote controls.
  • Visible light: the narrow band the eye sees, red to violet.
  • Ultraviolet: shorter wavelength, higher energy than visible; can cause sunburn.
  • X-rays: pass through soft tissue; medical imaging.
  • Gamma rays: shortest wavelength, highest frequency; radioactive sources.
c = f λ   ;   E = h f    (h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s)   ; wavelength and frequency are inversely related
Worked exampleFM radio at 100 MHz: λ = 3×10⁸/10⁸ = 3 m. Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz: λ = 12.5 cm. Green light at 550 nm: f = 3×10⁸/550×10⁻⁹ = 5.5×10¹⁴ Hz, photon energy 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 5.5×10¹⁴ = 3.6×10⁻¹⁹ J (2.3 eV).

P9. Elements of a Communication System

Key terms — know these words
  • Source — origin of the information.
  • Transducer — converts between a physical quantity and an electrical signal (microphone ↔ speaker).
  • Transmitter — converts the information into a signal suited to the channel (modulates, amplifies).
  • Channel — the wired or wireless medium.
  • Noise — unwanted signals that distort the message.
  • Receiver — reverses the transmitter's process.
  • Destination — final recipient.
  • Modulation — impressing the message on a high-frequency carrier.
  • SNR — signal-to-noise ratio, in dB = 10 log₁₀(Ps/Pn).

Block diagram of a communication system: the message is converted to an electrical signal, shaped by the transmitter, sent over a channel that adds noise, then recovered by the receiver and converted back.

  • Source: origin of the information.
  • Input transducer: converts the source's information into an electrical signal (e.g. a microphone for voice).
  • Transmitter: converts the information into a signal suitable for transmission.
  • Channel: medium (wired or wireless) the signal travels through; noise enters here and can distort the message.
  • Receiver: reverses the transmitter's process, recovering a form the destination understands.
  • Output transducer: electrical signal back to a perceivable form (a speaker).
  • Feedback: signal from receiver to source indicating success of the communication.
Why modulate?An antenna needs a length of about λ/4. A 3 kHz audio tone has λ = c/f = 3×10⁸/3000 = 100 km (λ/4 = 25 km!) — impractical. Modulating a 1 MHz carrier (λ = 300 m, λ/4 = 75 m) is far more practical; modulation also allows many stations to share the air (different carriers) and improves noise immunity.
Worked exampleSignal power 2 mW, noise power 0.02 mW: SNR = 100 = 10 log₁₀100 = 20 dB.

Types of modulation: amplitude (AM), frequency (FM), phase (PM) for analog messages; digital schemes for data. Duplex modes: simplex (one way), half-duplex (both ways, one at a time), full-duplex (both at once).

P10. Overview of Cellular Communication

Key terms — know these words
  • Cell — the small coverage area served by one base station.
  • Base station — transmitter/receiver (tower) serving a cell.
  • MSC — mobile switching centre connecting cells and the public network.
  • Frequency reuse — using the same frequencies in cells that are far apart.
  • Cluster (N) — group of cells that together use all the available channels once.
  • Handoff — passing an ongoing call from one cell to the next as the user moves.
  • Roaming — using the network away from one's home network.
Source noteThis topic is in the syllabus ('overview of cellular communication') but was not in the slides you shared, so it is written from the standard textbook treatment — check it against your lecturer's slides.

Cellular layout with frequency reuse, cluster size N = 7: the service area is tiled by hexagonal cells, and each of the 7 frequency groups is reused in a distant cell.

The service area is divided into cells, each with its own low-power base station. Low power lets the same frequencies be reused in distant cells, so a limited spectrum serves very many users. When a mobile moves out of a cell, a handoff passes the call to the next base station without interruption; the MSC coordinates this and links to the telephone network.

co-channel reuse distance: D / R = √(3N)    (R = cell radius, N = cluster size)
Worked exampleN = 7: D/R = √21 = 4.58, so co-channel cells are about 4.6 cell radii apart. Smaller N gives more capacity per cell but more interference.

P11. Fundamentals of Satellite Communication

Key terms — know these words
  • Satellite — an orbiting repeater that relays signals between ground stations.
  • Uplink / downlink — ground-to-satellite / satellite-to-ground links.
  • Transponder — receiver-transmitter combination on the satellite that receives, amplifies, shifts frequency and retransmits.
  • TWT amplifier — travelling-wave-tube power amplifier.
  • LEO / MEO / GEO — low (≈160–2000 km), medium and geostationary (≈35 786 km) Earth orbit.
  • Ground control station — monitors and maintains the satellite's position and health.

Principle: a ground station transmits (uplink) to the satellite, which, acting as a repeater, receives, amplifies and retransmits the signal back to Earth (downlink). Components: transmitting earth station; satellite with transponders; receiving earth station; ground control station.

Satellite link: the ground station sends the uplink at a higher frequency; the satellite amplifies it, shifts it down in frequency and returns it as the downlink.

Satellite transponder chain (C-band): the 6 GHz uplink is filtered, amplified, mixed with a 2.2 GHz oscillator to shift it down to about 4 GHz, amplified again by a travelling-wave-tube amplifier and sent back down.

Uplink frequencies are higher than downlink frequencies to prevent interference (C-band: 5.9–6.4 GHz up, 3.7–4.2 GHz down). In the transponder: 6 GHz − 2.2 GHz local oscillator ≈ 3.8 GHz, inside the downlink band.

P12. Fundamentals of Radar

Key terms — know these words
  • Radar — Radio Detection And Ranging.
  • Echo — reflected portion of the transmitted wave.
  • Duplexer — switch letting one antenna transmit and receive.
  • Doppler effect — frequency shift of the echo due to target motion; gives velocity.
  • PRF / PRI — pulse repetition frequency / interval.
  • Unambiguous range — c·PRI/2 — longest range without confusing echoes.

A radar transmits electromagnetic energy and receives the echo reflected off an object. Transmission: a transmitter sends high-power pulses (or continuous waves). Reflection: part of the signal returns. Reception: a sensitive receiver, often sharing the antenna through a duplexer, picks up the weak echo. Processing: a computer extracts the object's characteristics.

Pulse radar: a short pulse goes out, reflects off the target and returns after time t; since it travelled out and back, the range is R = ct/2.

range R = c t / 2   ;   direction = antenna orientation at echo   ;   Doppler fd = 2 v f₀ / c
Worked exampleEcho returns after t = 200 µs: R = 3×10⁸ × 200×10⁻⁶ / 2 = 30 km. A 10 GHz radar sees a target closing at 30 m/s: fd = 2×30×10¹⁰/3×10⁸ = 2000 Hz.

Applications: air-traffic control, weather, speed guns, ships, wind sensing, automotive collision avoidance.

Self-Test Checklist

Can you do each of these right now, without looking back? If not, that is the section to re-read.

  • Explain the depletion layer and the barrier potential, and sketch the diode V–I curve for forward and reverse bias.
  • Apply the diode equation and the Si/Ge differences (knee voltage, temperature behaviour).
  • Explain why a Zener is used reverse-biased and design the series resistor of a Zener regulator.
  • Define α and β, state α = β/(1+β), and compare CB, CE and CC.
  • Contrast BJT and MOSFET; describe enhancement vs depletion and the three regions of operation.
  • Derive Idc, Irms and efficiency for half-wave and full-wave rectifiers; compare bridge and centre-tap.
  • Derive the gain of the inverting and non-inverting op-amp and solve a summing-amplifier problem.
  • Use c = fλ for the EM spectrum and name the bands in order.
  • Draw and label a communication block diagram and explain why modulation is needed.
  • Explain frequency reuse and handoff in a cellular network.
  • Describe the satellite uplink/downlink and transponder, and compare LEO/MEO/GEO.
  • Find the range and Doppler shift in a radar problem.
