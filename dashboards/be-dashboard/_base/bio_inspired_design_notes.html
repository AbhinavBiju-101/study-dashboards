0. Roadmap

One move repeats in every lecture: observe a natural solution → ask how (mechanism) and why (purpose) → re-implement in engineering terms. §1 names the vocabulary. §2 and §3 apply it to materials and buildings. §4 and §5 apply it to flying and crawling machines. §6 applies it to computation. Look for the recurring threads: hexagons (§1, §2), passive stack airflow (§1, §3), hooks (§1, §2), store-and-release energy (§4), and sum → threshold → fire (§1 Schmitt trigger, §6 neuron).

1. Introduction to bio-inspired design (Dr Shankar)

Definition [lecture]Bio-inspired design develops concepts, approaches and technologies that build and control "the way nature does": biological process → concept → proof of concept → technological design. It targets engineering and societal problems.
What does not count [lecture](1) Purely aesthetic or decorative designs with no function. (2) Purely human technologies (microprocessors, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi). (3) Conventional mathematical optimisation, such as thermodynamic-cycle optimisation. The test: is a biological process the source of the idea, and does it do a function?

Rawling's classification: three terms, one spectrum

Why the distinction mattersThe termite example shows it. Asking how the mound holds temperature, then copying the airflow, is biomimetics. Asking why nature solves it passively and adopting passive, low-energy planning for cities is biomimicry. The lecturer notes termites are interesting not for eating wood but for "how they maintain internal temperature", which is the principle, not the animal.

Worked example: why a Schmitt trigger is "neuron-like"Inputs pass through a resistor divider; the output flips ON only when the input crosses a threshold, and flips back at a (different) lower one. A neuron likewise sums incoming signals and fires only above threshold. Foreshadowing: §6 builds the artificial neuron from exactly this behaviour, adding weights and a bias.

Fixing a misconception"Bio-inspired means copying the animal." The lecture's own examples are properties (cell geometry, direction-dependent strength, spiral growth), not animal outlines. §2 shows each of these properties being transferred to a product.

Why the hexagon keeps winning [connects §1]Hexagons tile a plane with no gaps and the least wall length per cell, so they hold the most volume per unit of material. The same shape explains the honeycomb panel (§1), the tadpole foot channels, and the car floorboard. The sandwich adds stiffness because the faces carry bending while the light core only spaces them apart.
Fixing a misconception"The cave effect means caves are cold." No: it moderates. Hot outside → slightly cooler inside; cold outside → slightly warmer inside. That damping is exactly what the wine cellar uses.

Skin self-healing → self-healing e-skin

The engineering copy [lecture]Microcapsules of monomer (dicyclopentadiene) and a ruthenium Grubbs catalyst are embedded in the elastic e-skin. A crack breaks the capsules; monomer meets catalyst and polymerises, filling the crack, the analogue of collagen proliferation. Conductive fillers with reversible bonds (graphene, carbon nanotubes, silver) re-form so electrical paths heal too. Skin's two properties, mechanical (collagen + elastin → silicones, polymers) and sensory (tactile sensors, plus power and a communication module), plus healing, make three design requirements.
ForeshadowingSelf-healing returns in §5 (soft robots are made of the same elastomer family), and sensing → processing → actuation in the e-skin pipeline is the same loop as the nervous system in §6.

3. Termite-mound ventilation in green buildings

Claim [lecture]Mounds keep internal temperature (the lecture quotes about 13–31 °C) and humidity stable despite outside swings, by passive design: tunnels and chambers, a central flue/chimney, thick walls. HVAC use is the problem: energy-hungry and CO2-heavy.
Derivation of the stack effectIdeal gas: ρ = p/(RT), so warm air is lighter. Two air columns of height h (outside cool, inside warm) differ in weight, giving Δp = g h (ρout − ρin). This unbalanced pressure pushes cool air in low and warm air out high, with no fan.
Worked exampleh = 10 m, outside 293 K, inside 303 K, ρ ≈ 353/T. ρout = 1.205, ρin = 1.165, difference 0.040 kg/m³. Δp = 9.81 × 10 × 0.040 ≈ 3.9 Pa. Doubling h doubles Δp, which is why mounds and solar chimneys are tall.
Thermal mass check: delay across a wall scales as τ ∼ L²/α. For α ≈ 5×10−7 m²/s, L = 0.2 m gives ≈ 22 h, so a midday heat peak arrives about a day later, the night-time "recharge" the lecture describes (order-of-magnitude estimate).

Myths vs facts [lecture]Myth: mounds simply use wind to cool the nest. Fact: some ventilation involves heating and mound structure; it is not 100% passive. Myth: Eastgate is a perfect copy. Fact: it follows the principle but sometimes uses powered fans. Myth: mounds are static. Fact: termites keep modifying them (rain damage, heat, humidity).

Triple bottom line: environmental (lower energy, CO2), economic (lower operating cost), social (better indoor air, health). Limits: less effective in very humid or stagnant-air climates (so add dehumidification, evaporative pre-cooling, solar chimneys), design complexity, higher initial cost, regular maintenance and monitoring. Future: CFD modelling, advanced materials, sensors that adjust openings, hybrid solar/wind.

Foreshadowing"Sensors that open and close vents" is a control loop; §4 gives insect drones the same idea (sensing, actuation, coordination).

4. Insect-inspired drones, part 1: design of a robotic insect

Learning outcomes: how insects and birds fly, how insect intelligence guides autonomous flying robots, and how to map flight mechanisms onto drones. These drones are research devices (about 2 cm scale), not today's commercial drones.

Insect partNatural functionDrone counterpart
Head: compound eyesMany photoreceptors: high resolution, panoramic, adjustable view, UV coloursFPV (first-person view) camera + video transmitter; compound lenses; thermal cameras
Head: antennaSmell: food, prey, pollinationSilkworm-moth antenna read by electroantennography (EAG): sniffs chemicals and hazardous gases
Thorax: muscles + wingsFlapping: linear + rotary motion, 3 degrees of freedomSpring (bending beam) + rotary ball bearing; shape-memory-alloy "smart wings"
AbdomenOrgansBattery, GPS, electronics
Why store-and-release energy matters [lecture]Insect wings behave like a spring: contracting stores elastic energy; spreading releases it, so each stroke reuses energy from the last. Even the best drones fly under ~30 min [lecture], so extracting more from each wing beat is the research goal. Rigid wings cannot bend or twist; flexible wings (shape-memory alloy) store and return energy, with ~40% performance gain reported [lecture].
Scale and Reynolds numberRe = ρvL/μ compares inertial to viscous force. Lecture: insect scale has low wing inertia, passive shape deformation and fast response (wing-to-body mass ~1–5%), while bird scale has higher wing inertia and more distributed sensing (~10–20%). Smaller insects must flap faster.
Derivation: lift = weight gives v ∝ √(m/S) ∝ √L when all lengths scale by L. Small flyers need less speed but live at low Re.
Worked exampleFly: v = 1 m/s, L = 3 mm: Re = 1.2×1×0.003/1.8×10−5 = 200. Airliner: v = 250 m/s, L = 5 m: Re ≈ 8×107. Shrink a design 100× (k = 0.01): required speed falls to √0.01 = 0.1 of the original, so a 60 m/s craft needs ~6 m/s.

Measuring performance [lecture]: force transducer (lift/weight change), high-speed camera (flapping frequency, slow-motion comparison with the real insect), electrical power (current × voltage). Advantages: tiny, can enter confined spaces, work in swarms, minimalist design. Challenges: complex small-scale design (MEMS), full autonomy (collision avoidance, navigation, self-recharge), computing power (GPU/FPGA/neuromorphic) within tiny size and power budgets, specially designed motors and propellers.

5. Insect drones part 2: intelligence, swarms and soft robots

Parsimony [lecture]Insects achieve robust behaviour in complex, hostile environments with limited resources. Three pillars: embodiment (body, physiology and environment together shape behaviour), sensory-motor coordination (the insect's sensors-and-actuators loop), swarming (a colony outperforms an individual).
Fixing a misconception"Intelligence lives in the brain." Embodied cognition says it also depends on the body and how it senses and interacts with the world. This is why soft bodies (below) can "compute" through their shape.

The controller has three parts: biomechanics (choice of sensors and actuation), locomotion control (when, how fast), high-level cognitive control (direction, forward vs rotational movement).

Insect-inspired soft robots

Soft robots copy worms, larvae and snails. Gaits in the lecture: horizontal tumbling, vertical tumbling (jumping for height), gastropod-style crawling, wriggling, and inchworm crawling (e.g. approaching and grasping a tablet).

Larva-inspired rolling robot [lecture]Larvae have circular muscles (around the body) and axial muscles (along top and bottom). Contracting the axial side bends the body into a C-shape; sequenced actuation turns crawling into rolling. A pneumatic version has four internal chambers: pressurise one (it lengthens) while depressurising another, following a pressure-vs-time schedule per chamber, and the body rolls.

Blood-swimming micro-robots

Purpose: targeted drug delivery, e.g. to a tumour rather than the whole body. They copy the helical propulsion of E. coli flagella (artificial bacterial flagella, ABFs): a magnetic head and a rigid helical, planar-flexible or cilia-like tail. Control is from outside:

Field typeSuitsMotion
RotatingHelical robots, nanorods (free swimmers)Torque-driven rotation / corkscrew
Oscillating or on-offHead-and-tail planar designs, surface walkersPlanar tail beating; tumbling
GradientFree swimmers, groupsForce-driven linear or planar motion

Fabrication routes: self-scrolling (nickel head; etching curls the tail into a helix), GLAD (glancing-angle deposition), helical liposomes (palladium seed, cobalt-alloy tail), and 3D lithography (photoresist polymerisation, development, magnetic coating).

Why torque vs force mattersA uniform field only twists a magnet (torque, rotation). Only a field gradient pulls it (net force). So rotating fields drive corkscrew swimmers, and gradients drive linear motion, which is the lecture's "torque-driven → rotary; force-driven → planar".

6. Neural networks based on the human brain

The biological neuron [lecture]The neuron is the basic unit of the nervous system. Dendrites receive signals (inputs). The soma sums them and applies a threshold (processing, like a CPU). The axon carries the fired signal to terminals, which meet the next neuron's dendrites across a synapse. System loop: sensing organ → central nervous system → effector (actuator), with feedback.
Chemical vs electrical synapses [lecture]Over 95% of transmission is chemical: neurotransmitters cross the synaptic gap, delay > ~5 ms, supports complex tasks and lasting learning. Electrical: ion exchange, delay < 0.1 ms, bidirectional, used for fast reflexes (more common in snakes). Trade-off: speed vs ability to carry out learning.
Fixing a misconceptionThe lecture itself notes the brain's higher-order dendrites split repeatedly (primary, secondary, tertiary). An artificial neuron does not split inputs; instead you feed extra "features" such as x₁², x₁x₂, sin x₁. So the network is brain-inspired, not a copy (this is §1's how-vs-why distinction again).
Worked example (lecture numbers, completed)Inputs 0.1, 0.2, 0.6 with weights 1, 2, 1 (the third is implied by the lecturer's sum of 1.1). Products: 0.1, 0.4, 0.6. Sum = 1.1. Add bias 0.5: z = 1.6. Then apply f(z): sigmoid(1.6) ≈ 0.83; tanh(1.6) ≈ 0.92; ReLU(1.6) = 1.6; linear = 1.6. Training means adjusting the weights each iteration (epoch) so predictions approach the true output.

Evaluating and tuning

Error metrics: MAE = (1/n)Σ|y−ŷ|; MSE = (1/n)Σ(y−ŷ)²; RMSE = √MSE; R² = 1 − Σ(y−ŷ)²/Σ(y−ȳ)². A perfect model has R² = 1 and RMSE = 0. Squaring penalises big errors more (error −5 gives 5 under MAE but 25 under MSE). When several models give R² ≈ 1, RMSE separates them. Hyperparameters (neurons per layer, number of layers, learning rate, activation, features) are set before training; weights are learned during it. Tuning (e.g. with the Adam optimiser) hunts for low-RMSE regions: too few neurons in L1/L2 gives a high-RMSE peak, and a third layer was dispensable in the lecturer's study.

Case study [lecture]: battery state of charge and lifeA cascade predicts SOC from voltage and current, then power density, energy density, then life cycle, each stage feeding the next. Before the network: feature selection (keep inputs that matter, drop the rest; in the water-heating analogy keep flame and water amount, drop air pressure) and PCA (compress repetitive data). Wide NN beat narrow NN, SVM and decision tree in residual plots and RMSE; the cited paper reports R² = 1 for a tri-layer wide network (hidden layers of 289 and 12 neurons, tanh).

8. Self-test checklist

  • ☐ Define biomimetics, biomimicry and bionics, and give one example of each without notes.
  • ☐ Name three things that are not bio-inspired design and say why.
  • ☐ Match eight animals or plants to their products (kingfisher, boxfish, tadpole, snake, ear bones, beehive, burdock, skin).
  • ☐ Recite the four stages of wound healing and say which one the e-skin copies.
  • ☐ Derive Δp = gh(ρout−ρin) and compute it for h = 20 m, 5 K difference (about 4 Pa).
  • ☐ Explain how thermal mass delays a day's heat to night, and give two limits of termite-style ventilation.
  • ☐ Map head, antenna, wings, abdomen of an insect to drone parts.
  • ☐ Compute Re for a 2 mm insect at 0.5 m/s (about 67) and explain why it flaps rather than glides.
  • ☐ Explain why a gradient field gives force but a rotating field gives rotation in magnetic micro-robots.
  • ☐ Compute a neuron output by hand with bias (the 1.6 example) and apply sigmoid.
  • ☐ Say when to choose a wide, deep or narrow network, and classification or regression.
  • ☐ Compute MAE and RMSE for errors (−5, 3, 1) and explain which punishes the −5 more (MAE 3, RMSE ≈ 3.4).
