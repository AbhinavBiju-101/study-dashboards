13.1 Double Integrals: Basic Evaluation

\(\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA\) is evaluated as an iterated integral: integrate w.r.t. one variable first (holding the other fixed, exactly the reverse of a partial derivative), then integrate that result w.r.t. the second variable.

Worked Example

\(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 xy^2\,dy\,dx\). Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 xy^2\,dy=x\left[\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\frac{8x}{3}\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{8x}{3}\,dx=\frac83\cdot\frac12=\frac43\).

13.2 Change of Order of Integration

WHY this is true

When the inner limits depend on the outer variable, you are integrating over a non-rectangular region — the limits are really just a description of that region’s boundary. Changing order means re-describing the same region with \(x\) and \(y\)’s roles swapped, and the only reliable way to do that is to sketch the region first and read the new boundary curves directly off the picture. Attempting to swap limits by algebra alone, without the sketch, is the single biggest source of errors on this topic.

Worked Example

Change the order in \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_y^1 f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\).

Sketch (above): the region is the triangle with vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\), bounded by \(x=y\), \(x=1\), \(y=0\).

Reversed: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^x f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\).

13.3 Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

Switch to polar when: the region is a circle/sector/annulus, or the integrand involves \(x^2+y^2\).

WHY this is true

\(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), and crucially \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\) — not just \(dr\,d\theta\). Here’s why the extra \(r\) appears: a small “rectangle” in \((r,\theta)\)-space of size \(\Delta r\times\Delta\theta\) maps to a thin wedge-shaped patch in the \(xy\)-plane. That wedge has inner arc length \(r\,\Delta\theta\) and outer arc length \((r+\Delta r)\Delta\theta\), and radial thickness \(\Delta r\); its area is approximately (average arc length)\(\times\Delta r\approx r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta r\). So equal steps in \((r,\theta)\) sweep out more actual area the farther out you are (larger \(r\)) — the factor \(r\) corrects for that stretching, exactly analogous to the \(\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\) stretching factor for surfaces in Section 9.

Worked Example

\(\iint_R(x^2+y^2)\,dA,\ R: x^2+y^2\le4\).

Polar: \(x^2+y^2=r^2\); region becomes \(0\le r\le2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\).

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}4\,d\theta=8\pi. \]

13.4 Triple Integrals: Rectangular, Cylindrical, Spherical

Rectangular triple integrals are mechanically identical to double integrals with one more nested integral.

Worked Example

\(\iiint_V xyz\,dV\) over the box \([0,1]\times[0,2]\times[0,3]\). Since the box and integrand separate cleanly:

\[ \int_0^1 x\,dx\cdot\int_0^2 y\,dy\cdot\int_0^3 z\,dz=\frac12\cdot2\cdot\frac92=\frac92. \]

Cylindrical (circular symmetry about the \(z\)-axis): \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ z=z\), with \(dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\) — the same \(r\)-factor as polar, since cylindrical coordinates are just polar coordinates in \(x,y\) extended along \(z\).

Spherical (regions centered at the origin — spheres, cones): \(x=\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ y=\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ z=\rho\cos\varphi\), with

\[ dV=\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta. \]
Worked Example

Cylindrical: Volume under \(z=4-x^2-y^2\), above \(z=0\). Paraboloid meets \(z=0\) at \(r=2\) (since \(4-r^2=0\)); for fixed \(r,\theta\), \(z\) runs 0 to \(4-r^2\).

\[ V=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{4-r^2} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 r(4-r^2)\,dr\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\left[2r^2-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}4\,d\theta=8\pi. \]

Spherical: Volume of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le a^2\).

\[ V=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^a\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta=\underbrace{\frac{a^3}{3}}_{\rho\text{-integral}}\cdot\underbrace{2}_{\varphi\text{-integral}}\cdot\underbrace{2\pi}_{\theta\text{-integral}}=\frac43\pi a^3, \]

matching the familiar sphere-volume formula — confirming the setup is right.
