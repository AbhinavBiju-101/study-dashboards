25 Formation of Partial Differential Equations

↗ original PDF, page 42

Module 5 — Partial Differential Equations. Throughout, \(z=z(x,y)\) and we write \(p=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x},\ q=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\). A first-order PDE is a relation \(F(x,y,z,p,q)=0\).

WHY this is true

The one idea behind all of Module 5. A surface \(z=z(x,y)\) has, at each point \((x,y,z)\), a tangent plane determined by the slopes \((p,q)\). A first-order PDE is therefore a condition on tangent planes: “at every point, the tangent plane must have slopes \((p,q)\) satisfying \(F=0\)”. Every method below is a way of building surfaces whose tangent planes obey the condition: planes themselves (Sections 26, 29), surfaces whose shape depends on a single combination of \(x,y\) (Section 27), surfaces swept out by special curves (Section 30), or products of one-variable functions (Section 31).

Definition

A PDE is formed by eliminating from a given family of surfaces either

  • arbitrary constants \(a,b\) in \(z=F(x,y,a,b)\), using \(p=F_x,\ q=F_y\) (result: first order), or
  • an arbitrary function, e.g. in \(z=f(u(x,y))\) or \(\phi(u,v)=0\) (result: first order).
WHY this is true

Counting. From \(z=F(x,y,a,b)\) we get three equations: \(z=F,\ p=F_x,\ q=F_y\). They contain two unknown constants \(a,b\); eliminating both leaves one relation among \(x,y,z,p,q\) — a first-order PDE. With more constants than two, first derivatives are not enough and second derivatives enter. With an arbitrary function \(f\), differentiating gives \(p\) and \(q\) in terms of \(f'\); dividing the two removes \(f'\) and leaves a PDE with no constants and no function: it is satisfied by every member of the family, and that is why a PDE has “more solutions than an ODE” — its general solution contains an arbitrary function.

↗ original PDF, page 43
Worked Example

(1) Constants: \(z=(x+a)(y+b)\). \(p=y+b,\ q=x+a\), so \(z=pq\).

(2) Constants: \(z=ax+by+a^2+b^2\). \(p=a,\ q=b\), so \(z=px+qy+p^2+q^2\).

(3) Function: \(z=f(x^2+y^2)\). \(p=2xf',\ q=2yf'\); dividing, \(\dfrac pq=\dfrac xy\), so \(yp-xq=0\). (Figure 13 explains the meaning.)

(4) Function: \(z=f(y/x)\). \(p=-\dfrac{y}{x^2}f',\ q=\dfrac1xf'\), hence \(xp+yq=0\).

(5) Two functions: \(z=f(x+y)+g(x-y)\). \(z_{xx}=f''+g''=z_{yy}\), so \(\dfrac{\partial^2z}{\partial x^2}=\dfrac{\partial^2z}{\partial y^2}\) — the wave equation. Removing two arbitrary functions needs second derivatives.

General case: \(\phi(u,v)=0\), with \(u,v\) functions of \(x,y,z\)

WHY this is true

Differentiate \(\phi(u,v)=0\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) (remembering \(z\) depends on both):

\[ \phi_u(u_x+u_zp)+\phi_v(v_x+v_zp)=0,\qquad\phi_u(u_y+u_zq)+\phi_v(v_y+v_zq)=0. \]

These are two linear equations for \((\phi_u,\phi_v)\) which must have a non-zero solution, so the determinant vanishes:

\[ (u_x+u_zp)(v_y+v_zq)-(u_y+u_zq)(v_x+v_zp)=0. \]

Expanding (the \(pq\) terms cancel) and collecting \(p\) and \(q\) gives

\[ P\,p+Q\,q=R,\qquad P=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(y,z)},\ Q=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(z,x)},\ R=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}. \]

The coefficients of the resulting PDE are Jacobians (Section 17).

Worked Example

\(\phi(x+y+z,\ x^2+y^2+z^2)=0\), so \(u=x+y+z,\ v=x^2+y^2+z^2\). Then \(P=u_yv_z-u_zv_y=2z-2y\), \(Q=u_zv_x-u_xv_z=2x-2z\), \(R=u_xv_y-u_yv_x=2y-2x\). Dividing by 2 and changing signs:

\[ (y-z)\,p+(z-x)\,q=x-y. \]

(This exact equation is solved in reverse in Section 30, giving back \(\phi(x+y+z,\ x^2+y^2+z^2)=0\).)

26 Equations of the Form \(F(p,q)=0\)

Definition

Method. Assume \(z=ax+by+c\), so \(p=a,\ q=b\). The PDE forces \(F(a,b)=0\); solve \(b=\varphi(a)\). The complete integral is

\[ z=ax+\varphi(a)\,y+c. \]
↗ original PDF, page 44
WHY this is true

Why planes work. \(F\) contains only \(p\) and \(q\) — no \(x,y,z\). So it only constrains the slopes of the tangent plane, not where the plane is. A plane \(z=ax+by+c\) has the same tangent plane (itself) everywhere, with constant slopes \((a,b)\); it is a solution exactly when \((a,b)\) satisfies \(F(a,b)=0\). This gives a two-parameter family (free: \(a\) and \(c\)), the complete integral.

The general integral. Choose \(c=\psi(a)\) with an arbitrary \(\psi\) to get a one-parameter family of planes \(z=ax+\varphi(a)y+\psi(a)\), and form its envelope by setting \(\partial/\partial a=0\): \(0=x+\varphi'(a)y+\psi'(a)\), then eliminate \(a\). The envelope is again a solution because at each of its points it touches one of the planes, sharing that plane’s position and slopes \((p,q)\), and the PDE depends only on position and slopes.

Fixing a Common Misconception

“The only solutions are the planes \(z=ax+by+c\).” Planes give only the complete integral. For \(p^2+q^2=1\) the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) also works (\(p=x/r,\ q=y/r\), so \(p^2+q^2=1\)), yet it is not a plane; it is the envelope of the planes \(z=x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha\) (take \(c=0\) and eliminate \(\alpha\)), i.e. it comes from the general integral.

Worked Example

(1) \(p+q=pq\). \(a+b=ab\Rightarrow b=\dfrac{a}{a-1}\). Complete integral: \(z=ax+\dfrac{a}{a-1}y+c\).

(2) \(\sqrt p+\sqrt q=1\). \(b=(1-\sqrt a)^2\) (for \(0\le a\le1\)): \(z=ax+(1-\sqrt a)^2y+c\).

(3) \(q=p^2\). \(b=a^2\): \(z=ax+a^2y+c\). General integral with \(c=0\): \(\partial/\partial a\) gives \(x+2ay=0\), so \(a=-\dfrac{x}{2y}\) and \(z=-\dfrac{x^2}{2y}+\dfrac{x^2}{4y}=-\dfrac{x^2}{4y}\). Check: \(p=-\dfrac{x}{2y},\ q=\dfrac{x^2}{4y^2}=p^2\). ✓

27 Equations of the Form \(F(z,p,q)=0\)

Definition

Method. Put \(u=x+ay\) and look for \(z=f(u)\). Then \(p=\dfrac{dz}{du},\ q=a\dfrac{dz}{du}\), and the PDE becomes an ODE in \(z\) and \(u\). Solve it and replace \(u\) by \(x+ay\).

WHY this is true

Why this substitution. Because \(F(z,p,q)=0\) contains no explicit \(x\) or \(y\), shifting the whole picture sideways in the \(xy\)-plane maps solutions to solutions (translation invariance). So we can look for solutions that are constant along a whole family of parallel lines \(x+ay=\) const — surfaces that depend on position only through \(u=x+ay\). For such a surface, \(z=f(u)\) gives \(p=f'(u)\cdot1\) and \(q=f'(u)\cdot a\) by the chain rule. The parameter \(a\) (the direction of the lines) is the free constant, and integrating the ODE introduces a second one: a complete integral.

↗ original PDF, page 45
Fixing a Common Misconception

Using \(u=x+ay\) on any PDE. It only works when \(x\) and \(y\) do not appear explicitly. For \(pq=xz\) the explicit \(x\) destroys the translation invariance, and the substitution does not reduce to an ODE in \(u\) alone.

Worked Example

(1) \(p(1+q)=qz\). With \(p=z',\ q=az'\): \(z'(1+az')=az'z\Rightarrow1+az'=az\). So \(\dfrac{dz}{az-1}=\dfrac{du}{a}\), giving \(\ln(az-1)=u+\) const:

\[ az-1=Ce^{x+ay}\iff z=\frac{1+Ce^{x+ay}}{a}. \]

(2) \(pq=z\). \(az'^2=z\Rightarrow\dfrac{dz}{\sqrt z}=\dfrac{du}{\sqrt a}\Rightarrow2\sqrt z=\dfrac{u+b}{\sqrt a}\), i.e. \(4az=(x+ay+b)^2\).

(3) \(z^2(p^2+q^2+1)=1\). \(z^2\left[(1+a^2)z'^2+1\right]=1\Rightarrow\dfrac{z\,dz}{\sqrt{1-z^2}}=\dfrac{du}{\sqrt{1+a^2}}\). Integrating, \(-\sqrt{1-z^2}=\dfrac{u+b}{\sqrt{1+a^2}}\), so

\[ (x+ay+b)^2=(1+a^2)(1-z^2), \]

a family of ellipsoids/spheres.

28 Equations of the Form \(F(x,p)=G(y,q)\)

↗ original PDF, page 46
Definition

Method. Set both sides equal to a constant \(a\): \(F(x,p)=a=G(y,q)\). Solve \(p=f(x,a)\) and \(q=g(y,a)\), then integrate \(dz=p\,dx+q\,dy\):

\[ z=\int f(x,a)\,dx+\int g(y,a)\,dy+b. \]
WHY this is true

The left side involves only \(x\) (and \(p\)), the right only \(y\) (and \(q\)). We look for solutions in which \(p\) depends on \(x\) alone and \(q\) on \(y\) alone. Then the equation says “a function of \(x\) equals a function of \(y\) for all \(x\) and \(y\)”. Vary \(x\) while keeping \(y\) fixed: the right side does not move, so the left side cannot either. Hence both are the same constant \(a\). This is separation of variables applied to the slopes \(p,q\) rather than to \(z\).

Fixing a Common Misconception

“\(p+q=x+y\) can’t be separated because \(x\) and \(y\) are mixed.” Rearrange first: \(p-x=y-q\) has the form \(F(x,p)=G(y,q)\). Then \(p=x+a,\ q=y-a\), and \(z=\frac{x^2}{2}+ax+\frac{y^2}{2}-ay+b\). Always try to move all \(x,p\)-terms to one side and all \(y,q\)-terms to the other.

Worked Example

(1) \(p^2+q^2=x+y\). \(p^2-x=y-q^2=a\): \(p=\sqrt{x+a},\ q=\sqrt{y-a}\), so \(z=\frac23(x+a)^{3/2}+\frac23(y-a)^{3/2}+b\).

(2) \(pe^y=qe^x\). \(pe^{-x}=qe^{-y}=a\): \(p=ae^x,\ q=ae^y\), so \(z=a(e^x+e^y)+b\).

(3) \(p^2y(1+x^2)=qx^2\). Divide both sides by \(x^2y\): \(\dfrac{p^2(1+x^2)}{x^2}=\dfrac qy=a\), so \(p=\dfrac{\sqrt a\,x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}},\ q=ay\) and \(z=\sqrt a\sqrt{1+x^2}+\dfrac{ay^2}{2}+b\). Check: \(p^2y(1+x^2)=\dfrac{ax^2}{1+x^2}y(1+x^2)=ax^2y=qx^2\). ✓

29 Clairaut’s Form and the Singular Solution

↗ original PDF, page 47
Definition
\[ z=px+qy+f(p,q). \]

Complete integral: replace \(p,q\) by constants: \(z=ax+by+f(a,b)\). Singular solution: differentiate the complete integral with respect to \(a\) and \(b\): \(x+f_a=0,\ y+f_b=0\); eliminate \(a,b\).

WHY this is true

Why the complete integral is this simple. If \(z=ax+by+f(a,b)\) then \(p=a,\ q=b\), and substituting gives \(z=ax+by+f(a,b)\) — an identity. So replacing \(p,q\) by constants is a solution.

What the singular solution is. The complete integral is a two-parameter family of planes. Their envelope — the surface tangent to every plane of the family — is the singular solution. Its tangent plane at each point is one of the family’s planes, so it satisfies the same PDE, yet it cannot be obtained by fixing \(a,b\). The conditions \(\partial/\partial a=\partial/\partial b=0\) are exactly the envelope conditions. The one-variable picture below shows the same thing: lines \(y=ax+a^2\) (Clairaut’s ODE \(y=xy'+y'^2\)) and their envelope, the parabola \(y=-x^2/4\).

Fixing a Common Misconception

“\(z=-xy\) solves \(z=px+qy+pq\) by putting \(a=-y,\ b=-x\) in the complete integral.” The constants \(a,b\) must stay constant; substituting functions is not allowed. \(z=-xy\) is a solution (\(p=-y,\ q=-x\): \(px+qy+pq=-xy-xy+xy=-xy\)) but it is the singular solution — the envelope — obtained by \(x+b=0,\ y+a=0\).

↗ original PDF, page 48
Worked Example

(1) \(z=px+qy+p^2+q^2\). Complete: \(z=ax+by+a^2+b^2\). Differentiating: \(x+2a=0,\ y+2b=0\), so \(a=-\frac x2,\ b=-\frac y2\), and \(z=-\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{4}=-\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{4}\), i.e. \(4z=-(x^2+y^2)\).

(2) \(z=px+qy+\sqrt{1+p^2+q^2}\). Complete: \(z=ax+by+s\) with \(s=\sqrt{1+a^2+b^2}\). Then \(x=-\dfrac as,\ y=-\dfrac bs,\ z=-\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{s}+s=\dfrac1s\), so \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\dfrac{a^2+b^2+1}{s^2}=1\): the singular solution is the unit sphere (tangent planes of a sphere are exactly this family).

(3) Reduce to Clairaut first. \((p+q)(z-xp-yq)=1\) rearranges to \(z=px+qy+\dfrac{1}{p+q}\): Clairaut form with \(f=\dfrac{1}{p+q}\). Complete integral: \(z=ax+by+\dfrac{1}{a+b}\).

30 Lagrange’s Linear Equation \(Pp+Qq=R\)

↗ original PDF, page 49
Definition

Here \(P,Q,R\) are functions of \(x,y,z\). The auxiliary (subsidiary) equations are

\[ \frac{dx}{P}=\frac{dy}{Q}=\frac{dz}{R}. \]

If \(u(x,y,z)=c_1\) and \(v(x,y,z)=c_2\) are two independent solutions, the general solution is \(\phi(u,v)=0\) (\(\phi\) arbitrary), or equivalently \(v=f(u)\).

WHY this is true

Step 1: what the PDE says geometrically. A surface \(z=z(x,y)\) has normal \((p,q,-1)\). The equation \(Pp+Qq=R\) says \((P,Q,R)\cdot(p,q,-1)=0\): the vector \((P,Q,R)\) is perpendicular to the normal, so it is tangent to the surface at every point. An integral surface is therefore a surface to which the vector field \((P,Q,R)\) is everywhere tangent.

Step 2: such surfaces are woven from characteristic curves. Follow a curve with \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=P,\ \dfrac{dy}{dt}=Q\) starting on the surface. By the chain rule along the surface, \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}=p\dfrac{dx}{dt}+q\dfrac{dy}{dt}=pP+qQ=R\). So the curve has tangent \((P,Q,R)\) and stays on the surface: eliminating \(t\) gives the auxiliary equations. The surface is a union of such curves.

Step 3: the general solution. The curves are \(u=c_1,\ v=c_2\) (two independent first integrals). A surface made of a one-parameter family of them is obtained by linking the parameters through any relation \(\phi(c_1,c_2)=0\) — hence \(\phi(u,v)=0\), with \(\phi\) arbitrary. This also matches Section 25, where eliminating an arbitrary \(\phi\) from \(\phi(u,v)=0\) produced exactly this PDE.

Solving the auxiliary equations

WHY this is true

Two techniques. (i) Grouping: if two ratios involve only two variables, integrate directly, e.g. \(\frac{dx}{P}=\frac{dy}{Q}\). (ii) Multipliers: for any functions \(l,m,n\), equal ratios satisfy

\[ \frac{dx}{P}=\frac{dy}{Q}=\frac{dz}{R}=\frac{l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz}{lP+mQ+nR}. \]

(If every ratio equals \(\kappa\), then \(dx=\kappa P\), etc., so the numerator is \(\kappa(lP+mQ+nR)\).) Choose \(l,m,n\) so that \(lP+mQ+nR=0\); then \(l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz=0\), and if that is an exact differential you have a first integral.

↗ original PDF, page 50
Worked Example

(1) \(xp+yq=z\). \(\frac{dx}{x}=\frac{dy}{y}=\frac{dz}{z}\) gives \(\frac yx=c_1,\ \frac zx=c_2\). Solution: \(\phi\left(\frac yx,\frac zx\right)=0\), i.e. \(z=x\,f(y/x)\) — cones, as in the figure.

(2) \(yz\,p+zx\,q=xy\). \(\frac{dx}{yz}=\frac{dy}{zx}=\frac{dz}{xy}\). From the first two, \(x\,dx=y\,dy\); from the last two, \(y\,dy=z\,dz\). So \(x^2-y^2=c_1,\ y^2-z^2=c_2\): \(\phi(x^2-y^2,\ y^2-z^2)=0\).

(3) \((y-z)p+(z-x)q=x-y\) (multipliers). With \((l,m,n)=(1,1,1)\): \(lP+mQ+nR=(y-z)+(z-x)+(x-y)=0\), so \(d(x+y+z)=0\), giving \(x+y+z=c_1\). With \((l,m,n)=(x,y,z)\): \(x(y-z)+y(z-x)+z(x-y)=0\), so \(x\,dx+y\,dy+z\,dz=0\), giving \(x^2+y^2+z^2=c_2\). Solution:

\[ \phi(x+y+z,\ x^2+y^2+z^2)=0. \]

This is exactly the surface family from which Section 25 formed this PDE.

(4) \((y+z)p+(z+x)q=x+y\). Taking differences: \(\dfrac{d(x-y)}{-(x-y)}=\dfrac{d(y-z)}{-(y-z)}\), so \(\dfrac{x-y}{y-z}=c_1\). Using \((1,1,1)\) (numerator \(d(x+y+z)\), denominator \(2(x+y+z)\)) against \(\dfrac{d(x-y)}{-(x-y)}\): \(\dfrac{d(x+y+z)}{2(x+y+z)}=-\dfrac{d(x-y)}{x-y}\), so \((x-y)^2(x+y+z)=c_2\). Solution: \(\phi\left(\dfrac{x-y}{y-z},\ (x-y)^2(x+y+z)\right)=0\).

Fixing a Common Misconception

“The general solution is \(u+v=c\).” The general solution is \(\phi(u,v)=0\) with \(\phi\) arbitrary. Writing \(u+v=c\) picks one specific \(\phi\) and gives only a one-parameter family — far fewer solutions than the PDE has.

31 Linear First-Order PDEs by Separation of Variables

↗ original PDF, page 51
Definition

Method. Assume \(u=X(x)Y(y)\) (or \(X(x)T(t)\)). Substitute and rearrange so that each side depends on one variable only; set both equal to a separation constant \(k\). Solve the two ODEs, form the product, and use the given condition to fix \(k\) and the multiplicative constant.

WHY this is true

Why a constant? After substitution one side is a function of \(x\) only and the other a function of \(y\) (or \(t\)) only, and they are equal for all values of both variables — so each equals the same constant \(k\) (as in Section 28). Why does the product form lose nothing? For a linear PDE, sums of solutions are solutions (superposition), so if the given condition is a sum of exponentials, we solve each term separately and add.

Worked Example

(1) \(u_x=2u_t+u\), \(u(x,0)=6e^{-3x}\). \(u=XT\): \(X'T=2XT'+XT\Rightarrow\dfrac{X'}{X}=2\dfrac{T'}{T}+1=k\). So \(X=e^{kx},\ T=e^{(k-1)t/2},\ u=Ce^{kx+(k-1)t/2}\). At \(t=0\): \(Ce^{kx}=6e^{-3x}\), so \(C=6,\ k=-3\):

\[ u=6e^{-3x-2t}. \]

(2) \(3u_x+2u_y=0\), \(u(x,0)=4e^{-x}\). \(\dfrac{X'}{X}=-\dfrac23\dfrac{Y'}{Y}=k\): \(u=Ce^{kx-3ky/2}\). Condition: \(C=4,\ k=-1\), so \(u=4e^{-x+3y/2}\).

(3) Superposition. If instead \(u(x,0)=6e^{-3x}+4e^{-5x}\) in (1), each term has its own \(k\) (\(-3\) and \(-5\)): \(u=6e^{-3x-2t}+4e^{-5x-3t}\).

Connection — where this reappears

Example (2) is also a Lagrange equation (\(P=3,\ Q=2,\ R=0\)): the auxiliary equations give \(2x-3y=c_1,\ u=c_2\), so \(u=f(2x-3y)\). The separated answer \(4e^{-x+3y/2}=4e^{-(2x-3y)/2}\) is exactly of this form with \(f(s)=4e^{-s/2}\). Two different methods, one answer — and Lagrange’s method shows that every solution (not just exponential ones) is a function of \(2x-3y\).
