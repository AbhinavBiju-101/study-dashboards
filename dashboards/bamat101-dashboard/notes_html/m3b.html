6 Scalar and Vector Potential

↗ original PDF, page 25
WHY this is true

This is where irrotational/solenoidal fields become useful, not just classified. If \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\), then a scalar function \(\varphi\) exists with \(\mathbf{F}=\nabla\varphi\) (this is provable in general using Stokes’ Theorem, Section 10: it forces every line integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) between two fixed points to be path-independent, which is exactly the condition under which a potential exists). In practice, you don’t need the general existence proof — you just need to know when to look for \(\varphi\) (curl is zero) and how to find it, shown below.

Definition

If \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\), then \(\mathbf{F}=\nabla\varphi\) for some scalar \(\varphi\), called the scalar potential.
If \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=0\), then \(\mathbf{F}=\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{A}\) for some vector \(\mathbf{A}\), called the vector potential.

Finding \(\varphi\): integrate \(F_1\) w.r.t. \(x\) (adding an unknown function of \(y,z\) instead of a bare constant); differentiate the result w.r.t. \(y\) and match against \(F_2\) to pin down that unknown function; repeat against \(F_3\) for \(z\).

Worked Example

Find \(\varphi\) for \(\mathbf{F}=(y^2+2xz^2)\mathbf{i}+2xy\mathbf{j}+2x^2z\mathbf{k}\) (confirmed irrotational above).
Step 1. \(\varphi=\int(y^2+2xz^2)\,dx=xy^2+x^2z^2+g(y,z)\).
Step 2. \(\partial\varphi/\partial y=2xy+\partial g/\partial y\). Match to \(F_2=2xy\): forces \(\partial g/\partial y=0\), so \(g=h(z)\).
Step 3. \(\varphi=xy^2+x^2z^2+h(z)\); \(\partial\varphi/\partial z=2x^2z+h'(z)\). Match to \(F_3=2x^2z\): forces \(h'(z)=0\), so \(h=\text{const}\).

\[ \varphi=xy^2+x^2z^2+C. \]

Check: \(\nabla\varphi=(y^2+2xz^2)\mathbf{i}+2xy\mathbf{j}+2x^2z\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{F}\). ✓

7 Line Integrals

↗ original PDF, page 26
Definition

For a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) and a curve \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\), parametrized as \(\mathbf{r}(t)\):

\[ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt \]

represents the total work done by \(\mathbf{F}\) moving a particle along \(C\).

WHY this is true

Why this integral means “work.” Break \(C\) into many tiny steps \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_i\). Over each tiny step, the work contributed is approximately (force)·(displacement) \(=\mathbf{F}(P_i)\cdot\Delta\mathbf{r}_i\) — the same dot-product idea as ordinary physics work, just over an infinitesimal straight step where \(\mathbf{F}\) is roughly constant. Summing over all the little steps gives a Riemann sum \(\sum\mathbf{F}(P_i)\cdot\Delta\mathbf{r}_i\), and letting the steps shrink to zero turns that sum into the integral \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\). Parametrizing by \(t\) is simply the bookkeeping device that turns the curved, multi-dimensional path into a single ordinary 1-D integral over \(t\) — this is the entire reason parametrization is the “whole game” here: it’s what makes an otherwise geometric object computable with single-variable calculus.

Worked Example

\(\mathbf{F}=x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}\). Evaluate \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) along the straight line from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), and separately along the parabola \(y=x^2\) between the same two points.
Straight line: \(x=t,y=t,\ t:0\to1\). \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=x\,dx+y\,dy=t\,dt+t\,dt=2t\,dt\).

\[ \int_0^1 2t\,dt=1. \]

Parabola: \(x=t,y=t^2,\ t:0\to1\), so \(dy=2t\,dt\). \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=t\,dt+t^2(2t\,dt)=(t+2t^3)dt\).

\[ \int_0^1 (t+2t^3)\,dt=\left[\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac12+\frac12=1. \]

Both paths give 1 — not a coincidence. It happens because this particular \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative (curl-free, with potential \(\varphi=\tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\), Section 6), and Section 10’s discussion of Stokes’ Theorem explains why curl-free forces path-independence.

8 Green’s Theorem in the Plane

↗ original PDF, page 27
Definition

For a closed curve \(C\) (counterclockwise) bounding a plane region \(R\), and \(\mathbf{F}=M(x,y)\mathbf{i}+N(x,y)\mathbf{j}\):

\[ \oint_C (M\,dx+N\,dy)=\iint_R\left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right)dA \]
WHY this is true

Why this is true — the cancellation argument. Chop \(R\) into a fine grid of tiny rectangles. For each small rectangle, the “local circulation” (line integral around its own tiny boundary, counterclockwise) can be computed directly and, by exactly the same face-by-face argument used for curl in Section 5, works out to \(\left(\partial N/\partial x-\partial M/\partial y\right)dA\).
Now add up the circulation around every tiny rectangle in the grid. Look at any edge shared by two neighboring rectangles: it gets traversed once by each rectangle, but in opposite directions (since both rectangles go counterclockwise) — so its two contributions exactly cancel. This happens for every internal edge. The only edges that don’t get cancelled are the ones on the outer boundary of \(R\), which together trace out \(C\) exactly once, counterclockwise.
So: (sum of all tiny local circulations) = (circulation around the outer boundary \(C\)), and simultaneously (sum of all tiny local circulations) \(=\iint_R(\partial N/\partial x-\partial M/\partial y)\,dA\) by definition of the double integral as a limit of a Riemann sum. Equate the two and you have Green’s Theorem.

Worked Example

Evaluate \(\oint_C(xy\,dx+x^2\,dy)\), \(C=\) boundary of the unit square \([0,1]\times[0,1]\), counterclockwise. \(M=xy,\ N=x^2\). \(\partial N/\partial x=2x,\ \partial M/\partial y=x\). So integrand becomes \(2x-x=x\).

\[ \oint_C=\iint_R x\,dA=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 x\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 x\,dx=\tfrac12. \]

This reused the exact double-integral machinery from Module 2 — confirming that Green’s Theorem is precisely where Modules 2 and 3 meet.

9 Surfaces and Surface Integrals

↗ original PDF, page 28
Definition

For a surface \(S\) with unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) at each point, and vector field \(\mathbf{F}\), the flux integral

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS \]

measures the net rate \(\mathbf{F}\) “flows through” \(S\).

WHY this is true

Why you need \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) at all. Only the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) perpendicular to the surface actually crosses — a component of \(\mathbf{F}\) lying flat within the surface slides along it without ever passing through. Dotting with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) extracts exactly that perpendicular component, which is why flux uses a dot product with the normal (rather than, say, the raw magnitude of \(\mathbf{F}\)).
Why \(dS\neq dA\) for a tilted surface. If a surface is tilted relative to the \(xy\)-plane, a small patch of surface area \(dS\) projects down onto a smaller patch \(dA\) in the \(xy\)-plane — the more tilted the surface, the more the projected area shrinks relative to the true surface area. For \(z=g(x,y)\), this stretching factor works out to

\[ dS=\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\right)^2}\;dA, \]

which you can derive by noting that a small rectangle in \(x,y\) maps to a small parallelogram on the surface spanned by the tangent vectors \((1,0,g_x)\) and \((0,1,g_y)\), and the parallelogram’s area is the magnitude of their cross product — which simplifies to exactly this square root.

Worked Example

Flux of \(\mathbf{F}=z\mathbf{k}\) upward through \(z=4-x-y\) over the square \(0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le1\).
\(g=4-x-y,\ g_x=-1,g_y=-1\). Upward normal \(\propto(1,1,1)\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3\), and \(dS=\sqrt3\,dA\).
\(\mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS=z\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\sqrt3\,dA=z\,dA=(4-x-y)\,dA\).

\[ \iint_S=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1(4-x-y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1(3.5-x)\,dx=3.5-0.5=3. \]

10 Stokes’ Theorem

↗ original PDF, page 29
Definition

For a surface \(S\) bounded by a closed curve \(C\) (right-hand-rule orientation: curl fingers along \(C\), thumb along \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\)):

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_S(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS \]
WHY this is true

Stokes’ Theorem is Green’s Theorem lifted into 3-D. If \(S\) happens to be flat and sits in the \(xy\)-plane, then \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{k}\), and \((\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\) works out (Section 5) to exactly \(\partial N/\partial x-\partial M/\partial y\) — so Stokes’ Theorem literally becomes Green’s Theorem in that special case. The general proof uses the same tiny-cell cancellation idea from Section 9, just applied to a curved mesh of tiny patches covering \(S\) instead of a flat grid: the local circulation around each tiny patch equals \((\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\) for that patch (by the same argument as Section 5, run in the tangent plane of the patch), internal shared edges cancel between neighboring patches, and only the outer rim \(C\) survives.
The practical payoff: since any surface bounded by \(C\) gives the same answer, you are always free to pick the flattest, simplest surface stretched across \(C\) (usually a flat disk) rather than whatever oddly-shaped surface the question describes.

Worked Example

\(\mathbf{F}=y\mathbf{i}+z\mathbf{j}+x\mathbf{k}\); \(C=\) circle \(x^2+y^2=1,\ z=0\), counterclockwise viewed from \(+z\).
Pick \(S=\) the flat disk \(x^2+y^2\le1\) in \(z=0\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{k}\).
\(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\): \(\mathbf{i}\)-comp \(=\partial x/\partial y-\partial z/\partial z=0-1=-1\); \(\mathbf{j}\)-comp\(=-[\partial x/\partial x-\partial y/\partial z]=-[1-0]=-1\);
\(\mathbf{k}\)-comp\(=\partial z/\partial x-\partial y/\partial y=0-1=-1\).
\(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}-\mathbf{k}\), so \((\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}=-1\).

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_S(-1)\,dA=-(\text{area of unit disk})=-\pi. \]

11 Volume Integrals

A volume integral \(\iiint_V f\,dV\) (scalar) or \(\iiint_V\mathbf{F}\,dV\) (vector, done componentwise) is mechanically identical to the triple integrals of Module 2 — no new technique. Its role here is purely as the right-hand side of the Divergence Theorem below.

12 The Gauss Divergence Theorem

Definition

For a closed surface \(S\) enclosing a solid region \(V\), with outward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\):

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS=\iiint_V(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F})\,dV \]
WHY this is true

This is the same cancellation idea as Green’s Theorem (Section 9), one dimension further up. Chop \(V\) into a fine 3-D grid of tiny cubes. The flux out of a single tiny cube, computed directly, equals \((\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F})\,dV\) for that cube — this is exactly the box argument used to define divergence in Section 4, just packaged as a flux statement rather than a limit. Now sum the outward flux over every tiny cube in the grid. Any face shared between two neighboring cubes gets counted twice: once as “outflow” from one cube, once as “inflow” into its neighbor through the very same face — and these two contributions are equal and opposite, so they cancel. This happens at every internal face. Only the faces on the outer boundary \(S\) survive uncancelled. So: (sum of all tiny local outflows) = (total flux through \(S\)), and also (sum of all tiny local outflows) \(=\iiint_V\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dV\) by definition of the triple integral as a limit of a Riemann sum. Equate the two.

↗ original PDF, page 31
Worked Example

Verify the Divergence Theorem for \(\mathbf{F}=x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k}\) over the unit cube \([0,1]^3\).
Volume side: \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=1+1+1=3\). \(\iiint_V 3\,dV=3\times1=3\).
Surface side (face by face): on \(x=1\) (\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{i}\)): \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=x=1\), area 1, contributes 1. On \(x=0\) (\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=-\mathbf{i}\)): \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=-x=0\), contributes 0. By the same pattern, the \(y=1/y=0\) pair contributes \(1+0\), and the \(z=1/z=0\) pair contributes \(1+0\).
Total flux \(=1+0+1+0+1+0=3\).
Both sides equal 3. ✓ This cube/sphere/cylinder verification style is the most frequently asked question type for this theorem — practice it until it’s automatic.
