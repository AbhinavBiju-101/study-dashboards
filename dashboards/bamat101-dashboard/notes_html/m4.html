14.1 The Homogeneous Case: Complementary Function

Standard form: \(ay''+by'+cy=0\).

WHY this is true

Why try \(y=e^{mx}\)? An exponential is the only kind of function that reproduces itself (up to a constant multiple) under differentiation — \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{mx}=m\,e^{mx}\). That self-reproducing property is exactly what’s needed to make a linear combination of \(y,y',y''\) collapse to zero for every \(x\), since every term will share the common factor \(e^{mx}\). Substituting \(y=e^{mx}\):

\[ am^2e^{mx}+bme^{mx}+ce^{mx}=0\implies e^{mx}(am^2+bm+c)=0. \]

Since \(e^{mx}\) is never zero, this forces \(am^2+bm+c=0\) — the auxiliary equation. The entire theory of constant-coefficient linear ODEs reduces to solving this one ordinary quadratic and reading off what kind of solution each type of root produces.

Case 1 — real, distinct roots \(m_1\neq m_2\):

\[ y_{CF}=C_1e^{m_1x}+C_2e^{m_2x}. \]

Case 2 — real, equal roots \(m_1=m_2=m\):

\[ y_{CF}=(C_1+C_2x)\,e^{mx}. \]
WHY this is true

Why the extra factor of \(x\)? With a repeated root, \(e^{mx}\) alone gives only one independent solution — a second-order ODE needs two. You can find the second one by treating \(m_2=m_1+\varepsilon\) as a nearby distinct root and taking the limit \(\varepsilon\to0\) of \(\dfrac{e^{(m_1+\varepsilon)x}-e^{m_1x}}{\varepsilon}\), which is precisely the definition of \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m}e^{mx}=x\,e^{mx}\) evaluated at \(m=m_1\). So \(xe^{mx}\) emerges naturally as the “other” solution hiding at the repeated root, and direct substitution confirms it indeed solves the ODE.

Case 3 — complex roots \(m=\alpha\pm i\beta\):

\[ y_{CF}=e^{\alpha x}(C_1\cos\beta x+C_2\sin\beta x). \]
WHY this is true

By Euler’s formula, \(e^{(\alpha+i\beta)x}=e^{\alpha x}(\cos\beta x+i\sin\beta x)\). Since the ODE is linear with real coefficients, the real part and imaginary part of this complex solution each independently solve the ODE — giving the two real, independent solutions \(e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x\) and \(e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x\) used above.

Worked Example

\(y''-5y'+6y=0\): \(m^2-5m+6=0\Rightarrow(m-2)(m-3)=0\Rightarrow m=2,3\). \(y_{CF}=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{3x}\).

\(y''-4y'+4y=0\): \(m^2-4m+4=0\Rightarrow(m-2)^2=0\Rightarrow m=2\) (repeated). \(y_{CF}=(C_1+C_2x)e^{2x}\).

\(y''+2y'+5y=0\): \(m=\dfrac{-2\pm\sqrt{4-20}}{2}=-1\pm2i\). \(y_{CF}=e^{-x}(C_1\cos2x+C_2\sin2x)\).

14.2 Method of Undetermined Coefficients: Particular Integral

For \(ay''+by'+cy=R(x)\), the full solution is \(y=y_{CF}+y_{PI}\), where \(y_{PI}\) is any one solution to the complete equation.

WHY this is true

The core idea. Since differentiation of \(e^{kx}\), \(\sin kx/\cos kx\), and polynomials always produces more terms of the same family (an exponential differentiates to an exponential, a degree-\(n\) polynomial differentiates to degree \(n-1\) but its combination with lower terms still spans the same polynomial family, etc.), a linear combination of \(y,y',y''\) built from a guess in that same family can be made to match \(R(x)\) exactly by choosing the right coefficients. This is why the guess-and-match method works at all — it isn’t accidental, it’s guaranteed by which functions are closed under differentiation.

Worked Example

Exponential RHS. \(y''-5y'+6y=e^{4x}\) (CF as above: \(C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{3x}\)).

Try \(y_{PI}=Ae^{4x}\): \(y_{PI}'=4Ae^{4x}\), \(y_{PI}''=16Ae^{4x}\). Substitute:

\[ 16Ae^{4x}-20Ae^{4x}+6Ae^{4x}=e^{4x}\Rightarrow2A=1\Rightarrow A=\tfrac12. \] \[ y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{3x}+\tfrac12e^{4x}. \]

\[ (-4A+A)\cos2x+(-4B+B)\sin2x=\cos2x\Rightarrow-3A=1,\ -3B=0\Rightarrow A=-\tfrac13,\ B=0. \] \[ y=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x-\tfrac13\cos2x. \]

14.3 The Failure Case (Resonance)

WHY this is true

If the guessed \(y_{PI}\)’s functional form already appears in \(y_{CF}\), substituting it produces \(0=R(x)\) — impossible, because that guess is already a solution of the homogeneous equation, so plugging it into the left side automatically gives zero no matter what coefficient you choose. The fix, exactly parallel to the repeated-root case in Section 14.1, is to multiply the guess by \(x\) (or by \(x^2\) if it collides with a repeated root) — this produces a function no longer in the homogeneous solution family, breaking the automatic-zero problem while still respecting the “closed under differentiation” idea from the WHY box above.

Worked Example

\(y''-5y'+6y=e^{2x}\) (roots \(m=2,3\), so \(e^{2x}\in y_{CF}\) already).

Naive guess \(Ae^{2x}\) fails: substituting gives \((4A-10A+6A)e^{2x}=0\neq e^{2x}\).

Try instead \(y_{PI}=Axe^{2x}\): \(y_{PI}'=Ae^{2x}+2Axe^{2x}\), \(y_{PI}''=4Ae^{2x}+4Axe^{2x}\). Substitute into \(y''-5y'+6y\):

\(xe^{2x}\) terms: \((4A-10A+6A)=0\) — cancel as expected (confirms compatibility).

Plain \(e^{2x}\) terms: \((4A-5A)e^{2x}=-Ae^{2x}\). Set \(-A=1\Rightarrow A=-1\).

\[ y_{PI}=-x\,e^{2x},\qquad y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{3x}-xe^{2x}. \]

If it had collided with a repeated root (say \(m=2\) double, so even \(xe^{2x}\in y_{CF}\)), you would need \(y_{PI}=Ax^2e^{2x}\) instead.

14.4 Putting It Together: Initial Conditions

The general solution is always \(y=y_{CF}+y_{PI}\). Apply given initial/boundary conditions after combining both parts.

Worked Example

Solve \(y''-5y'+6y=e^{4x}\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\).

From before: \(y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{3x}+\tfrac12e^{4x}\), so \(y'=2C_1e^{2x}+3C_2e^{3x}+2e^{4x}\).

At \(x=0\): \(C_1+C_2+\tfrac12=0\) and \(2C_1+3C_2+2=0\).

From the first: \(C_1=-\tfrac12-C_2\). Substitute into the second: \(2(-\tfrac12-C_2)+3C_2+2=0\Rightarrow C_2=-1\), so \(C_1=\tfrac12\).

21 Method of Variation of Parameters

↗ original PDF, page 36
Definition

For \(y''+Py'+Qy=R(x)\) with complementary-function solutions \(y_1,y_2\) and Wronskian \(W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2\):

\[ y_p=-y_1\int\frac{y_2R}{W}\,dx+y_2\int\frac{y_1R}{W}\,dx. \]
WHY this is true

Where the formula comes from. The CF is \(c_1y_1+c_2y_2\) with constant \(c_i\). The idea: let the constants vary — seek \(y_p=u_1(x)y_1+u_2(x)y_2\). Differentiate:

\[ y_p'=u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2+u_1y_1'+u_2y_2'. \]

We have two unknown functions \(u_1,u_2\) but only one equation to satisfy (the ODE), so we are free to impose one extra condition. Choose the one that keeps things simple:

\[ u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0.\qquad(*) \]

Then \(y_p'=u_1y_1'+u_2y_2'\) and \(y_p''=u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'+u_1y_1''+u_2y_2''\). Substitute into the ODE and group by \(u_1\) and \(u_2\):

\[ u_1\underbrace{(y_1''+Py_1'+Qy_1)}_{=0}+u_2\underbrace{(y_2''+Py_2'+Qy_2)}_{=0}+u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=R. \]

The brackets vanish because \(y_1,y_2\) solve the homogeneous equation. So we are left with a clean pair of equations in \(u_1',u_2'\):

\[ u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0,\qquad u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=R. \]

Solving by Cramer’s rule, with \(W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2\neq0\) (independent solutions), gives \(u_1'=-\dfrac{y_2R}{W}\) and \(u_2'=\dfrac{y_1R}{W}\). Integrating gives the formula. The choice \((*)\) is what made the second derivatives of \(u_i\) disappear.

↗ original PDF, page 37
Fixing a Common Misconception

“My particular integral contains a term that is already in the CF, so it must be wrong.” A particular integral is any one solution of the full equation — it is not unique. A CF term inside it just gets absorbed into the arbitrary constants. (See the second example: \(-xe^x\) appears and is harmless.)

Worked Example

(1) \(y''+y=\sec x\). \(y_1=\cos x,\ y_2=\sin x,\ W=\cos^2x+\sin^2x=1\). Since \(\sin x\sec x=\tan x\) and \(\cos x\sec x=1\):

\[ y_p=-\cos x\int\tan x\,dx+\sin x\int dx=\cos x\ln|\cos x|+x\sin x. \]

General solution: \(y=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x+\cos x\ln|\cos x|+x\sin x\).

(2) \(y''-2y'+y=\dfrac{e^x}{x}\). \(y_1=e^x,\ y_2=xe^x,\ W=e^{2x}\).

\[ y_p=-e^x\int\frac{xe^x\cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}}\,dx+xe^x\int\frac{e^x\cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}}\,dx=-e^x\int1\,dx+xe^x\int\frac1x\,dx=-xe^x+xe^x\ln x. \]

The term \(-xe^x\) merges into \(C_2xe^x\), so \(y=(C_1+C_2x)e^x+xe^x\ln x\).

Connection — where this reappears

In Part I (Section 14) undetermined coefficients rested on the fact that derivatives of \(e^{kx}\), polynomials and \(\sin/\cos\) stay inside a finite family. For \(\tan x\) or \(\sec x\) they do not — derivatives keep producing new functions — and that is exactly the situation this method rescues.

22 The Cauchy–Euler Equation

Definition
\[ x^2y''+a\,xy'+b\,y=R(x)\qquad(x>0). \]

Substitute \(x=e^t\) (so \(t=\ln x\)) and write \(D=\dfrac{d}{dt}\). Then \(xy'=Dy\) and \(x^2y''=D(D-1)y\), turning the equation into one with constant coefficients.

WHY this is true

Why \(x=e^t\) works. With \(t=\ln x\), the chain rule gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac1x\dfrac{dy}{dt}\), so \(x\,y'=\dfrac{dy}{dt}\). Differentiating \(y'=\frac1xy_t\) once more:

\[ y''=-\frac{1}{x^2}y_t+\frac{1}{x^2}y_{tt}\implies x^2y''=y_{tt}-y_t=D(D-1)y. \]

Every term \(x^ky^{(k)}\) becomes a constant-coefficient expression in \(D\) because the equation is equidimensional: replacing \(x\) by \(cx\) scales all terms equally. Multiplication in \(x\) is addition in \(t=\ln x\), so “scale-invariant in \(x\)” means “translation-invariant in \(t\)” — and translation-invariant equations are exactly the constant-coefficient ones.

Shortcut

Shortcut. Trying \(y=x^m\) directly gives \(x^2y''=m(m-1)x^m\), \(xy'=mx^m\), so the homogeneous equation reduces to the auxiliary equation

\[ m(m-1)+a\,m+b=0. \]
Roots of \(m(m-1)+am+b=0\)Solution (for \(x>0\))
real, distinct \(m_1\neq m_2\)\(C_1x^{m_1}+C_2x^{m_2}\)

Fixing a Common Misconception

“Try \(y=e^{mx}\), as for constant-coefficient equations.” That trial works only because \(e^{mx}\) keeps its shape under differentiation, leaving a common factor to cancel. With variable coefficients \(x^2,\ x\) there is no common factor (\(x^2m^2e^{mx}+xme^{mx}+\cdots\)), so nothing cancels. The natural trial is the function that is scale-invariant: \(x^m\).

Worked Example

(1) \(x^2y''-2xy'-4y=0\). \(m(m-1)-2m-4=m^2-3m-4=(m-4)(m+1)=0\), so \(y=C_1x^4+C_2x^{-1}\).

(2) \(x^2y''+3xy'+y=0\). \(m^2+2m+1=0,\ m=-1\) (repeated): \(y=(C_1+C_2\ln x)\dfrac1x\).

(3) \(x^2y''+xy'+y=0\). \(m^2+1=0,\ m=\pm i\): \(y=C_1\cos(\ln x)+C_2\sin(\ln x)\).

(4) Non-homogeneous with resonance: \(x^2y''-3xy'+4y=x^2\). With \(x=e^t\): \(\left[D(D-1)-3D+4\right]y=(D-2)^2y=e^{2t}\). CF \(=(C_1+C_2t)e^{2t}\). Since \(e^{2t}\) collides with a repeated root, the resonance rule of Part I (multiply by \(t^2\)) gives \(y_p=\frac12t^2e^{2t}\). Returning to \(x\):

\[ y=x^2(C_1+C_2\ln x)+\tfrac12x^2(\ln x)^2. \]
Connection — where this reappears

Example (4) reuses the failure-case rule of Part I (Section 14) verbatim — but in the variable \(t\). Once \(x=e^t\) is made, everything you learned about constant-coefficient equations applies unchanged.

23 The Cauchy–Legendre Equation

↗ original PDF, page 39
Definition
\[ (ax+b)^2y''+A(ax+b)y'+By=R(x). \]

Substitute \(ax+b=e^t\), \(D=\dfrac{d}{dt}\): then \((ax+b)y'=a\,Dy\) and \((ax+b)^2y''=a^2D(D-1)y\). Cauchy–Euler is the special case \(a=1,\ b=0\).

WHY this is true

Let \(\xi=ax+b=e^t\). Then \(\dfrac{dt}{dx}=\dfrac a\xi\), so \(y'=\dfrac a\xi y_t\) and \(\xi y'=a\,y_t\). Differentiating \(y'=\frac a\xi y_t\) again, using \(\frac{d\xi}{dx}=a\):

\[ y''=-\frac{a^2}{\xi^2}y_t+\frac{a^2}{\xi^2}y_{tt}\implies\xi^2y''=a^2(y_{tt}-y_t)=a^2D(D-1)y. \]

Geometrically, the equation is a Cauchy–Euler equation in the shifted and scaled variable \(\xi=ax+b\); the “special point” where the coefficient of \(y''\) vanishes has moved from \(x=0\) to \(x=-b/a\).

Worked Example

(1) \((3x+2)^2y''+3(3x+2)y'-36y=0\). Put \(3x+2=e^t\): \(9D(D-1)+3\cdot3D-36=9D^2-36=9(D^2-4)\), so \(D=\pm2\) and

\[ y=C_1(3x+2)^2+C_2(3x+2)^{-2}. \]

(2) \((2x+3)^2y''-2(2x+3)y'-12y=6x\). Put \(2x+3=e^t\) (so \(x=\frac{e^t-3}{2}\) and \(6x=3e^t-9\)): \(4D(D-1)-2\cdot2D-12=4(D^2-2D-3)\), giving

\[ (D^2-2D-3)y=\tfrac14(3e^t-9). \]

CF: roots \(3,-1\), so \(C_1e^{3t}+C_2e^{-t}\). PI: for \(e^t\), \(\dfrac{3/4}{1-2-3}e^t=-\frac{3}{16}e^t\); for the constant, \(\dfrac{-9/4}{-3}=\frac34\). Back in \(x\):

\[ y=C_1(2x+3)^3+C_2(2x+3)^{-1}-\tfrac38x+\tfrac{3}{16}. \]

Check the particular part directly: \(y_p=-\frac38x+\frac3{16}\) has \(y_p''=0\), \(y_p'=-\frac38\), so \(-2(2x+3)(-\frac38)-12(-\frac38x+\frac{3}{16})=\frac34(2x+3)+\frac92x-\frac94=6x\). ✓

24 Applications of Second-Order Linear ODEs

↗ original PDF, page 40

Spring–mass–damper

WHY this is true

Newton’s second law, \(m\,x''=\)(total force). The forces are the spring \(-kx\) (Hooke’s law, restoring), the damper \(-c\,x'\) (opposing velocity) and an external force \(F(t)\). So

\[ m\,x''+c\,x'+k\,x=F(t). \]

This is exactly a constant-coefficient second-order ODE, and the auxiliary equation \(mr^2+cr+k=0\) has discriminant \(c^2-4mk\), whose sign decides the behaviour.

mass \(m\)inductance \(L\)inertia
damping \(c\)resistance \(R\)dissipation
stiffness \(k\)\(1/C\) (elastance)restoring
displacement \(x\)charge \(q\)unknown
force \(F(t)\)EMF \(E(t)\)driving term

Kirchhoff’s voltage law (voltages around the loop sum to the EMF) gives \(Lq''+Rq'+\dfrac qC=E(t)\) — the same equation. Every conclusion about springs transfers to circuits, and vice versa.

↗ original PDF, page 41
Fixing a Common Misconception

“Complex roots mean the system oscillates forever.” Only if there is no damping. Roots \(-\alpha\pm i\beta\) with \(\alpha>0\) give \(e^{-\alpha t}(\cdots)\): oscillation with a decaying amplitude. Persistent oscillation needs a purely imaginary pair \(\pm i\beta\), i.e. \(c=0\) (or \(R=0\)).

Worked Example

(1) Free vibration. A 0.5 kg mass on a spring with \(k=8\) N/m is pulled 0.3 m and released from rest; no damping. \(0.5x''+8x=0\Rightarrow x''+16x=0\), \(\omega=4\), so \(x=0.3\cos4t\): amplitude 0.3 m, period \(2\pi/4=\pi/2\) s.

(2) Critical damping. \(x''+6x'+9x=0\), \(x(0)=1\), \(x'(0)=0\). Repeated root \(-3\): \(x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-3t}\), \(C_1=1\), \(x'(0)=C_2-3=0\), so \(x=(1+3t)e^{-3t}\).

(3) Circuit. \(L=1\) H, \(R=4\ \Omega\), \(C=\frac15\) F, constant EMF \(E=10\) V, \(q(0)=0\), \(i(0)=q'(0)=0\). Then \(q''+4q'+5q=10\) with roots \(-2\pm i\) and \(q_p=2\), so \(q=e^{-2t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)+2\). \(q(0)=0\Rightarrow A=-2\); \(q'(0)=-2A+B=0\Rightarrow B=-4\):

\[ q=2-e^{-2t}(2\cos t+4\sin t). \]

The charge settles at 2 C — the transient dies away and only the particular solution survives.

Resonance

WHY this is true

Drive an undamped system at its own natural frequency: \(x''+4x=\cos2t\) (natural frequency \(\omega_0=2\)). The right-hand side matches the CF (\(\cos2t,\sin2t\)), so the failure case of Part I applies and \(x_p=\dfrac t4\sin2t\) (check: \(x_p''=\cos2t-t\sin2t\), so \(x_p''+4x_p=\cos2t\) ✓). The amplitude of \(x_p\) grows linearly with \(t\): each push arrives exactly in phase with the motion, so energy is added every cycle and nothing removes it. With damping the growth is capped, which is why real structures survive — but badly tuned ones (the classic bridge-collapse stories) do not.

Connection — where this reappears

The failure case of Part I, Section 14 was introduced as an algebraic nuisance; here it is revealed as a physical phenomenon. The “multiply by \(x\)” rule is the mathematical signature of resonance.
