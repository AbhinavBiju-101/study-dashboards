1Hydroelectric Power

DEFINITION Hydroelectric power converts the gravitational potential energy of stored water into electricity: water falls, spins a turbine, the turbine's shaft spins a generator. Globally it supplies over 18% of all electricity — the fourth-largest energy source worldwide.

Two methods to harvest hydropower

MethodHow it works
Dam-basedReservoir accumulates water; opening underwater gates releases it through the penstock to spin turbines; output is throttled by how far the gates are opened
Pumped-storageTwo reservoirs at different elevations. Off-peak surplus electricity pumps water from the lower to the upper reservoir; during peak demand, that water is released back down to generate electricity on demand
WHY PUMPED-STORAGE IS EFFECTIVELY A BATTERY A conventional dam only converts energy once — stored water height into electricity. A pumped-storage plant converts energy twice: off-peak electricity (cheap, often surplus) into stored gravitational potential energy, then back into on-demand electricity during peak hours. It doesn't generate net new energy — it shifts when electricity is available, which is precisely the function a battery serves in a power grid.
AdvantagesDisadvantages
High efficiency (>80%, vs. ~40% for thermal plants); low running cost; output is controllable on demand; 40–50 year operational lifespan; near-zero emissions once builtVery high construction cost; floods large areas, displacing people (often tribal communities); disrupts river ecosystems and fish migration; alters downstream flow, sometimes across national borders

2Wind Energy

DEFINITION Wind energy is indirect solar energy: the sun heats Earth's surface unevenly, creating differences in temperature, density, and pressure that drive air movement. A wind turbine converts that moving air's kinetic energy into electricity via a spinning shaft and generator — mechanically the same last step as hydropower, just a different upstream energy source.
TypeAxis of rotationNote
Horizontal-axisHorizontalMost widely used; more efficient than vertical-axis
Vertical-axisVerticalLess common, less efficient, but works regardless of wind direction

India is the world's fifth-largest producer of wind power, led by Tamil Nadu and Gujarat.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Renewable, inexhaustible, low-cost once built; zero greenhouse gas emissions during operationUnreliable — wind speed/availability is inconsistent; noise pollution; location-specific (windiest sites are often remote); visual impact; bird/bat mortality from collisions

3Ocean Thermal Energy (OTEC)

DEFINITION Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC) exploits the temperature difference between sun-warmed surface seawater and cold deep seawater to generate electricity. It requires a temperature gradient of at least 20 °C, which is why it only works well in the tropics (where the gradient reaches ~20–25 °C).
WHY EFFICIENCY IS SO LOW (1–3%) YET IT'S STILL WORTH BUILDING OTEC's efficiency is fundamentally limited by thermodynamics: the smaller the temperature difference driving a heat engine, the lower its maximum possible efficiency — and a 20 °C gap is tiny compared to the hundreds of degrees driving a coal plant's steam cycle. But OTEC's fuel (the temperature gradient itself) is free and continuously renewed by the sun, so low efficiency matters far less than it would for a fuel-consuming plant — the meaningful comparison is availability and running cost, not raw thermodynamic efficiency.
MethodMechanismBonus output
Closed-cycleWarm water vaporizes a separate low-boiling working fluid (ammonia/propane/R-134a); cold water condenses it back to liquid for reuseNone extra — fluid is fully recycled
Open-cycleWarm seawater itself is flashed to steam in a low-pressure chamber (lowering pressure drops the boiling point); that steam drives the turbine directlyDesalinated water — the steam condenses back to pure, impurity-free water

Advantages: reliable regardless of weather (unlike wind/solar, since the temperature gradient exists as long as the sun shines); no pollution; open-cycle plants also produce freshwater as a byproduct. Disadvantages: very expensive to install; low efficiency; location-specific (needs coastline + deep cold water nearby); pumping large water volumes disrupts marine life.

4Geothermal Energy

DEFINITION Geothermal energy taps heat stored deep inside the Earth — not a form of solar energy at all, but heat generated by ongoing nuclear fusion-like reactions in the Earth's core. Temperature increases roughly 1 °C per 35–40 m of depth.
COMMON MISCONCEPTION Because wind and OTEC are both "indirect solar energy," it's tempting to assume all renewables trace back to the sun. Geothermal is the exception in this set — its energy source is the Earth's own internal heat, which is why it is available 24/7/365 regardless of weather or sunlight, unlike every other renewable covered this week.

Where geothermal energy is harvested

Magma rising from the mantle creates localized "hot spots." When magma meets underground water, steam forms — and where that steam finds a natural surface opening, you get a hot spring or geyser. Geothermal plants are therefore built near known hot springs, geysers, or volcanic activity, using a hydrothermal convection system: wells drilled 1–2 miles deep bring the heat to a turbine at the surface.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Available 24/7/365 for an estimated 2–3 billion years; renewable; non-polluting; no by-productsHigh drilling/installation cost; strictly location-specific (needs geologically active sites); drilling can trigger earthquakes; can release toxic gases (SO₂, H₂S)

5Comparing the Five Renewables

All five ultimately do the same last step — spin a shaft, drive a generator. What differs is the upstream energy source and its reliability.
SourceUpstream energy originReliabilityLocation constraint
HydroelectricSolar-driven water cycle (rain → rivers) + gravityHigh, controllable on demandNeeds a large river/suitable dam site
WindIndirect solar (uneven surface heating)Low — intermittentNeeds consistently windy sites, often remote
OTECDirect solar heating of ocean surfaceHigh — gradient persists as long as sun shinesNeeds tropical coastline with deep cold water nearby
GeothermalNot solar — Earth's internal heatVery high — 24/7 regardless of weatherNeeds geologically active zones (hot springs, volcanoes)
WHY RELIABILITY VARIES SO MUCH ACROSS "RENEWABLE" SOURCES "Renewable" only describes whether the source replenishes naturally — it says nothing about whether that source is steady. Wind is renewable but wildly variable minute-to-minute; geothermal is renewable and essentially constant. This is exactly why grid planners pair intermittent sources (wind) with controllable/steady ones (hydro's pumped-storage, geothermal) rather than relying on one renewable alone.

6Thermal Pollution

DEFINITION Thermal pollution is a sudden rise or fall in a water body's temperature caused by human activity, disrupting the aquatic ecosystem — heat contamination, not chemical contamination. A power plant might return cooling water at 40–50 °C into a river that naturally sits at 20–25 °C.
COMMON MISCONCEPTION "A degree or two of warming can't matter much — it's not like dumping a toxic chemical." In fact even a 1–1.5 °C rise can measurably reduce oxygen solubility in water and stress aquatic organisms, because warmer water simply holds less dissolved gas — this is a direct physical/chemical property of water, not a biological tolerance issue that organisms could adapt around quickly.

Causes

  • Industrial discharges (steel mills, refineries, paper/chemical plants) using water for cooling
  • Power plants (nuclear and coal-fired) — the largest single contributor
  • Hydroelectric projects — water released from the bottom of a reservoir is cooler than surface releases, creating temperature swings downstream
  • Deforestation/soil erosion — removes shade, increases turbidity (which absorbs more heat)
  • Urban sewage discharge — typically warmer than the receiving water

Consequences — a causal chain, not a list of unrelated symptoms

Remedial measures

MeasureHow it dissipates heat
Cooling pondsShallow reservoirs — evaporation, convection, radiation before discharge
Cooling towers (wet)Hot water sprayed downward, cooled by rising air + evaporation
Cooling towers (dry)Cools air without any water loss
Spray pondsNozzles break hot water into droplets to speed evaporative cooling
Closed-loop recyclingReuses warm water internally instead of discharging it

This same mechanism connects directly back to Week 1's oxygen-solubility chemistry and Week 2's eutrophication chain — thermal pollution is really "eutrophication's accelerant," not a separate phenomenon.

7Water Management & Conservation

DEFINITION Only 2.5–3% of Earth's water is fresh, and less than 1% is easily accessible for human use. Water management is planning/distributing that scarce resource; conservation is using it efficiently to reduce waste. Together they're the practical response to a hard physical constraint, not a lifestyle preference.

Principles of water management

PrincipleMeaning
SustainabilityToday's use must not compromise tomorrow's supply
EquityFair distribution across agriculture, industry, and domestic users
EfficiencyMinimising losses in storage, transmission, and end use
IWRMIntegrated Water Resources Management — coordinating water, land and related resources together rather than in silos

Conservation methods, with their real-world savings

MethodTypical savings
Drip / micro-irrigation28–35% (up to 80% in Israeli smart-drip systems, which also boosted yield 100%)
Rainwater harvesting10–30%+ reduction in household water use
Wastewater reuse & recyclingCan displace ~82% of potable-water demand for non-drinking uses
Industrial water recycling~26% global efficiency improvement (2006–2015)
WHY DRIP IRRIGATION SAVES SO MUCH MORE THAN IT "SHOULD" Flood/surface irrigation loses water to two places a plant never benefits from: evaporation off exposed wet soil, and runoff past the root zone. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root, cutting both loss pathways simultaneously — which is why the savings (28–80%) are so much larger than a naive "more careful watering" would suggest; it's not incremental efficiency, it's eliminating two structural waste routes at once.

India's key water policies

  • National Water Policy (1987, 2002, 2012) — IWRM, rainwater harvesting, groundwater regulation
  • Jal Shakti Abhiyan (2019) — nationwide rainwater harvesting & water-body renovation campaign
  • PMKSY ("Per Drop More Crop") — promotes drip/sprinkler irrigation
  • Atal Bhujal Yojana — community-driven groundwater management

State examples: Rajasthan (drip irrigation → ~50% less water for vegetables in arid land), Kerala (System of Rice Intensification saves ~30% water in paddy), Maharashtra (farm ponds storing rainwater for rabi crops).

8Electric & CNG Vehicles

DEFINITION An Electric Vehicle (EV) replaces the internal combustion engine with an electric motor powered by a battery. Three core components: battery → controller → electric motor. When the pedal is pressed, the controller draws exactly the right amount of energy from the battery and delivers it to the motor, which converts electrical energy into the mechanical energy that turns the wheels.

Four types of electric vehicle — the real distinction is "where does the energy come from?"

TypeEnergy sourceCan plug into the grid?
BEV (Battery EV / All-Electric)Onboard battery onlyYes — sole charging method
PHEV (Plug-in Hybrid)Battery + combustion engine (backup)Yes, plus can run on fuel for long trips
HEV (Hybrid)Combustion engine + regenerative braking onlyNo — cannot be plugged in at all
FCEV (Fuel-Cell)Hydrogen + oxygen reacted to generate electricity on the flyNo — refuels with hydrogen, not electricity
COMMON MISCONCEPTION "A hybrid (HEV) is basically a smaller plug-in hybrid." They are fundamentally different in one crucial way: an HEV cannot be recharged from the power grid at all — all its electrical energy originates from gasoline combustion and regenerative braking. A PHEV genuinely can plug in. Confusing the two misses the entire point of what "plug-in" means in PHEV's name.

Why EVs are more energy-efficient, not just cleaner

MECHANISM EV batteries convert roughly 59–62% of stored energy into actual vehicle movement; a gasoline engine converts only 17–21% — the rest is lost as waste heat through the fundamental inefficiency of internal combustion. This efficiency gap exists independent of emissions entirely: even ignoring tailpipe pollution, an EV simply wastes far less energy turning stored fuel into motion. Regenerative braking adds a further efficiency layer unique to EVs — braking energy that would be lost as friction heat in a combustion car is instead captured back into the battery.
EV advantagesEV disadvantages
No tailpipe emissions; ~99% fewer moving parts than combustion engines → lower maintenance; near-silent operation; lower centre of gravity improves crash stabilityHigh upfront cost; shorter range (~100–200 km typical, up to ~300 miles premium); charging takes 30 min–12+ hrs depending on charger speed; battery replacement needed every 3–10 years; risk of thermal runaway (fire/explosion) in damaged battery packs

CNG (Compressed Natural Gas)

DEFINITION CNG is natural gas (mostly methane) compressed to under 1% of its normal volume (3000–3600 psi), making it dense enough to store onboard a vehicle. It is not the same as LPG (which is propane/butane, stored as a liquid).
CNG advantagesCNG disadvantages
~1/3 to 1/5 the cost of petrol; burns cleaner — up to 95% less CO, CO₂ and NOₓ than gasoline; no additive residue, so engine oil and spark plugs stay cleaner for longer, reducing maintenanceLimited refuelling station availability in many regions; heavy, bulky storage tank affects vehicle handling and braking distance; methane itself is a potent greenhouse gas if it leaks

Self-Test Checklist

  • Explain why a pumped-storage plant is functionally a "battery" for the electrical grid, not a net energy generator.
  • Trace the common final mechanical step shared by hydroelectric, wind, and geothermal power generation.
  • Explain why OTEC's low efficiency (1–3%) matters less than it would for a fuel-burning plant.
  • Identify the one renewable source in this week's set that is not ultimately solar-powered, and explain why that makes it uniquely reliable.
  • Trace the full causal chain of thermal pollution from temperature rise to invasive-species takeover.
  • Explain why drip irrigation saves so much more water than "just being more careful" would predict.
  • Distinguish an HEV from a PHEV using the single defining test: can it be plugged into the grid?
  • State the efficiency gap between EV and combustion-engine energy conversion, and explain where the lost energy in a combustion engine actually goes.
