1Environmental Science, Studies & Engineering

COMMON MISCONCEPTION "Environmental science, environmental studies, and environmental engineering are basically the same subject with different names." They aren't — they differ in what question each one is trying to answer.
Three related but distinct fields
FieldCore question it asksCharacter
Environmental ScienceHow does the Earth system actually work, and how are we affecting it?A natural science — ecology, geology, chemistry, physics, biology all feeding into one picture.
Environmental StudiesHow do people and the environment interact, and what should we do about it?Broader — pulls in ethics, economics, law and policy alongside the science.
Environmental EngineeringHow do we design a fix — a treatment plant, a scrubber, a landfill liner?Applied — takes the science and studies as inputs and builds the physical solution.
WHY THIS DISTINCTION MATTERS A single real problem — say, a river turning toxic from tannery waste — needs all three. Science tells you which chromium compound is doing the damage and how it moves through the water column. Studies asks why tanneries cluster there, who is affected, and what policy (like the Water Act) applies. Engineering designs the effluent treatment plant that actually stops it. Miss one leg, and the "solution" is incomplete — a great treatment plant with no policy behind it just gets ignored; a strong policy with no engineering behind it has nothing to enforce.

2Scope & the Multidisciplinary Approach

Environmental problems are never confined to one department of knowledge. A single issue — water scarcity, say — is simultaneously a hydrology problem, a law-and-policy problem, an engineering problem, and a sociology problem. That is precisely why environmental studies exists as a field: to force these perspectives into the same room.

Why environmental studies matters

  • Protection: guides conservation of ecosystems, biodiversity, and natural resources.
  • Public health: connects pollution and degradation directly to human disease burden.
  • Sustainable development: balances economic growth against long-term ecological capacity.
  • Informed decisions: gives policymakers the evidence to choose responsibly, not just cheaply.

3Earth as a Life-Support System

Earth supports life because four interacting "spheres" each supply something no organism can live without: air, water, food, and solid ground. The word sphere literally means "to surround or encompass" — each one is a shell wrapped around the planet.

DEFINITION Lithosphere (rock) → land and minerals · Hydrosphere (water) → drinking water and climate moderation · Atmosphere (vapour) → breathable air and weather · Biosphere (life) → all living organisms, which depend on the other three to survive.

3.1 The Atmosphere — five layers, and why each behaves differently

MECHANISM Air temperature with altitude is not one smooth gradient — it zig-zags, because different layers are heated by different sources. The troposphere is warmed from below by the sun-heated ground, so it cools as you go up. The stratosphere is warmed from within, because its ozone directly absorbs UV radiation — so it gets warmer as you go up. That reversal is exactly why the tropopause acts like a lid: rising warm tropospheric air hits stratospheric air that is already warmer above it, so it stops rising. This single mechanism (differential heating) explains the whole zig-zag profile in the diagram below.
Atmosphere layers at a glance
LayerAltitudeDefining feature
Troposphere0 – ~15 kmWhere we live; holds nearly all weather; warmest at the surface
Stratosphere~15 – 50 kmContains the ozone layer; stable, dry air used by high-altitude aircraft
Mesosphere~50 – 85 kmColdest layer (down to −90 °C); meteors burn up here
Thermosphere~85 – 600 kmVery hot (500–2000 °C); auroras occur; satellites orbit here
Exosphere>600 km, no sharp topAir leaks gradually into space; no clear upper boundary

Composition: nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21%, remaining ~1% is water vapour, CO₂, methane and trace gases — small in quantity but outsized in effect on climate regulation.

3.2 The Hydrosphere & the water cycle

The hydrosphere is all the water on, under, and above Earth's surface: oceans (~97%), lakes/rivers (~2%), and — critically — only about 1% is fresh, accessible water for human use. That last figure is the one worth remembering: it's the entire reason "water management and conservation" (Week 3) is a serious engineering problem rather than a non-issue.

3.3 The Geosphere

The solid Earth, from inner core to crust. It has an interior structure that directly drives surface geology:

  • Inner core — solid iron-nickel.
  • Outer core — liquid iron-nickel; its motion generates Earth's magnetic field.
  • Mantle — semi-solid rock; convection currents here are the engine behind plate tectonics.
  • Crust — the thin outer shell we stand on, split into continental and oceanic plates.
WHY Mountains, earthquakes, and volcanoes are not separate phenomena — they are all surface symptoms of one process: mantle convection dragging crustal plates around. This is the same mechanism you'll meet again in Week 2 as the root cause of earthquakes.

3.4 The Biosphere

The sum of every ecosystem where life exists — forests, deserts, oceans, wetlands, grasslands — plus the living organisms in them: producers (plants), consumers (animals), and microorganisms (decomposers). This is the sphere the rest of this document lives in, so we now zoom into it directly.

4Ecosystems: Structure, Types, Components

DEFINITION An ecosystem is a dynamic complex of living organisms (biotic) and their non-living surroundings (abiotic), interacting as a system that exchanges energy and cycles matter. It is the smallest unit that includes both the community of species and its physical environment — a forest's trees alone aren't an ecosystem; the trees plus soil, water, air and sunlight together are.

Natural vs. artificial ecosystems

A forest or river is self-sustaining and evolves without human input. A garden or aquarium is artificial — it needs continuous human management (watering, feeding, filtering) because the natural regulatory loops aren't complete.

Biotic and abiotic components

Biotic (living)Abiotic (non-living)
Producers, consumers, decomposers, microorganismsLight, temperature, water, soil, air, minerals, pH, salinity

These two sets are interdependent: plants need sunlight, water and minerals to grow and, in turn, produce the oxygen that makes the abiotic atmosphere breathable for animals. Disruption of either side ripples through the whole system.

5Levels of Ecological Organization

Ecology studies life at a nested set of scales. Each level is built entirely out of the level below it — nothing new is added except interaction.

LevelWhat it is
OrganismA single individual living being
PopulationOrganisms of the same species in one area, interacting through breeding and competition
CommunityMultiple populations (different species) sharing an area and interacting
EcosystemA community plus its non-living environment
BiosphereThe sum of every ecosystem on Earth

6Food Chains, Food Webs & Ecological Pyramids

DEFINITION A food chain is a single linear path of energy transfer: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle. A food web is the full network of overlapping, interconnected food chains in a real ecosystem — because most organisms eat (and are eaten by) more than one thing.
COMMON MISCONCEPTION "A food chain is just a simplified food web, so the two are interchangeable teaching tools." Not quite — the simplification isn't just cosmetic. A food chain implies each organism has exactly one prey and one predator, which hides the redundancy that actually keeps ecosystems stable. In a real food web, if one prey species declines, a predator can often switch to another — that backup option is invisible in a food chain diagram, and it's precisely what gives ecosystems resilience (Section 10).

Ecological pyramids

Three ways to draw the same food chain, each revealing something different:

Pyramid typeMeasuresShape
NumbersCount of organisms per levelUsually upright, but can invert — one tree supports thousands of insects
BiomassTotal mass of living materialUsually decreases going up (energy loss reduces the mass that can be supported)
EnergyEnergy flow per levelAlways upright — energy loss at each transfer is a physical law, not a variable case
WHY THE ENERGY PYRAMID IS THE ONE THAT NEVER INVERTS Numbers and biomass pyramids can look "wrong" (one giant tree = one huge organism supporting a swarm of tiny insects above it, so numbers go up). But energy pyramids can't invert, because they're not counting objects — they're counting a one-way physical flow, and the 10% law (Section 7) guarantees each level receives strictly less than the one before it. That's the deepest reason the energy pyramid is considered the most accurate representation of ecosystem structure.

7Energy Flow in an Ecosystem

DEFINITION Energy flow is unidirectional: Sun → Producers → Primary consumers → Secondary consumers → Tertiary consumers. Unlike nutrients (Section 8), energy is never recycled — it dissipates as heat at every step and permanently leaves the ecosystem.

The 10% Law (Lindeman's Law)

When energy passes from one trophic level to the next, only about 10% is stored as new biomass; the remaining 90% is lost, mostly to respiration and incomplete digestion.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose producers in a grassland fix 10,000 kJ of energy via photosynthesis in a season. Apply the 10% law at each transfer:

  • Producers: 10,000 kJ
  • Primary consumers (herbivores): 10,000 × 0.10 = 1,000 kJ
  • Secondary consumers (carnivores): 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 kJ
  • Tertiary consumers (top predators): 100 × 0.10 = 10 kJ

Check: that's a 1000-fold drop from producer to top predator — which is exactly why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 links. By the fifth level there is so little energy left that supporting a sixth level usually isn't viable. This is a direct, checkable consequence of the 10% law, not a separate rule.

Gross vs. Net Primary Production

GPP = NPP + R

GPP (Gross Primary Production) is the total energy plants capture via photosynthesis. Plants then spend some of that on their own respiration (R) just to stay alive. What's left over — NPP (Net Primary Production) — is the only energy actually available to herbivores.

WORKED EXAMPLE A patch of grassland has GPP = 480 g C/m²/yr. Respiration consumes 180 g C/m²/yr.
NPP = GPP − R = 480 − 180 = 300 g C/m²/yr
Only those 300 g C/m²/yr are available to grasshoppers and other herbivores — the 180 g C/m²/yr the grass "spent on itself" never enters the food chain at all.

Two energy-flow models

ModelIdeaLimitation
Single-channelOne straight line: producers → herbivores → carnivoresToo simple; ignores dead organic matter (detritus)
Y-shaped (two-channel)Splits into a grazing food chain (living plant → herbivore) and a detritus food chain (dead matter → decomposer)More realistic — most biomass in a forest is never eaten alive; it falls as litter and is processed by decomposers instead

This detritus pathway matters more than it sounds: in forests, most standing biomass is not eaten by a herbivore at all — it dies, falls as litter, and is broken down by decomposers, which is exactly why forests are such large carbon and nutrient stores.

8Biogeochemical Cycles

WHY THIS IS THE COUNTERPART TO SECTION 7 Energy flows one way and leaves the system as heat — it can never be recycled. Matter is different: the same carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus atoms are used, released, and reused indefinitely. That's the single biggest contrast to hold onto: energy flows, nutrients cycle.

Carbon cycle

Nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen makes up 78% of the atmosphere, yet plants cannot use it in that gaseous (N₂) form directly — a key point that surprises many learners.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION "Since the air is 78% nitrogen, plants must have an abundant, easy supply of it." In fact atmospheric N₂ is an extremely stable triple-bonded molecule that plants cannot break apart themselves. Nitrogen only becomes usable after fixation — bacteria (or lightning) converting N₂ into ammonia (NH₃). This is why nitrogen is so often the limiting nutrient in agriculture despite being the most abundant gas around every leaf.
StepWhat happens
FixationN₂ → ammonia (NH₃), via bacteria or lightning
NitrificationAmmonia → nitrites (NO₂⁻) → nitrates (NO₃⁻), via bacteria
AssimilationPlants absorb nitrates and build amino acids/proteins
DecompositionDead organisms return nitrogen to soil as ammonia
DenitrificationBacteria convert nitrates back to N₂ gas, completing the loop

Phosphorus cycle

WHY PHOSPHORUS IS DIFFERENT FROM CARBON AND NITROGEN Carbon and nitrogen both have an atmospheric gas phase (CO₂, N₂) — they can move through the air. Phosphorus has no gas phase; it only moves by the weathering of rock into soil and water, uptake by plants, and eventual burial in sediment. Because there's no fast atmospheric shortcut, phosphorus moves far more slowly than carbon or nitrogen — which is exactly why it is so often the nutrient that limits growth in freshwater lakes, and why excess phosphorus from fertilizer/detergents (entering water directly, bypassing the slow rock-weathering step) causes such dramatic algal blooms.

Decomposition — the process that keeps every cycle turning

Fragmentation (detritivores like earthworms break up dead matter) → leaching (water dissolves out soluble nutrients) → catabolism (microbial enzymes break complex molecules into simple ones). Without this final decomposer step, none of the cycles above could close — nutrients would stay locked inside dead tissue forever.

9Ecological Succession

DEFINITION Ecological succession is the gradual, predictable replacement of one biological community by another over time, ending (usually) in a stable climax community.
WHY PRIMARY SUCCESSION IS SO MUCH SLOWER The rate-limiting step in primary succession is soil formation — lichens secrete mild acids that slowly weather rock into the first thin mineral soil. Secondary succession skips this step entirely, because a fire or flood destroys the vegetation but leaves the soil (and its nutrients, seed bank, and root systems) intact. That single difference in starting material is the entire reason for the 5–10× speed gap.

The four stages of any succession

  1. Nudation — a bare area forms (volcanic eruption, landslide, fire, flood, glacier retreat).
  2. Invasion — pioneer species arrive (lichens/mosses in primary; weeds/grasses in secondary).
  3. Competition — as numbers grow, species compete (intraspecific or interspecific) for shared space and nutrients, and the environment is modified until it favours the next wave of species.
  4. Climax — a stable community forms that persists until the next major disturbance.

Two theories of what "climax" even means

TheoryProposed byClaim
MonoclimaxF. E. ClementsWithin a region, all land converges on one climate-determined climax type
PolyclimaxA. TansleyClimax vegetation varies — controlled by many factors (soil, topography, fire), not climate alone

Named variants by starting environment: Hydrosere (starts in water), Xerosere (starts dry/waterless), Lithosere (starts on bare rock), Halosere (starts in saline conditions).

10Stability, Resilience & Human Impact

DEFINITION Stability = resisting change in the first place. Resilience = bouncing back after a disturbance. These are not the same property — an ecosystem can be low-stability but high-resilience (grassland: burns easily, recovers fast) or high-stability but low-resilience (coral reef: resists day-to-day change, but collapses hard and slowly recovers once bleached).
WHY BIODIVERSITY DRIVES BOTH Higher species diversity means more functional redundancy — recall the food-web argument from Section 6. If one pollinator disappears, another can often fill its role; that backup capacity is what resilience actually consists of mechanistically, not a vague "balance of nature."

Feedback loops

  • Negative feedback (stabilizing): more prey → more predators → predators reduce prey → population settles back down. This is what keeps most ecosystems in dynamic equilibrium.
  • Positive feedback (amplifying): overgrazing → vegetation loss → soil erosion → even less vegetation. Left unchecked, this spirals toward ecosystem collapse rather than a new equilibrium.

Human pressures, summarised

PressureMechanism of damage
DeforestationHabitat loss + disrupted water cycle + lost carbon sink + soil erosion
PollutionToxins enter food chains and accumulate at higher trophic levels
Climate changeShifts temperature/rainfall faster than species can migrate or adapt
UrbanizationFragments habitat, isolating populations below their viable breeding size
OverexploitationExtraction rate exceeds the system's regeneration rate
