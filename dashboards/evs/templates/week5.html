1Global climate change: greenhouse effect, impacts and causes

The greenhouse effect, derived rather than memorised

DEFINITION

The natural greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth’s surface caused by gases in the troposphere (water vapour, CO2, CH4,  N2O) that are nearly transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb outgoing infrared (heat) radiation and re-emit part of it back downwards. Global warming is the human-enhanced part of this warming; climate change is the long-term shift in temperature and weather patterns that results.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“A greenhouse gas layer works like the glass of a garden greenhouse.” Only loosely. Glass warms a greenhouse mainly by stopping warm air from leaving by convection. The atmosphere does something different: it intercepts infrared radiation. (Your solar cooker in Week 6 is the closer analogue: glass lets visible light in and traps the infrared it becomes.)
“The greenhouse effect is the ozone hole.” No. The ozone hole is stratospheric ozone loss (more UV reaches the ground); the greenhouse effect is about infrared absorption in the troposphere. They are separate problems with some overlapping chemicals.
“The greenhouse effect is bad.” The natural effect is what makes Earth habitable; the problem is the enhancement.

Why Earth would be about −18∘C without it

WHY THIS IS TRUE

In steady state, energy in = energy out. Sunlight arrives on a disc of area πR2 but the planet’s surface area is 4πR2, so the average incoming flux is S0/4. A fraction α (the albedo, about 0.3) is reflected. A surface at temperature T radiates σT4 (Stefan–Boltzmann, σ=5.67×10−8Wm−2K−4). Balance: S04(1−α)=σTe4⇒Te=[S0(1−α)4σ]1/4.

WORKED EXAMPLE

S0=1361W/m2, α=0.3. absorbed=1361×0.74=238W/m2(≈240)Te=(238.25.67×10−8)1/4=(4.20×109)1/4=254.6K≈−18.6∘C. The measured global mean is about 288K (+15∘C). The ∼33K gap is the natural greenhouse effect.

One absorbing layer shows how the extra warmth arises

Now add a single atmospheric layer that is transparent to sunlight but absorbs all infrared and re-emits it equally up and down. Let the sun supply 240 units to the surface (and nothing is absorbed in the layer from sunlight).

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Top of atmosphere: the layer is the only thing radiating to space, so it must emit 240 upward. It emits equally up and down, so it also emits 240 downward.
Surface: it receives 240 (sun) +240 (from the layer) =480, so it must emit 480.
Layer check: it absorbs the surface’s 480 and emits 240+240=480 ✓.
So σTs4=480=2×240⇒Ts=21/4Te=1.19×254.6≈303K.

Why sunlight passes in but heat is trapped: wavelength

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Hot bodies emit shorter wavelengths. Wien’s law gives the peak: λmax=2898μmK/T. Sun (5772 K): λmax≈0.50μm (visible). Earth (288 K): λmax≈10μm (infrared). CO2 absorbs strongly near 15μm and water vapour in several bands, i.e. inside the Earth’s emission range but outside the Sun’s. That mismatch is the entire mechanism.

Not all greenhouse gases are equal

DEFINITION

Global Warming Potential (GWP) compares the warming caused by 1 kg of a gas with 1 kg of CO2, integrated over a fixed horizon (usually 100 years). CO2 is defined as 1. Lifetime is how long a pulse of the gas stays in the air.

Gas Main human sources GWP100 (typical) Behaviour
Carbon dioxide fossil fuels, cement, deforestation 1 largest total contribution; very long-lived
Methane CH4 livestock digestion, rice, landfills, leaks ≈28 more powerful per kg, but lifetime only about a decade
Nitrous oxide N2O nitrogen fertiliser, combustion ≈265 accumulates over decades–centuries
Fluorinated gases refrigeration, equipment up to ∼23,000 (course) tiny quantities, extremely strong

The course states the “up to 23,000” figure for fluorinated gases; the ≈28 and ≈265 are typical IPCC-style values added here for the calculation below.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A leaking pipeline releases 2 tonnes of CH4. In CO2-equivalent: 2×28=56tCO2e. This is why capturing biogas methane (Week 6) is a climate win even before counting the energy.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Methane is the bigger problem because its GWP is bigger.” GWP is per kg. Total warming = GWP × amount emitted × how long it persists. CO2 dominates because humans emit it in enormous quantity and it lasts centuries; methane matters because it acts fast and is cuttable fast.

Natural versus human causes: attribution by elimination

WHY THIS IS TRUE

The course lists the natural drivers (reflectivity changes, orbit and tilt, solar output, volcanoes, natural CO2 swings). The logic of attribution is to test each one against two questions: does it act on the right timescale, and does it point in the right direction for warming since the 1950s?

Natural driver Timescale Why it cannot explain post-1950 warming
Orbit and axial tilt (Milankovitch) ∼20,000–100,000 yr far too slow; it paces ice ages
Solar output 11-yr cycle satellite record since 1978 shows no net rise while temperature rose
Volcanic aerosols (SO2) 1–few yr they cool, and settle out quickly
Natural CO2 changes thousands of yr acted as a feedback amplifying orbital forcing, not as the initial cause
Surface reflectivity (ice, forests, cities) decades real, but mostly a feedback to warming (see the feedbacks section)
CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course says CO2 is “52% higher than pre-industrial” (2020) and “414.8 ppm”. The percentage depends on the baseline you pick: 414.8/280≈1.48 (48%), while a ∼272 ppm baseline gives ≈52%. For MCQs use the course figure; for understanding, know that the rise is roughly half again above pre-industrial, to levels not seen for millions of years. Also: the same notes give >95% certainty (“extremely likely”) that humans are the dominant cause of post-1950 warming.

Human sources in the course list: burning coal, oil and gas (CO2, N2O); deforestation (lost sink and released stored carbon); manufacturing (cement, steel, plastics); livestock (methane); nitrogen fertiliser (N2O); fluorinated gases.

Feedbacks: why small pushes become big changes

DEFINITION

A feedback is a loop in which a change causes effects that change the original quantity again. A positive (reinforcing) feedback amplifies the initial change; a negative (balancing) feedback reduces it.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Positive feedback means good feedback.” In systems language, positive only means same direction: warming → more warming. Here every named feedback is positive, and that is exactly why they are worrying.

Ice–albedo feedback (worked out)

WORKED EXAMPLE

Take incoming sunlight =200W/m2 on a patch. Using the course figure, ice and snow reflect about 85% (α=0.85) while open ocean reflects about 7% (α=0.07). absorbed by ice=200×(1−0.85)=30W/m2absorbed by ocean=200×(1−0.07)=186W/m2ratio=186/30≈6.2 Replace ice by water and that patch absorbs about six times more energy. That heat melts more ice, exposing more water.

Permafrost and glacial-cycle CO2

Permafrost soils frozen for up to ∼40,000 years hold trapped carbon. Thawing lets microbes decompose it and release CO2 and CH4, which warm the planet and thaw more soil. Over glacial cycles, warm periods carried higher CO2 and cold periods lower CO2; this natural feedback amplified temperature swings that orbital changes started.

The eight impacts, each with a mechanism

More frequent and severe weather

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Warmer air can hold more water vapour (Clausius–Clapeyron). Using the Magnus approximation for saturation vapour pressure, es(T)=6.112exp(17.62T243.12+T) hPa with T in ∘C: es(15)=17.0 hPa and es(16)=18.1 hPa, so +1∘C⇒+6.6% capacity. More moisture in the atmosphere means heavier downpours when it rains, while more evaporation dries soils between rains, which explains both the floods and the droughts in the course notes.

Melting ice caps and permafrost

Covered in the feedbacks section above: both are reinforcing feedbacks as well as impacts.

Higher air pollution: ground-level ozone

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Ozone is always good.” Stratospheric ozone shields us from UV; ground-level (tropospheric) ozone is a pollutant and the main component of smog. It is not emitted directly: NO2 is split by sunlight, NO2+hν→NO+O, and then O+O2→O3, with volatile organic compounds sustaining the cycle. Heat and sunlight speed this up, so warmer weather means more smog, more asthma admissions, and more airborne pollen.

More acidic oceans

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Ocean water absorbs about a quarter of human CO2. Dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions: CO2+H2O⇌H2CO3⇌H++HCO3−. The extra H+ combines with carbonate ions, H++CO32−→HCO3−, leaving less carbonate for animals that build shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate (molluscs, crabs, corals).

WORKED EXAMPLE

pH is logarithmic: [H+]=10−pH. A drop from pH 8.2 to 8.1 multiplies [H+] by 100.1=1.26, i.e. a 26% increase from a change that looks tiny. Note the ocean is still alkaline (pH >7): “acidification” means moving toward acidic, not becoming acidic.

Rising sea levels

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Two contributions: (i) thermal expansion, since water expands when it warms; (ii) land-ice melt, which adds new water. For expansion alone, Δh=βHΔT.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Warm the top 700 m of ocean by 1∘C with β≈2.1×10−4K−1 (illustrative): Δh=2.1×10−4×700×1=0.147 m ≈15 cm, with no melting at all.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Melting Arctic sea ice raises sea level.” Floating ice already displaces its own weight of water (Archimedes), so melting it changes the level almost not at all (a tiny effect arises only because fresh meltwater is slightly less dense than seawater). Land ice (Greenland, Antarctica, glaciers) is the dangerous one, because that water was not in the ocean before.

Higher death rates and wildlife extinction

Heat waves, pollution, floods and vector-borne disease raise illness and death, hitting children, the elderly and low-income communities hardest. For wildlife, species move toward cooler latitudes or altitudes and change migration timing; the course cites a 2015 study in which vertebrates are disappearing about 114× faster than the natural background rate, linked with climate change, pollution and deforestation.

2Carbon sequestration

Carbon sequestration: putting carbon back

DEFINITION

Carbon sequestration is the long-term storage of CO2 (or other carbon forms) outside the atmosphere to slow global warming. A carbon sink absorbs more carbon than it releases; a source releases more than it absorbs. The course groups methods as biological, physical and chemical.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

The natural carbon cycle swaps carbon among the atmosphere, biosphere, soil (pedosphere), rocks (geosphere) and oceans (hydrosphere). Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from the slow, deep reservoir (rocks) into the fast one (air) far quicker than natural processes move it back. Sequestration is deliberately speeding up the return trip. Always ask two questions of any method: how much can it hold and how long does it stay put (permanence)?

Biological sequestration on land

One model explains peat, soil and cropland

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Let C be the carbon stored in a pool (a soil, a bog), I the yearly carbon input (plant litter, roots) and k the fractional decay rate per year (microbes turning it back into CO2). Then dCdt=I−kC⇒C(t)=Ik(1−e−kt),Ceq=Ik,τ=1k. Two levers raise the stored carbon: increase I (leave residues, add roots, perennials) or decrease k (waterlogging, cold, no-till, mineral protection). Peat bogs work by the second lever: waterlogged, oxygen-poor conditions make microbial decay very slow, so partly decayed biomass piles up instead of vanishing. The pool also needs roughly 3τ to reach 95% of Ceq, so soil sinks saturate.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Forest soil: I=2tC/ha/yr, k=0.05yr−1 ⇒Ceq=40 tC/ha, τ=20 yr (95% reached in ≈60 yr).
Same land as cropland where harvest removes 40% of the input: I=1.2 ⇒Ceq=24 tC/ha, i.e. 40% lower. This reproduces the course’s observation that cropland soils hold roughly 30–40% less organic carbon than natural vegetation (Fig. 8).
Peat bog: I=1, k=0.01⇒Ceq=100 tC/ha but τ=100 yr, so it fills slowly, which is why protecting existing peat beats creating new.

Forestry practices, defined by what was there before

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Afforestation, reforestation and proforestation sound identical but answer different questions: was there forest before, and is it healthy?

Wetlands and blue carbon.

Mangroves, seagrass and salt marshes hold about 20–30% of the world’s soil carbon (course figure) because waterlogged sediments have very small k (same model).

Agriculture and deep soil.

Leaving harvest residues and rotating perennial crops raises I; no-till reduces k and also burns less fuel per acre. Soils hold roughly four times the carbon in the atmosphere, about half of it deep and stabilised by mineral-organic bonds.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Converting carbon mass to CO2 mass. Photosynthesis, 6CO2+6H2O→C6H12O6+6O2, fixes one carbon atom per CO2. Atomic masses: C =12, CO2 =44, so 1 tonne C ↔44/12=3.67 tonne CO2.
A plantation locking 5 tC/ha/yr for 20 yr on 10 ha stores 5×20×10=1000 tC =3,667 tCO2 removed from the air (illustrative figures).

Oceanic biological sequestration

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Plankton photosynthesise, take up CO2, and some sink to deep water when they die, taking carbon with them (“biological pump”). Growth is limited by the scarcest nutrient (Liebig’s law of the minimum). In large ocean regions the limiting nutrient is iron, so adding iron (or urea, a nitrogen source) should raise plankton growth and carbon export. Sperm whales do this naturally: iron-rich faeces released near the surface fertilise phytoplankton. Artificial fertilisation is controversial because ocean effects are poorly understood (for example nitrogen oxide release and nutrient disruption).

Mixing layers and seaweed.

Vertical pipes can pump nutrient-rich deep water up to trigger algal blooms (similar logic to iron fertilisation). Seaweed grows fast in shallow areas, captures carbon, and can feed biomethane production; the course estimate says 9% ocean coverage could remove about 53 Gt CO2 per year (a modelling estimate, not an achieved figure).

Physical sequestration

BECCS: why “carbon-neutral” differs from “carbon-negative”

DEFINITION

BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage): grow biomass (removes CO2), convert it to energy (heat, electricity, biofuel via combustion, fermentation or pyrolysis), capture the CO2 and store it underground.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Ledger in units of “100 CO2”:
Fossil plant: emits +100. With 90% capture: +10.
Biomass plant: growth −100, burning +100, net 0 (neutral).
BECCS: growth −100, burning +100, capture −90, net −𝟗𝟎 (negative): it removes carbon from the atmosphere while producing energy.

Burial and biochar

Burying wood mimics how fossil fuels formed. Biochar is charcoal made by pyrolysis (heating biomass without oxygen); mixed into soil or landfill, a large fraction of its carbon resists decay for very long periods, and it improves soil (terra preta).

Ocean physical storage

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Gas solubility follows Henry’s law: dissolved concentration =kHpCO2. It rises with pressure and falls with temperature (the course’s “directly proportional to pressure, inversely to temperature”). Using kH(25∘C)=0.033 and a van’t Hoff correction, kH(5∘C)≈0.060 mol L−1atm−1. At 420 ppm: dissolved CO2 =1.4×10−5 M at 25∘C versus 2.5×10−5 M at 5∘C, i.e. ≈1.8× more in cold water. Deep water is both cold and under enormous pressure, hence the proposal to store CO2 there. Below roughly 3000 m, compressed liquid CO2 becomes denser than seawater and sinks, forming pools.

Environmental concerns.

Deep-sea life has evolved with very stable chemistry and slow reproduction, so a lethal CO2 pulse recovers slowly. Extra CO2 also lowers pH, and CO2 itself disrupts physiology; long-term effects on surface waters remain unknown.

Geological storage: why about 1 km down?

WHY THIS IS TRUE

CO2 becomes a dense supercritical fluid above its critical point: Tc=31.1∘C and Pc=73.8 bar. Depth supplies both conditions.
Pressure (hydrostatic): P≈1+0.098h bar (h in m). P=73.8 bar at h=(73.8−1)/0.098≈740 m.
Temperature (geothermal gradient, 1∘C per ∼37 m from the geothermal notes): T≈15+h/37. At 740 m, T≈35∘C>31.1∘C.
So below roughly 750 m both conditions hold; ∼1 km gives a margin: P≈99 bar, T≈42∘C.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Why compress it? At the surface (15∘C, 1 atm) CO2 has density ≈1.87 kg/m3, so 1 t occupies ≈535 m3. At 1 km depth the supercritical density is ≈630 kg/m3 (typical value), so 1 t occupies ≈1.6 m3, about 340× less volume. Storage in pores of a deep saline formation, depleted oil/gas reservoir or unminable coal bed becomes feasible; stability is expected over millions of years provided the caprock holds.

Chemical sequestration: mineral carbonation

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Calcium and magnesium oxides and silicates react with CO2 to form stable carbonates, which sit at a much lower energy than gaseous CO2 (the same reaction as rock weathering, but speeded up): CaO+CO2→CaCO3,MgO+CO2→MgCO3,Mg2SiO4+2CO2→2MgCO3+SiO2. Mass balance (atomic masses Ca 40, Mg 24.3, Si 28, O 16, C 12): 1 t CaO (56 g/mol) binds 44/56=0.78 t CO2; 1 t forsterite (Mg2SiO4, 140.7 g/mol) binds 88/140.7=0.63 t CO2. A large tonnage of rock is therefore needed, which is the main practical cost.

3Environmental health monitoring: sensors, IoT, AI

Environmental health monitoring with sensors, IoT and AI

DEFINITION

Environmental health monitoring is the systematic collection, analysis and assessment of environmental data (air, water, soil, biodiversity) to detect change, find health risks, track trends and predict future conditions, so that authorities can act. Its end goal is protecting public health, not merely recording numbers.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Monitoring reduces pollution.” Monitoring only measures. Pollution falls when a reading triggers an action (a notice, a shutdown, a warning, a policy). A dashboard that nobody acts on is just a record. Likewise, a high reading is hazard data; risk also needs how many people are exposed and for how long.

The pipeline every system shares

How often must you measure? A sampling argument

WHY THIS IS TRUE

An event of duration D (a pollution spike, an effluent release) can be missed completely if samples are spaced further apart than D. A safe rule of thumb is to sample at least about twice per event duration, i.e. interval ≤D/2 (the same idea as Nyquist sampling). Too slow and you miss events; too fast and you waste power, bandwidth and storage, which matters for battery-powered remote sensors.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Filtering noise without hiding real events. Turbidity readings (NTU) arrive as 4.1,4.3,9.8,4.2,4.4 (one stray spike) and later 6.0,6.8,7.4 (a sustained rise). Rule: raise an alert when the 3-point average exceeds 6.5 (illustrative threshold).
Spike case: (4.3+9.8+4.2)/3=6.1<6.5⇒ no alert (likely noise).
Sustained case: (6.0+6.8+7.4)/3=6.73>6.5⇒ alert.
Trade-off: averaging reduces false alarms but delays detection.

Why PM2.5 gets special attention.

The number is particle diameter in micrometres. A human hair is about 70μm, so a PM2.5 particle is roughly 28× narrower: small enough to travel deep into the lungs.

Remote sensing, with a computed example (NDVI)

DEFINITION

Remote sensing gathers information about an object or area without touching it, usually from satellites, aircraft or drones that record reflected or emitted radiation. (The course abbreviation “RMS” appears to be a slip for RS.) Applications: agriculture, forestry, land cover, water resources.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Different surfaces reflect different wavelengths. Healthy leaves absorb red light (chlorophyll) but strongly reflect near-infrared (NIR) (leaf cell structure). Water absorbs both. The Normalised Difference Vegetation Index uses that contrast: NDVI=NIR−RedNIR+Red∈[−1,1].

WORKED EXAMPLE

Vegetation: NIR =0.50, Red =0.08⇒(0.50−0.08)/(0.58)=0.72.
Bare soil: NIR =0.25, Red =0.20⇒0.05/0.45=0.11.
Water: NIR =0.02, Red =0.04⇒−0.02/0.06=−0.33.
High positive = dense healthy plants; near zero = bare ground; negative = water. A forest cleared by illegal logging drops from ∼0.7 toward ∼0.1 in one image comparison.

Databases and information systems

A database is a collection of inter-related data on various subjects. The course lists: the Ministry of Environment and Forests; NMIS (National Management Information System: research and development projects with scientists and personnel); and ENVIS (Environmental Information System, a network of database centres).

CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course sentence reads “ENVIS functions in 25 countries all over the country”: that is almost certainly a typo for centres/places across India. Learn it as an Indian environmental-information network, and follow the course wording if an MCQ uses it.

Where AI genuinely helps, and where you must be careful

  • Forecasting: sequence models such as LSTM learn how past readings predict the next ones (air quality, floods, CO2, sea level).

  • Detection: deep learning on satellite/aerial images maps habitat, deforestation, illegal dumping; anomaly detection flags unusual boat tracks or sudden sensor changes.

  • Simulation: digital twins (virtual copies of, say, a treatment plant) test operating choices safely; e-noses recognise gases from chemical signatures; robots enter hazardous sites.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“An AI model that predicts air quality must beat simple methods.” Not automatically. Always compare against baselines.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Hourly PM2.5 (μg/m3): 38,42,45,41,47,52. Predict hours 4, 5, 6.
Persistence (next = last): forecasts 45,41,47; absolute errors 4,6,5; MAE =5.0.
3-point moving average: forecasts 41.67,42.67,44.33; errors 0.67,4.33,7.67; MAE =4.22.
A slightly better rule from two lines of arithmetic; but both lag behind the rising trend, which is exactly what a trained model must learn to beat. Report an AI model’s error next to these baselines.

Self-test checklist (try each without looking)

  • Compute Earth’s no-atmosphere temperature and explain what the 33 K gap represents.

  • Explain why sunlight passes through the atmosphere but infrared does not, using wavelengths.

  • Show that a one-layer atmosphere doubles the surface radiation, and check the balance at each level.

  • Convert 1 t of CH4 to CO2-equivalent and explain why GWP is per kg, not a total.

  • Name three natural drivers and say why each fails to explain warming since 1950.

  • Draw the ice–albedo loop and compute the six-fold absorption difference.

  • Compute the percent rise in [H+] for a 0.1 pH drop, and say why the ocean is not “acidic”.

  • Explain why melting sea ice barely changes sea level but melting land ice does.

  • Use Ceq=I/k to explain peat, cropland carbon loss and why sinks saturate.

  • Derive why CO2 is stored at about 1 km (critical point + hydrostatic pressure + geothermal gradient).

  • Do the BECCS ledger and state the difference between neutral and negative.
