1Biodiversity — Definition & Three Levels

DEFINITION Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the full variety of life on Earth, existing simultaneously at three nested levels: genetic (within a species), species (across a region), and ecosystem (across habitat types).

Genetic diversity

Variation in genes within one species — different dog breeds, different rice varieties suited to different soils/climates. It is what lets a species adapt to disease, pests, or a changing climate; a genetically uniform population has no such backup options.

Species diversity

Measured by two separate things: richness (how many different species) and evenness (how balanced their populations are). A monoculture field has low richness and low evenness — one species dominates completely.

Ecosystem diversity

The range of distinct habitat types in a region — forests, wetlands, deserts, coral reefs. More ecosystem types means more distinct services (carbon storage, water filtration, climate regulation) are simultaneously available.

DEFINITION A biodiversity hotspot must meet two conditions simultaneously: high species richness and high endemism (many species found nowhere else) — and it must be under significant threat. A region can be species-rich without qualifying as a hotspot if it isn't also endangered.

India holds four of the world's 36 hotspots: the Himalayas, the Western Ghats, Indo-Burma, and Sundaland (including the Nicobar Islands).

Case study — Silent Valley (Kerala): a 1970s–80s hydroelectric project threatened to submerge this Western Ghats rainforest, home to the endangered lion-tailed macaque. Sustained scientific and public campaigning halted the project in 1983, and the area became a national park — a rare example of conservation stopping a major infrastructure project outright.

3Threats to Biodiversity

ThreatMechanism
Habitat loss & fragmentationUrbanization/agriculture/dams split ecosystems into small, isolated patches — disrupting migration and breeding
PollutionAir pollution → acid rain; water pollution → contaminated aquatic habitats; eutrophication from fertilizer runoff
Climate changeAlters temperature/rainfall faster than species can adapt or migrate; ocean acidification harms corals and shellfish
OverexploitationOverfishing, logging, poaching remove resources faster than natural regeneration
Invasive speciesNon-native species outcompete natives for food/space (e.g. water hyacinth clogging rivers, Lantana camara displacing native undergrowth)
Industrialization / infrastructureDams, highways, ports fragment habitat and disrupt ecological processes directly
WHY THESE THREATS COMPOUND EACH OTHER Fragmentation alone might leave a species at a stable (if small) population. Add climate change on top, and that same species has nowhere left to migrate to — the fragmented patches act as traps rather than refuges. This is why modern conservation planning increasingly works at landscape scale (wildlife corridors) rather than protecting isolated patches.

Case in point — coral bleaching, Great Barrier Reef: heat-stressed corals expel the algae (zooxanthellae) that give them both colour and food. Repeated bleaching events kill the coral outright, collapsing the reef ecosystem that depends on it.

4Species Interactions

Five patterns describe how any two species in a shared habitat affect one another. The distinguishing question is simple: who benefits, and does the other party gain, lose, or stay unaffected?

InteractionOutcomeExample
MutualismBoth benefitBees & flowers; mycorrhizal fungi & plant roots
CommensalismOne benefits, other unaffectedBarnacles on whales; cattle egrets feeding on insects stirred by grazing cattle
ParasitismOne benefits (parasite), one harmed (host)Ticks on mammals; parasitic wasps laying eggs inside other insects
PredationPredator kills and eats preyTiger hunting deer; hawk catching a mouse
CompetitionBoth harmed, fighting over the same resourcePlants competing for sunlight in dense forest (intraspecific or interspecific)

5Extinct, Endangered, Endemic, Rare

COMMON MISCONCEPTION These four terms are often used loosely as synonyms for "in trouble." They actually answer four different questions: Does it still exist anywhere? (extinct) · Is it about to disappear soon? (endangered) · Where does it live? (endemic — a geography question, not a risk-level one) · How many are there? (rare — can exist for entirely natural reasons and not be at high extinction risk at all).
CategoryDefining questionExample
ExtinctNo living individuals anywhere, wild or captiveDodo (Mauritius, extinct ~1680s)
EndangeredVery high risk of extinction in the near futureBengal tiger, Indian vulture (diclofenac poisoning), Ganges river dolphin
EndemicFound in only one specific geographic regionNilgiri tahr, lion-tailed macaque (both Western Ghats only)
RareSmall population/limited range, but not currently high extinction riskHimalayan quail, Gooty tarantula
WHY ENDEMIC SPECIES ARE DISPROPORTIONATELY VULNERABLE An endemic species, by definition, has nowhere else to go — its entire world population sits in one restricted range. A threat that would only dent a widespread species (local habitat loss, one bad flood season) can wipe out an endemic species entirely, because there is no second population elsewhere to fall back on. That's why endemism is one of the two conditions (with species richness) that defines a biodiversity hotspot.

Example — the Dodo: a flightless bird with no natural predators before humans arrived on Mauritius, so it had no instinct to flee. Hunting plus introduced rats/pigs eating its eggs drove it extinct within decades — a textbook case of how quickly an isolated, naive population can collapse once a new threat arrives.

6Conservation Strategies

DEFINITION In-situ conservation protects species within their natural habitat (national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves) — ecological processes and evolution continue undisturbed. Ex-situ conservation protects species outside their natural habitat (zoos, botanical gardens, gene banks) — used when in-situ protection alone isn't enough.
In-situEx-situ
AdvantagesPreserves natural behaviour & evolution; protects the whole ecosystem, not just one speciesControlled conditions; safe from immediate wild threats; enables research & breeding
LimitationsCan't stop threats that originate outside the boundary (poaching, pollution, climate)Expensive; animals may lose natural behaviours; cannot replace a functioning ecosystem

Conservation operates at three levels simultaneously

  • Species level: flagship species (e.g. the Tiger) draw public support and funding; keystone species (e.g. the Asian Elephant) have outsized ecological impact — protecting them protects everything that depends on them.
  • Genetic level: conserving crop wild relatives (e.g. wild rice) preserves traits like flood/salinity tolerance that breeders may need decades from now.
  • Ecosystem level: protecting whole habitats (wetlands, coral reefs, mangroves) rather than single species — this is where landscape-scale planning and wildlife corridors matter.

Key Indian legislation

ActFocus

Forest Conservation Act (1980)Requires central government approval before forest land is diverted to non-forest use

7Genetically Modified (GM) Crops

DEFINITION Genetic engineering directly inserts a useful gene from one organism into another's DNA — including between completely unrelated species — to introduce a trait that wouldn't occur through natural breeding.
WHY GM IS FASTER THAN CONVENTIONAL BREEDING Conventional cross-breeding works by crossing whole organisms and hoping the desired trait combination shows up among the offspring — a slow, imprecise process limited to closely related varieties. Genetic engineering instead inserts only the specific gene responsible for a trait, directly, and can cross species boundaries entirely (e.g. a soil bacterium's gene into cotton). That precision is the entire source of the speed advantage — you're not waiting on chance recombination, you're targeting exactly one change.

India's regulatory reality

GM crops are regulated by the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Ministry of Environment, requiring multi-stage field trials before commercial release. Bt cotton (approved 2002) is the only GM crop currently approved for commercial cultivation in India — it now covers over 90% of India's cotton-growing area. Bt brinjal was cleared in 2009 but placed under moratorium after public/scientific concern; GM mustard remains under review.

CASE STUDY — Bt COTTON IN MAHARASHTRA Rapid adoption after 2002 approval → by 2010, over 90% of the state's cotton area was Bt cotton. Initial wins: 50–60% reduction in insecticide spraying, higher yields, India becoming the world's largest cotton producer. Over time: secondary pests (whiteflies, aphids) emerged to fill the ecological gap left by bollworm suppression, and some bollworm populations began developing Bt resistance — showing that a single-trait solution needs ongoing management (crop rotation, refuge planting), not a one-time fix.

Weighing benefits against concerns

BenefitsConcerns
Higher yield, pest/disease resistanceCross-pollination risk with wild/non-GM relatives
Less chemical pesticide useHerbicide-tolerant crops can drive herbicide-resistant weeds
Drought/salinity toleranceDebated long-term health effects; limited consumer trust without labelling
Enriched nutrition (e.g. Golden Rice + Vitamin A)Patented seeds increase farmer dependency & cost

Global regulatory stance varies sharply: the USA, Brazil and Argentina cultivate GM crops widely; the European Union restricts most GM food crops under the precautionary principle. India sits between these positions — cautious, evidence-driven, and slow to expand beyond Bt cotton.

8Water Pollution

DEFINITION Water pollution is contamination that makes water unfit for drinking, cooking, cleaning or supporting aquatic life. Sources are either point sources (one identifiable location — an outfall pipe, a spill) or non-point sources (diffuse — farmland runoff, urban streets).
COMMON MISCONCEPTION "Non-point sources are less serious because no single culprit is dumping large amounts of waste." In fact non-point sources are often harder to control precisely because there's no single pipe to regulate — the pollution arrives as runoff from thousands of individual farms, lawns, and streets simultaneously, which is why they now account for a large share of real-world water quality problems despite looking "minor" at any one source.

Groundwater vs. surface-water pollution

TypeSourcesWhy it's hard to fix
GroundwaterSeptic tanks, tanneries, deep-well injection, mining, landfill leachateOnce an aquifer is polluted it may stay unusable for decades to thousands of years — groundwater moves extremely slowly, so there's no fast flushing
Surface waterSewage, industrial effluent, detergents, agrochemicals, oil spills, waste heatFast-moving but constantly refreshed with new contaminant loads from runoff

Types of contaminants

  • Organic: detergents, food-processing waste, petroleum hydrocarbons, pesticides.
  • Nitrogen & phosphorus compounds: feed algae growth (see eutrophication below).
  • Inorganic: acids, heavy metals, silt/sediment.
  • Pathogens: bacteria/viruses causing cholera, typhoid, jaundice; coliform bacteria are used as an indicator, not a disease-causer themselves.
  • Thermal: heated discharge from power-plant cooling (this is the direct overlap with Week 3's Thermal Pollution notes).
  • Radioactive: from mining, power plants, medical/research use — persists for millennia.

Eutrophication — the mechanism worth understanding in full

Biomagnification — a second, separate mechanism

WHY DDT-LIKE CHEMICALS GET MORE DANGEROUS GOING UP THE FOOD CHAIN Some pollutants (like DDT) are not water-soluble, so an organism's body can't flush them out — they accumulate in fat tissue instead. When a predator eats many prey, it inherits all of their accumulated chemical load, not just an average share. Repeated up the food chain, concentrations multiply at each trophic transfer — this is exactly the same 10%-law food-chain structure from Week 1, but running in the wrong direction: energy shrinks by 90% per level, while a persistent toxin's concentration grows.

Control measures: judicious pesticide/fertilizer use, nitrogen-fixing plants, diverting manure runoff to settlement basins, separating sewage from stormwater drainage, afforestation to reduce runoff, and treating wastewater before discharge (monitored via BOD, COD, nitrates, phosphates, oil/grease, toxic metals).

9Disaster Management

DEFINITION Disaster management is the cycle of prevention → preparedness → response → recovery, applied to both natural and human-made disasters. Note that this is a cycle, not a one-time action — recovery efforts feed back into improved prevention for the next event.

Floods

TypeTrigger
RiverineRivers overflow after prolonged rain or snowmelt
FlashSudden, localized heavy rain, dam breaks, or rapid snowmelt — most dangerous due to lack of warning time
CoastalStorm surges, high tides, tsunamis
UrbanDrainage systems overwhelmed by rain; worsened by concrete surfaces and poor planning

Mitigation: drainage planning, embankments/levees, afforestation, dams/reservoirs for controlled release, early warning systems, and porous/green urban infrastructure.

Earthquakes

MECHANISM Earthquakes are the release of stress that has slowly built up along a fault line as tectonic plates try to move past, apart from, or into one another (recall Week 1's mantle-convection driver). The focus is where this release happens underground; the epicenter is the point directly above it on the surface, where shaking is strongest. Reservoir-induced seismicity — where the sheer weight of a large dam's water changes stress on a nearby fault — is why the 1967 Koynanagar earthquake in India is linked to the Koyna Dam.

Mitigation: seismic zoning, strict building codes (reinforced concrete/steel that sways rather than collapses), retrofitting older structures, base isolators (rubber/metal shock absorbers under skyscrapers), and early warning systems that detect fast-but-weak P-waves before the slower, damaging S-waves arrive.

Cyclones

WHY CYCLONES SPIN THE WAY THEY DO Warm ocean water evaporates, rises, and leaves low pressure behind it; surrounding air rushes in to fill that gap. Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect) deflects that inrushing air, forcing it into a spiral rather than a straight line — counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern. Because the Coriolis effect is essentially zero at the equator, cyclones almost never form within about 5° latitude of it, regardless of how warm the water is.

Mitigation: coastal embankments, mangrove plantations (absorb storm energy, act as windbreaks), satellite/Doppler radar early warning, cyclone shelters, resilient infrastructure.

Landslides

TypeDescription
RockfallBoulders break loose and tumble down steep slopes
Debris flowFast-moving mix of soil, rock and water
MudslideSaturated soil flows downhill after heavy rain/deforestation
SlumpA block of land detaches and rotates downward
CreepSlowest type — often unnoticed until tilted trees/structures reveal it
WHY DEFORESTATION IS A RECURRING CAUSE ACROSS FLOODS, LANDSLIDES AND EROSION Tree roots physically bind soil particles together and their canopy intercepts rainfall before it hits bare ground. Remove the trees, and rain reaches soil at full force and the soil has lost its structural binding — the same missing mechanism destabilizes slopes (landslides), increases runoff (floods), and strips topsoil (erosion). This is why afforestation appears as a mitigation measure across all three disaster types in these notes.

Case study — Kedarnath (2013): intense rainfall combined with poor land management and inadequate drainage triggered a massive landslide/debris flow that buried villages, killed thousands, and destroyed roads and temples — a stark illustration of how mitigation failures compound during an extreme rainfall event.
