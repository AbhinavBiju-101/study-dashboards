1What Is EIA, and Why

DEFINITION Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) evaluates a proposed project's likely environmental — and social, cultural, and health — consequences before it begins, so that harm can be avoided or mitigated by design rather than discovered afterward.
WHY "BEFORE THE PROJECT BEGINS" IS THE WHOLE POINT Every mitigation option on an EIA's hierarchy — avoid, minimise, restore, offset — gets progressively weaker and more expensive the later it's applied. Avoiding a wetland entirely (chosen at the planning stage) costs nothing extra; restoring a wetland after it's been filled in is enormously expensive and often only partially reversible. EIA's entire value proposition rests on catching problems while "avoid" is still a cheap, available option — which is exactly why it's a pre-construction requirement, not a post-construction audit.

Objectives

  • Identify potential impacts, positive and negative
  • Predict their magnitude and significance
  • Evaluate alternatives to the proposed project
  • Develop mitigation measures
  • Inform decision-makers and the public
  • Promote environmentally sound, sustainable development

Key principles

ParticipationStakeholders are involved in the process
TransparencyInformation is open and accessible
AccountabilityResponsibility for outcomes is clearly assigned
CredibilityAssessment is reliable and objective
IntegrationEnvironmental considerations run through every stage of project planning, not bolted on at the end

2The EIA Process, Step by Step

Screening — deciding if a full EIA is even needed

Projects are checked against two schedules:

ScheduleRule
Schedule 1EIA required in every case (e.g. integrated iron/steel works; thermal power stations ≥300 MW)
Schedule 2EIA required only if the project's nature, size, or location is likely to cause significant environmental effects — a larger, more varied list than Schedule 1

Scoping — deciding what the EIA should actually study

Identifies key issues and concerns early, by asking: who is concerned, what are their concerns, why, and at what threshold does a change become unacceptable? This produces the Terms of Reference (ToR) that bound the rest of the assessment.

Impact assessment — classifying what kind of impact each effect is

AxisTwo poles
ValencePositive or negative
PathwayDirect or indirect
DurationShort-term or long-term
RecoverabilityReversible or irreversible

Mitigation hierarchy — in strict priority order

  1. Avoid the impact (e.g. relocate the project away from a sensitive site)
  2. Minimise the impact (e.g. control emissions)
  3. Rehabilitate / restore (e.g. replant vegetation)
  4. Offset / compensate (e.g. biodiversity offsets elsewhere)
WHY THIS ORDER IS NOT ARBITRARY Each step down this list is a fallback for when the step above genuinely can't be achieved — it's a hierarchy of decreasing certainty. "Avoid" guarantees zero impact. "Offset" only guarantees an attempt at equivalence elsewhere, which is inherently harder to verify (is a newly planted forest patch really ecologically equivalent to the old-growth one that was cleared?). Regulators default to the top of the list first for exactly this reason — every step down trades away certainty for feasibility.

3Methodologies for Impact Assessment

From simplest/fastest to most rigorous/slowest
MethodHow it worksStrengthLimitation
Ad hocOpinion poll, expert opinion, or Delphi (structured questionnaire) methodSimple, fast, minimal expertise neededGuess-based; no cause-effect shown; not suited to large or complex data
ChecklistStructured list of environmental factors (simple / descriptive / scaling / weighted)Broad coverage, easy to useNo interrelationships between impacts shown
Matrix (e.g. Leopold Matrix)2-D grid — project activities (columns) × environmental factors (rows); each cell scores magnitude & significanceVisual cause-effect; shows multiple impacts from one activityBecomes unwieldy with many parameters; weak on cumulative effects
Network analysisMaps chains of impact as a flowchart (e.g. road → deforestation → erosion → sedimentation → aquatic harm)Reveals indirect & cumulative impacts other methods missRequires more expertise and time; can get very complex
ModelingMathematical/computer simulation (air-quality, water-quality, noise, ecological, risk models)Quantitative, predictive, supports scenario testingNeeds accurate baseline data; sensitive to input assumptions; costly
Overlay (GIS)Transparent map layers showing spatial distribution of impactsDisplays physical and social dimensions together, before/afterRequires GIS technical capacity
WHY NETWORK ANALYSIS CATCHES WHAT A MATRIX MISSES A matrix only records the direct link between one activity and one environmental factor — "road construction" against "soil quality," scored in isolation. It has no mechanism for showing that the same road construction's effect on soil then causes a separate effect on river sedimentation, which then causes a third effect on aquatic life. Network analysis exists specifically to trace that kind of multi-step chain — which is exactly the "road → deforestation → erosion → sedimentation → aquatic harm" example above.

Persistent challenges

  • Weak technical/financial capacity in many developing regions
  • Limited public participation in practice, despite it being a stated principle
  • Weak enforcement of EIA conditions after approval
  • Assessments often examine one project in isolation, missing the cumulative impact of several nearby projects together

5Roots of Indian Environmental Law

India's core environmental legislation didn't emerge from abstract policy planning — it traces to two specific historical triggers worth remembering as anchors for the whole legal framework:

TriggerYearConsequence
UN Conference on the Human Environment, Stockholm1972India raised environmental concerns internationally; 5 June is now observed as World Environment Day
Bhopal gas tragedy — methyl isocyanate (MIC) leak from a Union Carbide plant, 2–3 December 19841984Directly triggered the Environment (Protection) Act, passed 19 November 1986
WHY BHOPAL SPECIFICALLY PRODUCED AN "UMBRELLA" LAW Before 1986, India already had separate Water (1974) and Air (1981) Acts — but Bhopal exposed a gap neither law covered well: a single catastrophic industrial accident releasing a hazardous substance that was neither purely a "water" nor purely an "air" problem. The Environment (Protection) Act was deliberately designed as umbrella legislation precisely to close that gap — giving the central government broad emergency powers that didn't depend on classifying a disaster into one narrow existing category first.

6The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

DEFINITION Passed under Article 253 of the Constitution, in force from 19 November 1986. 26 sections across four chapters, giving the Central Government broad powers to prevent, control and abate environmental pollution of every kind.
ChapterCovers
IDefinitions — Environment, Environmental Pollutant, Environmental Pollution, Hazardous Substance
IICentral Government's powers to protect and improve environmental quality
IIIPrevention, control and abatement of pollution
IVMiscellaneous — protects actions taken in good faith

Critical definitions

  • Environment: water, air, land, and the interrelationships among them and with living beings.
  • Environmental pollutant: any solid, liquid, or gaseous substance present in the environment beyond its permissible limit.
  • Environmental pollution: the process by which such pollution occurs.

What the Act actually empowers

  • Sets standards for air, water, and soil quality with maximum permissible pollutant limits
  • Lets the Central Government restrict or ban industries/operations in specified areas
  • Has evolved emission/effluent standards for 61 categories of industry
  • Requires highly polluting industries to meet standards within one year of notification
  • Requires an annual environmental audit report from industries needing Water/Air Act consent, due by 30 September each year

Role of pollution control boards: advise industries on effluent treatment and cleaner technology; promote recycle/reuse/reduce; encourage biogas recovery. Example: standard effluent treatment plants near Ranipet, Vellore — installed specifically to address chromium contamination from tannery clusters (the same case referenced in Week 1's hazardous-waste discussion).

7The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

DEFINITION Enacted to prevent and control water pollution and to maintain or restore the "wholesomeness" of water, applicable across all Indian states.

The consent mechanism — how it actually gets enforced

Core prohibitions

  • No person may knowingly discharge poisonous/noxious/polluting matter into a stream, well, sewer, or land beyond State Board standards.
  • No person may impede a stream's proper water flow in a way that aggravates pollution.
  • No industry may be established, or new/altered sewage outlets used, without prior SPCB consent.

Appeals against an SPCB order go to a state-constituted Appellate Authority — the same appeal structure applies under the Air Act.

Roles: Central vs. State Pollution Control Boards

Central PCBState PCB
Advises central government on corrective measures; coordinates technical guidance nationally; sets water-quality standards; develops/recognises testing labsAdvises state government on corrective measures for locally polluted water bodies; samples water directly; can close down non-compliant industries; grants/denies consent for new industries in-state

8The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

DEFINITION 50 sections, amended in 1987 to also cover noise pollution. Prevents and controls pollution from solid, liquid, and gaseous emissions — no industrial plant may operate without SPCB/PCC consent.

Regulated pollutants include lead, carbon, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, volatile organic compounds and other toxic substances beyond prescribed limits.

The consent process mirrors the Water Act's: an investor applies with prescribed fees; the Board must grant or deny consent within 4 months; consent conditions specify required pollution-control equipment.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION "The Air Act only deals with smoke and gases, so noise complaints have nothing to do with it." The 1987 amendment specifically folded noise pollution into the Air Act's scope — which is why firecracker/industrial noise regulation in India is often litigated under the same Act as smokestack emissions, not a separate noise-specific law.

9Forest & Wildlife Protection Acts

The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980

WHY FOREST PROTECTION IS FRAMED AS A HYDROLOGICAL ISSUE, NOT JUST A HABITAT ONE Forests aren't protected only because they house wildlife — the Act's justification explicitly rests on forests regulating the hydrological cycle (50–80% of atmospheric moisture in forested regions comes from transpiration) and absorbing CO₂ to moderate warming. Losing forest cover therefore doesn't just remove habitat; it directly destabilises regional rainfall patterns and soil retention, which is the same mechanism behind the flood/landslide risk explained in Week 2.
  • Objectives: protect and conserve forests; ensure judicious use of forest products.
  • Key rule: reserve forest land cannot be "de-reserved" without prior Central Government permission — this closes the loophole where states could quietly reclassify protected forest for development.
  • Also targets illegal cutting, encroachment, fire, grazing and shifting agriculture.

The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1971

(Amended 1982, 1986, 1991, 1993, 2002, 2006.) Provides for national parks and sanctuaries, regulates zoos and trade in wild animals, and establishes a Wild Life Advisory Board with a Chief Wild Life Warden per state.

Protected area typeCount in India (per this Act)
National Parks104
Sanctuaries551
Conservation Reserves86
Biosphere Reserves11

An updated Endangered Wildlife list is maintained annually. A noted limitation from the record: the Act has been criticised as more effective at restricting environment-friendly tribal communities' traditional access than at actually deterring determined poachers and illegal miners — a reminder that a strong law on paper doesn't automatically translate into effective enforcement against the intended target (echoing the "weak enforcement" challenge raised for EIA in Section 4).

Self-Test Checklist

  • Explain why EIA's mitigation hierarchy (avoid → minimise → restore → offset) is ordered by decreasing certainty, not arbitrarily.
  • Distinguish Schedule 1 from Schedule 2 projects in the screening stage.
  • Explain what a network-analysis method can show that a matrix method structurally cannot.
  • Name the two historical triggers (1972 and 1984) behind India's core environmental legislation, and what each one produced.
  • Explain why the Environment (Protection) Act is called "umbrella legislation" and what specific gap it was designed to close.
  • Walk through the Water Act's consent-then-verify enforcement loop from application to closure.
  • Explain why the Forest Conservation Act frames deforestation as a water-cycle issue, not only a habitat-loss issue.
  • State which amendment year folded noise pollution into the Air Act.
