1Energy resources and solar energy

Energy resources, net energy and the scale of the sun

DEFINITION

Energy comes from the Greek en-ergon, “in work”. Non-renewable resources (oil, petroleum, diesel, coal, natural gas) are exhaustible; renewable resources (solar, geothermal, wind, biomass, hydro, tidal) are replaced as fast as, or faster than, we use them. Net energy = useful energy produced − energy needed to develop it.

History in the course notes.

Humans began with firewood and animals; Franklin discovered electricity; Faraday built the dynamo in 1831 (mechanical → electrical); Edison invented the light bulb; Tesla the AC motor (electrical → mechanical). A country’s energy consumption is usually read as a sign of its development.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Energy is used up.” Energy is never destroyed, only converted to less useful forms (mostly heat). “Resource” questions are really about quality: can we get concentrated, controllable energy out of it for less energy than we put in?
“Renewable means no environmental cost.” Renewable describes the supply; every system still has manufacturing, land, wildlife or material costs (you will see these in each advantages/disadvantages list).

Net energy: output must clearly beat input, and the output must be clean

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Define EROI=Eout/Ein and net energy =Eout−Ein. Two sources can have the same output but very different net energy.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Source A: out =100, in =10⇒ net =90, EROI =10.Source B: out =100, in =80⇒ net =20, EROI =1.25.
B delivers little surplus for society. If its EROI fell below 1 it would be an energy sink, not a resource. This is the course’s argument against nuclear (large energy for uranium mining and enrichment, fuel-rod fabrication, safe storage of used rods ⇒ low net energy). Coal has higher net energy than nuclear in the notes, but burning it releases  and other gases, so high net energy alone is not enough.

Power versus energy, and how big is the sun?

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Power is a rate (watt, W); energy is power × time (watt-hour, Wh; 1 kWh =3.6 MJ). The sun’s power reaching Earth is S0πRE2 with S0=1361 W/m2 and RE=6.371×106 m: 1361×1.275×1014=1.74×1017 W =174,000 TW. World primary energy use is about 16 TW (the course chart). Ratio ≈104; equivalently, one year of world energy use (16×1012×3.16×107=5.0×1020 J) equals about 5.0×1020/1.74×1017≈2900 s, roughly 50 minutes of intercepted sunlight. (The course chart lists ∼23,000 TW for the practically usable solar resource; either way it exceeds demand by over a thousand times.)

WORKED EXAMPLE

Capacity factor. Bhadla Solar Park (Rajasthan; 14,000 acres; 2250 MW): 2250MW×8760h=19.7 TWh if it ran at full power all year. Solar produces nothing at night and less in cloud, so with a capacity factor of ∼25% (illustrative): 19.7×0.25≈4.9 TWh/yr. Land use: 14,000×4047m2=5.67×107 m2, so 2250MW/56.7km2≈40 MW/km2.

Direct and indirect solar energy

Passive solar: no pumps, no moving parts

DEFINITION

A passive system collects and uses sunlight without mechanical devices. Course examples: evaporating seawater for salt, drying food and clothes, the solar box cooker, and daylighting (south-facing windows).

WHY THIS IS TRUE

A dark surface absorbs sunlight and warms. In a box cooker the inner chamber is painted black, the box is insulated, and a glass cover lets visible light in. Hot surfaces re-emit infrared, which glass blocks, and the insulation and cover stop warm air escaping. This is the same trap as Week 5’s greenhouse effect on a small scale. Temperatures above 100∘C are reached, enough to cook, but slowly.

India’s largest solar steam kitchen.

At the Brahma Kumaris ashram, Mount Abu (Rajasthan), working since the 1990s: 84 parabolic concentrators on the roof heat a receiver and make steam; it can cook for more than 38,000 people.

Daylighting.

In the northern hemisphere the south side of a building receives the most sun, so buildings are designed south-facing with large south windows. Passive systems have no operating cost and need no pumps, fans or electricity, but depend on climate and fail under cloud.

Active solar thermal: collect, move, store

DEFINITION

An active system uses pumps or motors to move heated fluid or air to storage for later use. Components: solar collector (on the roof) + storage tank. Collectors are classified as non-concentrating (area catching light = absorber area) or concentrating (catching area > absorber area).

Flat-plate collector (non-concentrating)

A metal box with a transparent glass cover on top, a black absorber plate, parallel copper pipes carrying water, and insulation on all other faces. It sits on a roof facing south (northern hemisphere) at an inclination of 30–60∘ to the horizontal. Heated water goes to the tank; uses: domestic hot water, swimming pools in warm climates.

Concentrating collectors, and why concentration buys temperature

WHY THIS IS TRUE

A concentrator focuses light from aperture area Aa onto a smaller absorber area Ar; the concentration ratio is C=Aa/Ar. An ideal black absorber that loses energy only by radiation reaches equilibrium when CS=σT4, so Tmax=(CSσ)1/4(S=1000W/m2). Because of the fourth root, 10× more concentration gives only 101/4=1.78× higher absolute temperature. The limit is the sun’s own surface temperature (∼5800 K): you cannot heat something hotter than the thing that is heating it.

Solar furnace and heliostats.

A solar furnace reflects sunlight from parabolic mirrors or heliostats (sun-tracking flat mirrors) onto a focal point to make very high temperatures.

Solar thermal electricity and the Carnot limit

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Concentrated heat makes steam that drives a turbine and generator, as in any thermal plant. No heat engine can beat the Carnot efficiency ηmax=1−Tc/Th (temperatures in kelvin). Hotter input means higher possible efficiency, which is why concentration matters.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Solar tower steam at 600∘C (873 K), condenser at 30∘C (303 K): ηmax=1−303/873=65% (real plants achieve roughly 35–40%).
Compare OTEC (Week 3 notes): 25∘C (298 K) warm water, 4∘C (277 K) deep water: ηmax=1−277/298=7.0%, so the observed 1–3% is not bad engineering; it is thermodynamics.

Solar cooling.

Same idea as conventional refrigeration (a liquid refrigerant evaporates and absorbs heat from the surroundings), but solar heat replaces electricity to drive the cycle.

Photovoltaics: turning light directly into electricity

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Solar panels and solar water heaters are the same thing.” A solar water heater is thermal (light → heat). A PV panel is electronic (light → electricity, with no moving parts).
“Photovoltaic means electricity produced by a chemical reaction.” The course gloss is misleading: PV involves no net chemical change. “Photo” is light; “voltaic” honours Volta and refers to voltage.
“Hotter sun means more PV power.” More light increases current, but heat lowers voltage and efficiency (see below).

The physics in four steps

WHY THIS IS TRUE

1. Photon energy. E=hc/λ=1240eVnm/λ. Silicon has band gap Eg=1.12 eV, so only photons with λ<1240/1.12=1107 nm can free an electron. Longer-wavelength photons pass through (wasted); shorter ones free an electron but the excess energy becomes heat. This is why even a perfect single-junction silicon cell cannot reach 100% (theoretical ceiling ∼30–33%).
2. Doping. Pure silicon has 4 valence electrons. Adding phosphorus (5) gives spare electrons (n-type); adding boron (3) leaves missing electrons called holes (p-type).
3. The junction. Where n meets p, electrons and holes diffuse across and leave fixed ions behind, building an internal electric field that points from n to p.
4. Light. A photon makes an electron–hole pair. The field sweeps electrons to the n side and holes to the p side. Connect the two contacts through a load and electrons flow through it: current.

Cell, module, array: why series adds volts and parallel adds amps

One silicon cell gives only ∼0.6–0.7 V. Joining Ns cells in series adds voltages (same current flows through all); joining strings in parallel adds currents. A 60-cell module has Voc≈60×0.67=40 V. Modules form panels, panels form arrays, and arrays feed the grid. Output is DC, so an inverter converts it to AC for appliances and the grid.

Reading a PV curve and doing the sums

WHY THIS IS TRUE

At V=0 the cell gives its largest current, Isc (short circuit); at I=0 the largest voltage, Voc (open circuit). Power P=VI is zero at both ends and peaks at the maximum power point (MPP). Fill factor FF=Pmax/(VocIsc) measures how “square” the curve is. Efficiency η=Pmax/(GA) with irradiance G=1000 W/m2.

WORKED EXAMPLE

From the model: FF=299/(40×9.5)=0.79. Module area 1.6m2: η=299/(1000×1.6)=18.7% (course: commercial panels 10–300 W).
Daily energy: 299W×5 peak-sun hours ×0.8 (system losses) ≈1.2 kWh/day.
Heat penalty: power falls about 0.4% per ∘C above 25∘C; a cell at 65∘C loses 0.004×40=16%, giving ≈251 W.

Materials and uses (course).

Silicon with boron/phosphorus impurities; cadmium telluride, copper indium gallium diselenide; arsenic and antimony have also been used; emerging organic, quantum-dot and hybrid perovskite cells. A cell is thinner than a human hair. Uses: satellites, remote villages and street lighting, desalination and water pumping, telecom devices and railway signals, calculators and watches, and large plants such as Bhadla (14,000 acres, 2250 MW, Rajasthan, the world’s largest).

CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course calls the perovskite example “calcium titanate”. Perovskite is a crystal structure named after the mineral CaTiO3; solar-cell perovskites are usually lead-halide compounds. For MCQs follow the course wording; for understanding, treat “perovskite” as the structure.

2Hydroelectric and wind energy

Hydroelectric power

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Water of mass m at height H stores mgH. Per second, ṁ=ρQ, so the electrical power is P=ηρgQH (η efficiency, Q flow m3/s). Chain: gravitational potential → kinetic (in the penstock) → mechanical (turbine, shaft) → electrical (generator). Hydropower efficiency exceeds 80% whereas thermal plants are as low as 40% (course) because no heat-to-work conversion is involved, so no Carnot limit.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Q=100 m3/s, H=50 m, η=0.9: P=0.9×1000×9.81×100×50=44 MW.
Pumped storage: energy in V=106 m3 raised 300 m: E=ρgHV=1000×9.81×300×106=2.9×1012 J =817 MWh. Surplus off-peak power pumps water up; peak demand releases it. Dam + two reservoirs at different heights is the key difference from a plain dam; run-of-river uses natural flow at small scale.

Course facts: >18% of world electricity, fourth-largest source; Romans used waterwheels for grinding; first hydroelectric plant: Fox River, Wisconsin, 30 Sept 1882; plants last at least 40–50 years. Drawbacks: expensive dams, altered landscape, habitat loss, displacement of people (especially tribal communities), flooding of large areas, disputes between states or countries.

Wind energy

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Moving air of density ρ through rotor area A at speed v carries kinetic power 12ρAv3. A turbine captures a fraction Cp, and no turbine can exceed the Betz limit 16/27=59% (the wind must keep moving to leave the rotor). Because of v3, a site with twice the wind gives eight times the power: siting matters more than almost anything else.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Rotor diameter 100 m (A=7854 m2), v=12 m/s, ρ=1.225 kg/m3: available =12(1.225)(7854)(123)=8.3 MW; with Cp=0.40: 3.3 MW. At 6 m/s: 0.4 MW for the same Cp (one-eighth).

Course facts: wind is indirect solar (uneven heating → pressure differences). Ancient Egyptians sailed with wind ∼4000–3500 BC; windmills appeared in the 8th–9th centuries (Middle East/Western Asia). Horizontal-axis turbines are most common and more efficient; vertical-axis have a vertical shaft. Gearbox → generator → converter → grid. Wind farms feed the grid more easily. Tamil Nadu and Gujarat lead India (NIWE); India is fifth in the world. Drawbacks: unreliable wind, noise, remote sites, visual pollution, radio/TV interference, bird and bat deaths, costly setup.

3Ocean thermal and geothermal energy

Ocean thermal energy (OTEC)

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Needs a temperature difference >20∘C between warm surface and cold deep water, largest (20–25∘C) in the tropics. The Carnot ceiling for 25∘C and 4∘C is 7% (see the Carnot example in the solar-thermal section), explaining the real 1–3%. Closed cycle: warm water boils a low-boiling working fluid (ammonia −33∘C, propane −42∘C, R-134a −26.3∘C) in a heat exchanger; vapour drives the turbine; cold deep water condenses it; the fluid recycles. Open cycle: warm seawater itself is flash-boiled in a low-pressure chamber. Why that works: water boils at 25∘C when its saturation pressure is only ≈31.6 hPa (≈3% of atmospheric), so a partial vacuum makes warm seawater boil; the steam condenses to desalinated water.

Advantages: renewable, clean, reliable (as long as the sun shines), weather-independent, low maintenance, far from settlements. Disadvantages: costly installation, harm to marine life from pumping, low efficiency, location-specific, cost of transmitting power from floating plants to land.

Geothermal energy

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Temperature rises about 1∘C per 35–40 m of depth. With a 15∘C surface, drilling the course’s “1 or 2 miles” (1.6–3.2 km) gives only 15+1600/37=58∘C to 15+3200/37≈100∘C in ordinary rock: warm, not steam-hot. Power plants therefore sit where the gradient is much steeper (volcanic regions, hot springs, geysers, magma “hot spots” where water meets hot rock), which is why geothermal is location-specific. Hydrothermal cycle: cold water injected down one well, heated, rises as hot water/steam through another (pressure drops → water flashes to steam), drives a turbine, is condensed in cooling towers and re-injected.

CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course explains Earth’s core heat as “nuclear fusion reactions”. Fusion does not occur in Earth’s interior; the heat comes from leftover formation heat and radioactive decay. Answer in the course’s words for MCQs but know the real source. Other course facts: not indirect solar; magma, hot spots, geysers; available for 2–3 billion years; drawbacks include huge up-front cost, earthquake risk, release of H2S and SO2.

4Energy from biomass

Biomass energy

DEFINITION

Biomass is organic material or waste produced by plants or animals: wood, crop residues (wheat/rice straw, groundnut shells, jute sticks, maize cobs), oilseeds, latex plants, starch and sugar crops, municipal and industrial solid waste, sewage, cattle dung. Plant-based biomass is mostly lignocellulosic (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin). Biomass is indirect solar energy: photosynthesis stores sunlight as chemical energy. Three product families: bioenergy (heat, electricity), biofuels (bioethanol, biodiesel) and biogas.

Combustion and the carbon ledger

Burn the biomass, boil water, spin a turbine, drive a generator. Best suited to countries where fuelwood grows fast (tropical countries).

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Burning releases the CO2 the plant took in while growing, so the cycle is roughly carbon-neutral. Check with chemistry: dry wood is about 50% carbon by mass, so 1 t of wood contains 0.5 tC and releases 0.5×44/12=1.83 tCO2. A fast-growing crop regrows (and re-absorbs this) within months or years; fossil carbon was stored for millions of years, so burning it adds new carbon to the fast cycle.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Carbon-neutral means zero emissions.” The chimney still emits CO2. Neutrality holds only if the carbon is re-absorbed within a useful time and if growing, harvesting and transporting the fuel do not burn much fossil energy. Cutting a slow-growing forest can leave a long “carbon debt”. This is why the course also lists deforestation as a risk. (Week 5 BECCS goes further: capture the CO2 and it becomes carbon-negative.)

WORKED EXAMPLE

1 t dry wood at ∼18 GJ/t =18,000 MJ /3.6=5000 kWh of heat. At 25% electrical efficiency: 1250 kWh of electricity. The cooler the steam, the lower the Carnot ceiling, which is why small biomass plants are inefficient.

Anaerobic digestion and biogas

DEFINITION

Anaerobic digestion is microbial breakdown of biodegradable waste with no oxygen, producing biogas (methane about 65%, plus CO2, hydrogen sulphide, moisture) and digestate (a fertiliser-rich residue). A biogas plant is an anaerobic digester.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Why methane and CO2? Balance atoms for a general organic molecule CnHaOb turning into x CH4 + y CO2 using w H2O:
C: n=x+y; H: a+2w=4x; O: b+w=2y. Solving gives x=n2+a8−b4,y=n2−a8+b4. Glucose C6H12O6: x=3+1.5−1.5=3, y=3⇒ 50% CH4. A fat (palmitic acid C16H32O2): x=8+4−0.5=11.5, y=4.5⇒72% CH4. The more hydrogen-rich and oxygen-poor the waste, the more methane, consistent with the course’s ∼65% for mixed feed.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Household sizing (rule-of-thumb values). Cooking needs ∼0.34 m3 biogas per person per day: 5 people ⇒1.7 m3/day. Fresh cattle dung gives ∼0.04 m3/kg, so 1.7/0.04≈43 kg dung/day, about 4–5 cattle (India has the world’s largest cattle population: course).
Energy: methane ≈36 MJ/m3; at 65% ≈23 MJ/m3≈6.5 kWh/m3.
Climate: CH4 + 2O2→ CO2 + 2H2O, so 1 t CH4→44/16=2.75 t CO2. With GWP100≈28: vented =28 tCO2e, burned =2.75 (biogenic) ⇒≈90% cut. Capturing methane is the climate gain.

CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course says biogas burns “without producing harmful gases such as CO2, CO, NO2, SO2”. Strictly, burning biogas does release CO2 (biogenic, from recently fixed carbon) and traces of other gases; what is true is that it burns cleanly, without smoke or soot, and avoids methane escaping. Use the course wording in an MCQ; know the nuance for real understanding. Disadvantages in the notes: inefficient and uneconomical on a large scale; large methane leaks cause a greenhouse effect.

Pyrolysis: heat without oxygen

DEFINITION

Pyrolysis is thermochemical decomposition of biomass at high temperature in the absence of oxygen; the process is irreversible. Main product is a solid, charcoal/biochar; industrial pyrolysis can also make liquid fuel and syngas.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Without oxygen the material cannot burn; heat just drives off water and volatile compounds (rich in oxygen), leaving carbon-rich char. Removing water and oxygen raises energy per kilogram: course says charcoal has about double the energy of the original biomass, and it is easier to transport. Industrial conditions in the notes: pressure and above 800∘F; convert: (800−32)×5/9=427∘C.

Fermentation and bioethanol

DEFINITION

Fermentation: yeast or bacteria convert sugars into alcohol, acids or gases. Bioethanol is made from sugary/starchy crops (sugarcane, corn, sorghum). Blends: E10 =10% ethanol +90% gasoline; E85 =85%/15%. E100 (Brazil hydrous ethanol) and flex-fuel vehicles exist.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Reaction: C6H12O6→2C2H5OH+2CO2. Mass: 180→92 ethanol +88 CO2, so the ideal yield is 92/180=0.511 kg ethanol per kg glucose. Starch hydrolyses first, (C6H10O5)n+nH2O→nC6H12O6, giving 180/162=1.11 kg glucose per kg starch.

WORKED EXAMPLE

1 t starch →1.111 t glucose →1.111×0.511=0.568 t ethanol. At density 0.789 kg/L this is ≈720 L (theoretical maximum; real plants reach ∼90–95% of it).
Why ethanol lowers fuel economy: energy per litre: ethanol ≈21.1 MJ/L, gasoline ≈32 MJ/L. E10: 0.1(21.1)+0.9(32)=30.9 MJ/L =96.6% of gasoline, about 3.4% less energy; the course quotes 2–3% lower fuel economy since higher octane partly compensates. E85: 0.85(21.1)+0.15(32)=22.7 MJ/L =71% of gasoline.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Bioethanol runs any car.” The course says it cannot be used alone in ordinary cars and is blended (E10, E85). Regular cars take low blends (E10–E25); higher blends need flex-fuel or dedicated engines (Brazil’s E100 shows this works with suitable engines).

Advantages (course): clean fuel, raises octane number, lowers greenhouse emissions; farmer-owned plants support rural economy; a desirable alternative to gasoline. Drawback: lower calorific value, so 2–3% lower fuel economy.

Biodiesel

DEFINITION

Biodiesel is a renewable, biodegradable liquid fuel made of long-chain fatty-acid esters (FAME) from vegetable oils and animal fats plus an alcohol (methanol or ethanol) with a catalyst. It has properties similar to petroleum diesel and can be used alone or blended.

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Transesterification: triglyceride +3CH3OH→catalyst3 fatty-acid methyl esters + glycerol. Why needed: raw oil is too viscous for diesel engines; swapping the glycerol backbone for small methyl groups gives molecules that flow and burn like diesel.
Free fatty acids in the oil would form soap with a basic catalyst, so oils with many of them are first treated with an acid-catalysed esterification (the course figure).

WORKED EXAMPLE

Triglyceride ≈885 g/mol, methanol 32, FAME ≈296, glycerol 92. Check: 885+3(32)=981=3(296)+92=980. From 1000 kg oil: methanol ≈108 kg, biodiesel ≈905 kg, glycerol ≈104 kg.

Sources and India.

Rapeseed, soybean, oil palm, sunflower, jatropha, animal fats. India relies mainly on jatropha, a shrub of tropical countries (India, Brazil, Africa) that grows in hot, dry areas without frequent rain or fertiliser; it is non-edible, so it avoids competing with food crops. Indian Railways plans large-scale use in locomotives to cut oil bills.

5Hydrogen energy and the solar-hydrogen cycle

Hydrogen energy and the solar–hydrogen cycle

DEFINITION

Hydrogen is the simplest element (one proton, one electron). It is abundant but almost always combined (water, hydrocarbons), not free as H2 gas. So hydrogen is an energy carrier, not an energy source: it must be made using some primary energy (fossil, nuclear, solar, wind, biomass, hydro, geothermal), stored or moved, then converted to heat or electricity. Burning it, or reacting it in a fuel cell, produces water vapour and no CO2.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Hydrogen is a source like oil.” There are no hydrogen wells; making H2 costs energy (the course’s “net energy is negative” point). It is like a battery or electricity: valuable for moving and storing energy.
“Hydrogen is energy-dense.” Per kilogram: yes, the highest of any common fuel (∼120 MJ/kg versus ∼44 for gasoline, 2.7×). Per litre: no, because it is so light. Even at 700 bar (about 40 kg/m3): 40×120/1000=4.8 MJ/L versus gasoline 32 MJ/L, only 15%. Per litre at 1 atm (0.0899 kg/m3) it is ∼0.01 MJ/L. This is why storage is the main vehicle problem, yet it suits aircraft, where mass matters more than volume.

The text says “>95% of hydrogen is produced from steam reforming of natural gas”, while the pie gives SMR 49%. The two agree if you read the text loosely: fossil feedstocks together (49+29+17=95%) supply about 95%. Learn both: >95% and SMR 49%, oil 29%, coal 17%, electrolysis 3.9%, other 0.1%.

Steam methane reforming (SMR): a mass balance

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Step 1 (endothermic, steam reforming): CH4+H2O→CO+3H2.Step 2 (water-gas shift): CO+H2O→CO2+H2.Net: CH4+2H2O→CO2+4H2.
Masses: 16+36=52→44+8. So per tonne of H2: 2 t CH4, 4.5 t water, 5.5 t . The course diagram uses 1.1 t H2: CH4 2.2 t, water ≈4.9 t,  ≈6.0 t. These match the stoichiometry. With carbon capture (∼70% in the diagram) only about 1.5 t escapes. Disadvantages (course): natural gas is becoming scarce and costly, and the process releases .

Electrolysis: where the energy numbers come from

WHY THIS IS TRUE

Splitting one mole of liquid water, H2O→H2+12O2, needs ΔH=285.8 kJ in total, of which ΔG=237.1 kJ must be supplied as electrical work (the rest, TΔS=48.7 kJ, can come as heat). Electrical work per mole is nFE with n=2 electrons: E∘=ΔGnF=237,1002×96,485=1.23V,Ethermoneutral=ΔHnF=1.48V. Per kilogram: 285.8/2.016=141.8 MJ =39.4 kWh (the theoretical energy content, HHV). Real electrolysers need about 50–55 kWh/kg (∼70–75% efficient).

WORKED EXAMPLE

Mass balance of 2H2O→2H2+O2: 36→4+32, so 9 t water gives 1 t H2 and 8 t O2 (oxygen is a useful by-product). Reaction heat is why high-temperature electrolysis or thermochemical cycles can cut the electricity needed: heat supplies the TΔS share.

CHECK AGAINST YOUR COURSE NOTES

The course electrolysis diagram labels 1.1 t H2 with 8 t O2 and 9.1 t water. Total mass balances (1.1+8=9.1), but stoichiometry fixes O2:H2 at 8:1 by mass, so the labels are a rounded illustration. Reactions to memorise: anode 2H2O→O2+4H++4e−; cathode 4H++4e−→2H2; PEM electrolysis runs at 20–100∘C.

Thermochemical, solar-driven and biological routes

Thermochemical cycles use reusable chemicals (cerium oxide, copper chloride) so only water is consumed; direct thermal splitting of water needs impractically high temperatures, so the cycle splits the job into steps: a very hot step releases oxygen from the oxide, a cooler step lets the oxide take oxygen from steam, freeing H2. Solar-driven processes: photobiological (bacteria and green algae use photosynthetic activity), photoelectrochemical (specialised semiconductors split water directly; the semiconductor must deliver at least 1.23 V, so a band gap of roughly 1.6–2 eV in practice, which is why plain silicon at 1.12 eV is not enough alone), and solar thermochemical (concentrated solar heat with metal oxides). Biological routes use microbes breaking down biomass or wastewater.

Fuel cells, and a classic naming trap

DEFINITION

A fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy (like a battery, but fed continuously with fuel). Hydrogen goes to the anode, where it splits into protons and electrons. Electrons travel through the external circuit (current); protons cross the membrane; at the cathode they meet oxygen and electrons to form water. Outputs: electricity, heat, water.

FIXING A COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Anode is defined by oxidation (“An Ox”), not by sign. In the electrolyser the anode (water oxidised to O2) is the positive electrode; in the fuel cell the anode (H2 oxidised) is the negative electrode. The fuel cell is electrolysis run backwards.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Real cell voltage under load ≈0.7 V (ideal 1.23 V; losses from reaction kinetics, resistance, gas transport). Efficiency on the HHV basis =0.7/1.48=47%, versus roughly 20–25% for a petrol engine: about 2× (course: two to three times). A fuel cell is not limited by Carnot because it does not run on heat.

The solar–hydrogen cycle (“solar-hydrogen revolution”)

WORKED EXAMPLE

A 5 kW load for 12 night hours needs 60 kWh. Fuel cell at 50% (HHV) ⇒120 kWh of hydrogen energy =120/39.4=3.05 kg H2. Making it at 70% electrolyser efficiency takes 120/0.7=171 kWh of daytime solar electricity. Round trip =60/171=35%. Storage: at 350 bar (∼24 kg/m3) 3.05 kg takes 0.13 m3 (127 L), but at atmospheric pressure it would fill ∼34 m3. So a battery (∼90% round trip) wins for daily cycling, while hydrogen wins for long-duration or seasonal storage and heavy transport.

Hydrogen economy, uses, pros and cons

The hydrogen economy is the vision of hydrogen as a low- or zero-carbon energy carrier: made from diverse sources, stored, transported, and converted to heat and electricity (replacing natural gas for heating and petrol as transport fuel). Uses in the course: oil refining and fertilisers (ammonia, N2+3H2→2NH3) today; vehicles and buses; home fuel-cell units (about the size of a refrigerator, giving heat, hot water and electricity); Honda’s home unit that makes hydrogen from methane, used by many Japanese households; aviation (most energy per gram). Small fuel cells power laptops and phones; large ones supply grids, emergency power and off-grid sites.
Advantages: plentiful water feedstock, low environmental impact, renewable if made from renewables (solar-hydrogen), good oil substitute, easier to store than electricity, safer than gasoline and natural gas (course), efficient fuel cells now commercial.
Disadvantages: not found as H2, so energy must be spent to make it (net energy negative); hard to store in vehicle tanks; fuel cells are currently expensive.

EIA steps: screening (Schedule 1: always assess, e.g. thermal plants ≥300 MW; Schedule 2: if significant effects) → scoping → impact identification/prediction → assessment and mitigation → EIA report (Environmental Impact Statement) → decision-making → monitoring and evaluation.
