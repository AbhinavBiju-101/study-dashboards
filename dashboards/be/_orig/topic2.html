Part C — Electrical Machines Module 2

Machines convert energy between electrical and mechanical forms. Every derivation below leans on two things you already have: Fleming's left/right hand rules for force/EMF direction, and the RMS/phasor machinery from Part B for the transformer's alternating flux.

C1. DC Motor: Construction

Definition A DC motor has four essential parts: the yoke (outer magnetic frame), the field system (poles + field winding, produces the magnetic flux), the armature (rotating core + winding, carries current and experiences force), and the commutator + brushes (mechanically reverse the current in each armature coil every half-turn).

Cross section of a two-pole DC machine: N and S poles set up radial flux; conductors in the slots carry current axially.

The commutator here is doing the reverse job of the diode bridge you'd see in a rectifier — both take an alternating quantity and make it one-directional. The DC generator (same machine, opposite energy flow) uses exactly this commutator to turn the AC EMF from B1's rotating-coil picture into DC.

C2. DC Motor: Working Principle & Back EMF

Definition — force on a current-carrying conductor
F = BIL sinθ  (N)
where B is flux density (T), I is conductor current (A), L is conductor length in the field (m), θ the angle between conductor and field (usually 90°, so F=BIL). Direction: Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule: thuMb = Motion/force, First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current — all mutually perpendicular.

Why the motor also generates a back EMF The instant the armature conductor starts moving (because of the force above), it is itself a conductor moving through a magnetic field — which is precisely the generator condition of B1/C1. By Lenz's law this induced EMF must oppose the very current that created the motion, so it is called back EMF, Eb, and it grows with speed N and flux Φ (Eb ∝ ΦN). The armature circuit equation is:
V = Eb + IaRa
This is nothing but Ohm's law / KVL from Part A applied to the armature loop: supply voltage = back EMF (a voltage source opposing V) + the IaRa drop across armature resistance.
Fixing a common misconception "Back EMF wastes energy" — no. Back EMF is exactly how the motor converts electrical power into mechanical power: the electrical power associated with it, EbIa, is the mechanical power developed (minus small losses). Only the Ia²Ra term is a genuine (heat) loss.
Worked example A DC motor has V=220 V, Ra=1 Ω, and draws Ia=20 A. Find Eb and the mechanical power developed.
Eb = V − IaRa = 220 − 20(1) = 200 V
Mechanical power = EbIa = 200×20 = 4000 W = 4 kW
Input power = VIa = 220×20 = 4400 W. Armature copper loss = Ia²Ra = 400 W. Check: 4000+400=4400 W ✓.

C3. Types of DC Motors

Three ways to connect the field winding relative to the armature — each gives a different speed/torque personality.

TypeKey relationSpeed vs LoadTypical application
ShuntIa=I−Ish, Ish=V/Rshnearly constant speedfans, blowers, lathes
SeriesIa=Ise=I, V=Eb+Ia(Ra+Rse)high starting torque, speed falls sharply as load risescranes, traction, hair dryers
Compoundcombines both windingscompromise of the twopresses, rolling mills, elevators
Fixing a common misconception "A series motor is just a shunt motor wired differently, so it behaves similarly" — no: a series motor must never be started with no mechanical load. With Φ nearly zero at no load, Eb=V−IaRa stays small, so Ia (and hence speed, since low flux needs high speed to build back-EMF) can run away dangerously. A shunt motor's flux is fixed by V regardless of load, so it has no such runaway risk.

C4. Transformer: Construction

Definition A transformer has a laminated silicon-steel core (thin, insulated sheets — reduces eddy-current loss) carrying two windings, primary and secondary, linked by the same magnetic flux but with no electrical connection between them. Core type: windings surround two limbs of a rectangular core. Shell type: both windings sit on the central limb of a three-limb core.

Core-type construction: mutual flux Φ, set up by the primary's current, links the secondary winding on the opposite limb.

C5. Transformer: EMF Equation

Derivation Assume a sinusoidal mutual flux Φ = Φm sin(ωt). By Faraday's law, the EMF induced in a winding of N turns is:
e = −N dΦ/dt = −NΦmω cos(ωt)
The magnitude of this EMF peaks at Em = NΦmω = NΦm(2πf). Just as in B2, the RMS value of a sinusoid is its peak divided by √2:
E = Em/√2 = 2πfΦmN/√2 = 4.44 f Φm N
Applying this to both windings: E1=4.44fΦmN1, E2=4.44fΦmN2. Since the same Φm and f appear in both, dividing gives the turns-ratio rule:
E1/E2 = N1/N2
The "4.44" is nothing mysterious — it's 2π/√2 (≈4.44), the exact same √2 that converted peak to RMS back in B2. Anywhere you see 4.44 in a machines formula, a sinusoidal-flux EMF derivation like this one is hiding behind it.
Worked example A transformer has N1=1000 turns, N2=250 turns, is supplied at 50 Hz, and the core flux is Φm=0.02 Wb. Find E1, E2, and state whether it's step-up or step-down.
E1 = 4.44×50×0.02×1000 = 4440 V
E2 = 4.44×50×0.02×250 = 1110 V
Check via turns ratio: E1/E2 = 1000/250 = 4, and 4440/1110 = 4 ✓. Since N2<N1 (E2<E1), this is a step-down transformer.

C6. Three-Phase Induction Motor

Definition The stator carries three windings spaced 120° apart in space, fed by three currents 120° apart in time (a balanced 3-phase supply). Their combined magnetomotive force produces a magnetic field of constant magnitude that rotates at synchronous speed:
Ns = 120f/P  (rpm),   P = number of poles
This rotating field cuts the stationary rotor bars, inducing currents in them (transformer action — no electrical connection needed, hence "induction" motor); those currents, in the rotating field, experience a force (Fleming's left-hand rule again) that drags the rotor around.

Snapshots of the resultant stator flux vector at three instants — same magnitude, rotated by 120° each 1/3 of a cycle. This is the "rotating field" produced purely by three fixed windings and time-varying currents.

Slip The rotor can never spin at exactly Ns (if it did, the field would no longer cut the rotor bars, no EMF would be induced, and no torque would be produced). The fractional difference is called slip:
s = (Ns−Nr)/Ns
FeatureSquirrel-cage rotorWound (slip-ring) rotor
Rotor windingbars short-circuited by end rings3-phase winding, brought out to slip rings
External resistancenot possiblepossible, via brushes — boosts starting torque
Cost / maintenancelowhigher
Typical usefans, pumps, machine tools (~75% of industrial motors)cranes, hoists, compressors (high starting torque needed)
Worked example A 4-pole, 3-phase induction motor is supplied at 50 Hz and runs at 1440 rpm. Find the synchronous speed and the slip.
Ns = 120f/P = 120×50/4 = 1500 rpm
s = (1500−1440)/1500 = 60/1500 = 0.04 = 4%
This is exactly the same "difference from a reference" idea as Rth in A7 or φ in B4 — a small deviation from an idealised reference value that governs everything else about the system's behaviour.

Self-Test Checklist

Can you do each of these right now, without looking back? If not, that's exactly the section to re-read.

  • State Ohm's law and use it to find power two independent ways for the same resistor.
  • Write KCL at a node and KVL around a loop for a circuit you sketch yourself.
  • Derive the current-divider formula from KCL + Ohm's law (don't just quote it).
  • Solve a 2-node circuit by node-voltage analysis, unaided.
  • Solve a 2-mesh circuit by mesh-current analysis, and recover the actual branch current in the shared resistor.
  • Transform a voltage source + series R into its current-source equivalent, and explain why they're indistinguishable from outside.
  • Find Vth and Rth for a simple network and state Norton's equivalent from it.
  • Derive, from calculus, why RL=Rth maximises load power — and explain why this is not the same as maximum efficiency.
  • Use superposition to solve for one unknown in a two-source circuit, and check it against a direct method.
  • Derive Vrms=Vm/√2 and Vavg=2Vm/π from the defining integrals.
  • Explain in one sentence why phasors turn AC differential equations into algebra.
  • Given R and XL for a series RL circuit, find |Z|, φ, and I — and draw the impedance triangle.
  • Compute P, Q, S for an AC circuit and verify S²=P²+Q².
  • Explain why a low power factor costs money even though Q isn't dissipated as heat.
  • Sketch a DC motor cross-section and apply Fleming's left-hand rule to find the force direction.
  • Write V=Eb+IaRa and use it to find mechanical power developed.
  • State the difference between shunt, series and compound DC motors, and why a series motor must never run unloaded.
  • Derive the transformer EMF equation E=4.44fΦmN from Faraday's law.
  • Compute synchronous speed and slip for a 3-phase induction motor given pole count, frequency and rotor speed.
  • Explain, in your own words, why the induction motor needs a nonzero slip to produce any torque at all.
