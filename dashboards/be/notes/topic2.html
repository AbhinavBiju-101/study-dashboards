Part C — Electrical Machines Module 2

Machines convert energy between electrical and mechanical forms. Every derivation below leans on two things you already have: Fleming's left/right hand rules for force/EMF direction, and the RMS/phasor machinery from Part B for the transformer's alternating flux.

C1. DC Motor: Construction

Key terms — know these words
  • Yoke — the outer frame (cast/rolled steel): supports the poles, protects the machine and carries the magnetic flux between poles.
  • Field system — the stationary part that produces the main flux: an even number of pole cores bolted to the yoke, with field winding on each.
  • Salient pole — a pole that projects inward towards the armature.
  • Pole core / pole shoe — the core carries the field coil; the curved shoe spreads the flux over the armature, supports the coil and lowers reluctance.
  • Field winding — the coils on the poles that, when energised, create alternate N and S poles.
  • Armature — the rotating part: core + winding; it carries the current that interacts with the field to make torque.
  • Armature core — slotted cylinder of insulated soft-steel laminations mounted on the shaft.
  • Armature winding — insulated conductors placed in the slots and suitably interconnected — lap winding or wave winding.
  • Commutator — a mechanical rectifier/inverter of wedge-shaped copper segments insulated by mica; connects the rotating armature to the fixed supply.
  • Brushes / brush holder — carbon blocks pressed on the commutator by springs; they carry current between the supply and the armature.
  • Air gap — the small gap between pole shoe and armature across which the flux passes.
  • Lamination — thin insulated steel sheet; stacking them breaks up the paths for eddy currents.
  • Eddy current — circulating currents induced inside the iron by changing flux; they only waste heat, so they are minimised.
  • Spider — a spoked frame on which laminations are mounted in large machines instead of being keyed directly to the shaft.
Definition A DC motor has four essential parts: the yoke (outer magnetic frame), the field system (poles + field winding, produces the magnetic flux), the armature (rotating core + winding, carries current and experiences force), and the commutator + brushes (mechanically reverse the current in each armature coil every half-turn).

Cross-section of a two-pole DC machine: the yoke carries flux between the N and S poles; conductors in the armature slots carry current straight along the shaft, in opposite directions under the two poles.

The commutator here is doing the reverse job of the diode bridge you'd see in a rectifier — both take an alternating quantity and make it one-directional. The DC generator (same machine, opposite energy flow) uses exactly this commutator to turn the AC EMF from B1's rotating-coil picture into DC.

Why laminate the iron? Iron is a conductor. In the rotating armature core (and in AC-fed cores like transformers), changing flux induces circulating eddy currents inside the solid iron, wasting energy as heat. Slicing the core into thin, mutually insulated sheets breaks the circulating loops into tiny ones, so the currents (and the loss) fall drastically. The sheets are cut along the direction of flux so magnetic performance is unaffected.

Fixing a common misconception The commutator does not make DC current alternate 'because it's broken' — it is deliberate. Inside the armature the current must alternate (so each conductor keeps pushing the same way), while the supply is DC. The commutator is the bridge that lets a DC supply feed an alternating-current armature. That is why the slides call it a 'mechanical rectifier' — in a generator it does the opposite job: it turns the armature's AC into DC for the load.

C2. DC Motor: Working Principle & Back EMF

Key terms — know these words
  • Torque (T) — the turning effort of the shaft; T ∝ Φ·Ia.
  • Fleming's left-hand rule — for MOTORS: thuMb = Motion (force), First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current.
  • Fleming's right-hand rule — for GENERATORS: thuMb = Motion, First finger = Field, seCond finger = induced EMF/current.
  • Back EMF (counter EMF), Eb — the EMF induced in the armature of a running motor; it opposes the supply voltage.
  • Armature current (Ia) and resistance (Ra) — the current in, and resistance of, the armature winding.
  • Commutation — the reversal of current in an armature coil as it passes from under one pole to the next.
  • Motoring action — electrical input → mechanical output (supply pushes current against Eb).
  • Generating action — mechanical input → electrical output (E drives current out to the load).
  • Starter — a series resistor (or electronic controller) that limits the very large starting current.
  • Speed equation — N ∝ Eb/Φ — speed rises with back-EMF and falls with flux.
Definition — force on a current-carrying conductor
F = BIL sinθ  (N)
where B is flux density (T), I is conductor current (A), L is conductor length in the field (m), θ the angle between conductor and field (usually 90°, so F=BIL). Direction: Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motors): thuMb = Motion/force, First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current.

Why the motor also generates a back EMF The instant the armature conductor starts moving (because of the force above), it is itself a conductor moving through a magnetic field — which is precisely the generator condition of B1/C1. By Lenz's law this induced EMF must oppose the very current that created the motion, so it is called back EMF, Eb, and it grows with speed N and flux Φ (Eb ∝ ΦN). The armature circuit equation is:
V = Eb + IaRa
This is nothing but Ohm's law / KVL from Part A applied to the armature loop: supply voltage = back EMF (a voltage source opposing V) + the IaRa drop across armature resistance.
Fixing a common misconception "Back EMF wastes energy" — no. Back EMF is exactly how the motor converts electrical power into mechanical power: the electrical power associated with it, EbIa, is the mechanical power developed (minus small losses). Only the Ia²Ra term is a genuine (heat) loss.
Worked example A DC motor has V=220 V, Ra=1 Ω, and draws Ia=20 A. Find Eb and the mechanical power developed.
Eb = V − IaRa = 220 − 20(1) = 200 V
Mechanical power = EbIa = 200×20 = 4000 W = 4 kW
Input power = VIa = 220×20 = 4400 W. Armature copper loss = Ia²Ra = 400 W. Check: 4000+400=4400 W ✓.

Working principle, step by step

  1. Connect the DC supply: the field winding is energised and creates alternate N and S poles.
  2. The armature conductors carry current — in one direction under the N pole, in the opposite direction under the S pole.
  3. Each current-carrying conductor sits in a magnetic field, so a force F = BIL acts on it (Fleming's left-hand rule). For the layout shown in the slides, this tends to move each conductor anticlockwise.
  4. The forces on all conductors add to give a driving torque that turns the armature.
  5. As a conductor moves from under one pole to the next, the commutator reverses its current — and the new pole is of opposite polarity, so the force direction stays the same.
  6. Result: continuous rotation in one direction.

Torque and speed relations

T ∝ Φ · Ia   |   Eb ∝ Φ · N   |   N ∝ Eb/Φ = (V − IaRa)/Φ

Weaken the flux → the motor speeds up; raise the supply voltage → the motor speeds up. These two 'knobs' are how DC-motor speed is controlled.

Why a DC motor needs a starter At the instant of switching on, the armature is at rest, so N = 0 and Eb = 0. The armature circuit equation V = Eb + IaRa then gives Ia = V/Ra. Since Ra is tiny (a fraction of an ohm), that current is enormous. E.g. 220 V and Ra = 0.5 Ω gives 440 A versus a normal 20 A. A starter resistor limits it and is cut out as Eb builds up.
Worked example — speed change with load Motor: V = 220 V, Ra = 0.5 Ω, Ia = 20 A, N = 1000 rpm, flux constant.
Eb1 = 220 − 20 × 0.5 = 210 V.
Load is reduced and Ia falls to 10 A: Eb2 = 220 − 10 × 0.5 = 215 V.
Since N ∝ Eb (Φ constant): N2 = 1000 × 215/210 = 1024 rpm. Speed rises slightly at light load — this small change is what 'good speed regulation' means.

The same machine as a generator (DC generator principle)

Run the machine backwards — spin the armature with a prime mover and it produces an EMF. Consider a single-turn loop ABCD rotating clockwise at constant speed in a uniform field. The flux linked with coil sides AB and CD changes continuously, inducing an EMF in each side; the two EMFs add.

The EMF in the loop is therefore alternating. A commutator (one split ring for one loop) flips the connections to the brushes every half-turn, so the external load always sees the same polarity — pulsating DC. With many coils and many commutator segments, the ripples smooth out to nearly steady DC.

A single-loop generator: the loop's EMF alternates every half-turn; the commutator swaps the brush connections at each zero crossing so the load sees a one-directional, pulsating output.

A generator's generated EMF and a motor's back EMF are the same physical thing — a conductor cutting flux. Every machine does both at once; what decides whether it is a motor or a generator is which way the energy flows.

C3. Types of DC Motors

Key terms — know these words
  • Separately excited — field winding fed from an independent DC source.
  • Self-excited — field winding fed from the machine's own armature supply (shunt, series, compound).
  • Shunt motor — field winding (high resistance, many turns) in parallel with the armature.
  • Series motor — field winding (low resistance, few heavy turns) in series with the armature.
  • Compound motor — both shunt and series field windings.
  • Short shunt / long shunt — short: shunt field across the armature only, series field in the supply line. Long: shunt field across armature + series field together.
  • Cumulative / differential compound — cumulative: series field aids the shunt flux. Differential: it opposes it.
  • Starting torque — the torque at zero speed; high for series motors.
  • Speed regulation — how little the speed changes from no-load to full-load; excellent for shunt motors.
  • Over-compounded generator — a cumulative compound generator whose terminal voltage rises with load, compensating line drop.

Three ways to connect the field winding: the coil symbol is the field, the circle marked M is the armature. Each connection gives a different speed/torque personality.

Fixing a common misconception "A series motor is just a shunt motor wired differently, so it behaves similarly" — no: a series motor must never be started with no mechanical load. With Φ nearly zero at no load, Eb=V−IaRa stays small, so Ia (and hence speed, since low flux needs high speed to build back-EMF) can run away dangerously. A shunt motor's flux is fixed by V regardless of load, so it has no such runaway risk.

C4. Transformer: Construction

Key terms — know these words
  • Primary / secondary winding — the winding connected to the source / the winding connected to the load.
  • Core — the laminated silicon-steel path that carries the mutual flux.
  • Limb / yoke (transformer) — limb: the part of the core carrying a winding; yoke: the part that joins the limbs.
  • Mutual inductance — the property by which a changing current in one coil induces an EMF in a neighbouring coil.
  • Turns ratio — N1/N2, the number of primary turns to secondary turns.
  • Step-up / step-down — step-up: V2 > V1 (N2 > N1). Step-down: V2 < V1.
  • Core-type / shell-type — windings around the limbs of a two-limb core / windings on the centre limb of a three-limb core.
  • Cylindrical (concentric) winding — LV coil next to the core, HV coil concentric over it — used in core-type.
  • Sandwich (disc) winding — LV and HV sections stacked alternately — used in shell-type.
  • Leakage flux — flux that links only one winding; it is minimised by winding layout.
  • Hysteresis / eddy-current loss — core losses; silicon steel cuts the first, lamination cuts the second.
  • Excitation (magnetising) current — the small no-load primary current needed to set up the flux.
Definition A transformer has a laminated silicon-steel core (thin, insulated sheets — reduces eddy-current loss) carrying two windings, primary and secondary, linked by the same magnetic flux but with no electrical connection between them. Core type: windings surround two limbs of a rectangular core. Shell type: both windings sit on the central limb of a three-limb core.

Core-type construction: the alternating flux Φ set up by the primary circulates round the core and links the secondary winding on the opposite limb.

Working principle (mutual induction)

  1. An alternating voltage V1 is applied to the primary; a small alternating current flows and sets up an alternating flux Φ in the core.
  2. This flux links both windings, so by Faraday's law it induces EMF E1 in the primary and E2 in the secondary.
  3. The EMFs are proportional to the numbers of turns: E2/E1 = N2/N1.
  4. Connect a load across the secondary and a current I2 flows; power passes from the primary circuit to the secondary circuit magnetically, with no electrical connection and no change in frequency.
Ideal-transformer relations
V1/V2 = N1/N2 = I2/I1   (V1I1 = V2I2)
A transformer that steps voltage up steps current down by the same ratio; power in = power out (for an ideal one). If N1 > N2 it is step-down; if N2 > N1 it is step-up.

Core-type vs shell-type

Core typeShell type
Magnetic circuittwo vertical limbs + two horizontal yokes (a single rectangular path)three limbs; the two outer limbs give low-reluctance return paths for the central flux
Windingshalf of each winding on each limb (reduces leakage flux); LV next to the core, HV over it — cylindrical (concentric) coilsboth windings on the central limb, split into LV and HV sections stacked alternately — sandwich (disc) coils
Why put LV next to the core? The low-voltage winding needs less insulation from the grounded core than the high-voltage one. Putting LV closest to the core and wrapping HV over it means only the LV coil needs to be insulated from the core, and the HV winding is insulated by the LV winding and its own insulation — saving space and cost.
Worked example — ideal transformer currents A 2200 V / 220 V, 10 kVA transformer. Turns ratio = 2200/220 = 10 (step-down). Rated primary current = 10 000/2200 = 4.55 A; rated secondary current = 10 000/220 = 45.5 A — ten times larger, matching I2/I1 = N1/N2 = 10.

Losses and applications

  • Core (iron) loss: hysteresis (reduced by silicon steel) + eddy current (reduced by laminations); roughly constant regardless of load.
  • Copper loss: I²R in the windings; grows with load.
  • Uses: stepping voltage up/down for power transmission and distribution; supplying suitable voltages to electronic devices (TV etc.) and low-voltage electronic circuits; stepping up voltage in home inverter systems.
Fixing a common misconception A transformer does not work on DC. With a steady primary current the flux is constant, dΦ/dt = 0, and no EMF is induced in the secondary. Worse, the winding resistance alone limits DC current, which can burn the primary out. Only changing flux induces voltage.

C5. Transformer: EMF Equation

Key terms — know these words
  • Flux (Φ, Φm) — magnetic flux in webers (Wb); Φm is its peak value.
  • Faraday's law — induced EMF = rate of change of flux linkage: e = −N dΦ/dt.
  • RMS EMF (E) — E = 4.44 f Φm N — the effective value of the induced EMF.
  • Form factor — 1.11 for a sine wave; note 4 × 1.11 = 4.44, the constant in the EMF equation.
  • Phase of EMF — e lags the flux by 90°.
  • EMF per turn — E/N = 4.44 f Φm — the same in both windings.
Derivation Assume a sinusoidal mutual flux Φ = Φm sin(ωt). By Faraday's law, the EMF induced in a winding of N turns is:
e = −N dΦ/dt = −NΦmω cos(ωt)
The magnitude of this EMF peaks at Em = NΦmω = NΦm(2πf). Just as in B2, the RMS value of a sinusoid is its peak divided by √2:
E = Em/√2 = 2πfΦmN/√2 = 4.44 f Φm N
Applying this to both windings: E1=4.44fΦmN1, E2=4.44fΦmN2. Since the same Φm and f appear in both, dividing gives the turns-ratio rule:
E1/E2 = N1/N2
The "4.44" is nothing mysterious — it's 2π/√2 (≈4.44), the exact same √2 that converted peak to RMS back in B2. Anywhere you see 4.44 in a machines formula, a sinusoidal-flux EMF derivation like this one is hiding behind it.
Worked example A transformer has N1=1000 turns, N2=250 turns, is supplied at 50 Hz, and the core flux is Φm=0.02 Wb. Find E1, E2, and state whether it's step-up or step-down.
E1 = 4.44×50×0.02×1000 = 4440 V
E2 = 4.44×50×0.02×250 = 1110 V
Check via turns ratio: E1/E2 = 1000/250 = 4, and 4440/1110 = 4 ✓. Since N2<N1 (E2<E1), this is a step-down transformer.
Where does 4.44 come from — a second way For a sinusoidal flux the average EMF over a quarter cycle is 4 f Φm N (flux swings from 0 to Φm in T/4 = 1/(4f)). The RMS value = form factor × average = 1.11 × 4fΦmN = 4.44 fΦmN. It agrees with the derivation in the box above (2π/√2 = 4.44), and it shows why the form factor from the AC-circuits topic matters here.
Phase relations Because e = −N dΦ/dt and Φ = Φm sin ωt, the EMF is −NΦmω cos ωt = NΦmω sin(ωt − 90°). So the induced EMF lags the flux by 90°, and E1 and E2 are in phase with each other (the same flux induces both). The primary draws a small magnetising current in phase with the flux.
Worked example — find the turns A 220 V, 50 Hz primary; peak core flux Φm = 0.01 Wb. Neglect winding drop so E1 ≈ 220 V.
N1 = E1/(4.44 f Φm) = 220/(4.44 × 50 × 0.01) = 220/2.22 ≈ 99 ≈ 100 turns.
For a 22 V secondary: N2 = N1 × 22/220 = 10 turns.
What happens at a lower frequency? For a given voltage, Φm = E/(4.44 f N). If the frequency drops (say a 50 Hz transformer on a 25 Hz supply), Φm would double, pushing the core into saturation and causing very large magnetising current. That is why transformers are rated for a specific frequency, and why a 50 Hz design can't be freely used at lower frequency.

C6. Three-Phase Induction Motor

Key terms — know these words
  • Stator — the stationary part: steel frame + slotted silicon-steel core + three-phase winding.
  • Rotor — the rotating part, either squirrel-cage or wound.
  • Rotating magnetic field (RMF) — a field of constant magnitude that rotates in space, produced by a balanced three-phase supply.
  • Synchronous speed (Ns) — the speed of the RMF, Ns = 120f/P rpm.
  • Slip (s) — s = (Ns − Nr)/Ns; typically 2–5% at full load.
  • Poles (P) — number of magnetic poles wound into the stator; more poles = slower speed.
  • Squirrel-cage rotor — rotor bars (copper/aluminium) short-circuited by end rings.
  • End rings — the rings that short-circuit the rotor bars at both ends.
  • Slip rings — insulated rings on the shaft of a wound rotor that connect the rotor winding to external resistors through brushes.
  • Wound (slip-ring) rotor — rotor with a three-phase winding (star-connected) brought out to slip rings.
  • Induction (asynchronous) motor — a motor whose rotor current is induced by the stator field, and which never quite reaches synchronous speed.
  • Inrush (starting) current — the very large current drawn when the motor is first switched on, often 5–7× rated.
Definition The stator carries three windings spaced 120° apart in space, fed by three currents 120° apart in time (a balanced 3-phase supply). Their combined magnetomotive force produces a magnetic field of constant magnitude that rotates at synchronous speed:
Ns = 120f/P  (rpm),   P = number of poles
This rotating field cuts the stationary rotor bars, inducing currents in them (transformer action — no electrical connection needed, hence "induction" motor); those currents, in the rotating field, experience a force (Fleming's left-hand rule again) that drags the rotor around.

Stator cross-section at three instants. Each winding's axis is drawn dashed (A, B, C, 120° apart). As each phase current peaks in turn, the resultant field (arrow) jumps from A to B to C — a rotation.

Slip The rotor can never spin at exactly Ns (if it did, the field would no longer cut the rotor bars, no EMF would be induced, and no torque would be produced). The fractional difference is called slip:
s = (Ns−Nr)/Ns

Worked example A 4-pole, 3-phase induction motor is supplied at 50 Hz and runs at 1440 rpm. Find the synchronous speed and the slip.
Ns = 120f/P = 120×50/4 = 1500 rpm
s = (1500−1440)/1500 = 60/1500 = 0.04 = 4%
This is exactly the same "difference from a reference" idea as Rth in A7 or φ in B4 — a small deviation from an idealised reference value that governs everything else about the system's behaviour.

Stator construction

  • A cylindrical frame of fabricated steel encloses a hollow core of thin silicon-steel laminations (silicon steel and lamination together reduce hysteresis and eddy-current loss).
  • Evenly spaced slots on the inner surface hold the stator winding.
  • Three windings, connected in star or delta, form a balanced three-phase winding, wound for a definite number of poles.
  • More poles means lower speed (Ns = 120f/P): 2 poles → 3000 rpm, 4 → 1500, 6 → 1000, 8 → 750 (at 50 Hz).

Why the field rotates

The three winding currents are 120° apart in time. Watch what they do at a few instants (Im = peak current):

ωtiAiBiCWhich winding dominates?
0°0−0.87 Im+0.87 ImB and C (opposite signs)

210°−0.5 Im+Im−0.5 ImB
330°−0.5 Im−0.5 Im+ImC

The 'strongest' winding hops A → B → C in turn, so the resultant magnetic axis sweeps around the stator. Adding the three fluxes vectorially gives a resultant of constant magnitude 1.5 Φm that rotates at Ns.

Three balanced currents 120° apart. Each phase peaks in turn (A, then B, then C), and at any instant the three sum to zero.

How the rotor is driven

  1. The rotating field sweeps past the stationary rotor conductors and induces EMF in them (Faraday's law).
  2. The rotor circuit is closed (short-circuited bars, or external resistors for a wound rotor), so rotor currents flow.
  3. These currents lie in the stator's magnetic field, so each experiences a force (F = BIL) — together giving torque in the direction of the field's rotation (Lenz's law: the rotor 'chases' the change).
  4. The rotor speeds up but can never catch the field: at Nr = Ns there would be no relative motion, no induced EMF, no current and no torque. Hence slip.
Nr = Ns(1 − s)   |   rotor-current frequency fr = s·f
Worked example — 6-pole motor 50 Hz supply, 6 poles: Ns = 120 × 50/6 = 1000 rpm. At 3% slip, Nr = 1000 × 0.97 = 970 rpm, and the rotor current frequency = 0.03 × 50 = 1.5 Hz. At start (s = 1) the rotor frequency equals the supply frequency, 50 Hz.
Fixing a common misconception 'Induction motors run at synchronous speed' — no, that is a synchronous motor. An induction motor is called asynchronous because it always runs a few percent slower. Also, to reverse an induction motor you swap any two of the three supply leads — that reverses the direction the field rotates.

Why external resistance helps at start Adding resistance to the rotor circuit at start (a) reduces the starting current and (b) improves the rotor power factor, which together raise the starting torque — a rare case where adding resistance gives more torque. Once at speed the resistance is removed (the brushes are short-circuited) so the motor runs efficiently like a squirrel-cage machine.
