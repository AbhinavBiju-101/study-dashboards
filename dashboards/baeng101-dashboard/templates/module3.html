11Analysing Definitions

A definition is not "what the dictionary says". It is a precision tool, and, like any tool, the type must fit the job: purpose, audience and context decide which type you use. Using the wrong type in a research paper is like using a spoon to drive a nail.

1.1 What a definition does

DefinitionA definition explains the meaning, scope and boundaries of a term so that writer and reader share exactly the same understanding.
Why it mattersIt removes ambiguity (one term, one meaning), improves precision (exact communication), builds a foundation for technical and academic texts, and creates common ground between readers of different backgrounds. If two readers picture different things by "efficient", every later claim splits in two.
Misconception"A dictionary definition is always enough." A dictionary describes general usage; a research paper often needs a definition built for one study (stipulative or operational). See 1.6.

The next five subsections take each type in turn: what it is, how to write it, and the trap that costs marks.

1.3 Formal definition: term + class + distinguishing features

DefinitionA formal definition places the term in a broader class, then states the distinguishing features that separate it from other members of that class. Slide example: A diode is an electronic component that allows current to flow in one direction only.
Why the class comes firstFeatures alone never single anything out: "changes with temperature" also fits a thermometer and a metal rod. The class shrinks the field to resistors; the features then pick one member. Two filters, each cutting the field down.
How to write one
  1. Choose the smallest class that is still true ("electronic component", not "thing").
  2. Add the feature(s) that exclude the class's other members.
  3. Do not reuse the term in the definition, and avoid "is when / is where".
Check: cover the term; could a reader name it from class + features alone?
Common mistakeAn over-broad class ("A diode is a device that…") or missing features ("A diode is an electronic component") each lose the mark for that part.

1.4 Informal definition: plain words for a non-technical reader

DefinitionAn informal definition uses conversational language and drops the rigid three-part structure. Slide example: A diode is basically a device that controls which way electricity can flow through it.

Use it for first introductions, internal documentation, or audiences without a technical background. Write it in one sentence, with everyday words, an analogy if it helps, and none of the technical terms you are explaining.

Misconception"Informal means inaccurate." It must still be true; it is simply simpler. What it lacks is the "term is a class that features" frame, and that absence is exactly what the exam checks.

1.5 Operational definition: how you would measure it

DefinitionAn operational definition defines a concept by the procedure, measurement or test used to identify or quantify it, ending with the threshold that has to be reached. Examples: A plot is saline when its electrical conductivity exceeds 4 dS/m. · Stress was measured with the PSS-10 scale; scores above 20 indicate high stress.
SlotAskIn "saline"
1 What is measuredwhich quantity or scale?electrical conductivity
2 Howinstrument or procedure?measured on the plot's soil
3 Thresholdwhat value counts?above 4 dS/m
Why it works"Students who are stressed performed poorly" cannot be tested; nobody knows what "stressed" means. An operational definition turns a fuzzy idea into a repeatable rule: two researchers applying it to the same person must reach the same classification.
Misconception + a neighbourAn operational definition does not say what a thing is; it says how you decide it is present. Do not confuse it with a descriptive definition, which lists observable features as they naturally appear ("a tree is a perennial plant with a woody stem, branches and leaves") without prescribing or measuring.

1.6 Negative definition: what it is not

DefinitionA negative definition clarifies meaning by stating what something is not, narrowing scope and removing a misconception. Slide example: A tablet is not a desktop computer because it lacks permanent peripheral devices and relies on touch interfaces.
Why it is rarely used aloneIt excludes but does not include: "not a desktop computer" still fits a chair, a cloud and a kettle. So it always sits beside a formal definition, or serves as one method of expansion (Machine learning is not just programming rules; it learns from data).
Try itTurn "A firewall filters network traffic" into a negative definition: A firewall is not an antivirus program; it controls which connections may pass, rather than removing malicious files. Note the contrast is with a likely confusion, not a random non-member.

1.7 Extended definition and the eight methods of expansion

DefinitionAn extended definition is a formal definition developed across a paragraph (or more) with two to four expansion methods. Choose it when the concept is central, complex or contested, or the readers lack background. Each method answers a different question.
The eight methods, with a thermistor as the running example
MethodAnswersGiveawayThermistor example
FunctionWhat is it for?"used to", "allows"It lets a circuit sense temperature.
StructureWhat parts?"consists of"A ceramic bead with two wire leads.
ProcessHow does it work?"first… then…"As heat rises, resistance falls, so current rises.
AnalogyWhat is it like?"like", "just as"Like a tap that opens wider when warmed.
ExampleWhich instances?"such as"Found in car engines and thermostats.
HistoryWhere did it start?dates, namesDeveloped in the 1940s for early electronics.
ComparisonHow does it differ from a neighbour?"unlike", "whereas"Unlike a fixed resistor, its value drifts.
EtymologyWhere does the word come from?rootsFrom "thermal" + "resistor".

Other expansion techniques in the course slides: negation, classification, cause and effect, principle of operation, visual aids.

Misconception"Extended just means longer." Length is a side effect; the marks are for distinct, named methods that each add something new.

1.8 Worked example: build and label an extended definition

Formal startA thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes sharply with temperature.
Extended, three methods, each labelled in bracketsA thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes sharply with temperature. (Function) It is used to let a circuit measure or control temperature. (Process) As the bead warms, its resistance falls, so more current flows and the circuit reads the change. (Comparison) Unlike an ordinary resistor, whose value is meant to stay fixed, a thermistor is valued precisely because its resistance drifts.

Checks: formal sentence first ✔ · 3 methods (within 2–4) ✔ · label at the point of use ✔ · no circular wording ✔ · flows without repeating the same angle ✔

1.9 Exam tips: scoring the definition question

  1. Match the named type. If asked for operational, name what is measured and the threshold; if formal, term + class + features.
  2. Open extended answers with a formal definition, then add the exact number of methods requested, each named in brackets.
  3. Use precise academic wording: no circularity, no vague "thing" or "stuff".
  4. Typical tasks: write a definition of a named type; identify and label the methods in a paragraph; convert a simple definition into an extended one; compare types.

22Reading for Inference

Every inference question asks what a passage commits to without saying. Five tools cover all of them: surface vs deep reading, hedging, limitations, challenging the data, and the four traps. An inference is never a guess: you must be able to quote the words that force it.

2.1 Surface reading vs deep reading

DefinitionAn inference is a reasoned conclusion drawn from evidence in the text. Surface reading understands the words; deep reading understands what the text implies, assumes and carefully avoids saying.
Four things you can say about a text
LevelTest
StatedIt is on the page; you can underline it
InferredNot written, but the passage makes it necessary; you can quote the forcing words
AssumedYou brought it; the passage never said it
SpeculatedFits, but nothing in the passage supports it
ExampleA paper says "Sample size: n = 12." Surface: twelve participants were studied. Deep: this is a tiny sample, so the findings may not generalise beyond it. Forcing words: "n = 12".
Misconception"If I can summarise it, I understood it." That is surface reading. In scientific texts the gaps between the lines are often more revealing than the lines.

2.2 How to draw inferences: four moves

MoveLook forWhat it tells you
Hunt hedging"may", "suggest", "appears to indicate", "further research is needed"The authors are unsure and are being honest about it
Read limitationsWhat the study admits it could not controlEach one blocks a conclusion; name both
Challenge the dataDoes the evidence support the claim? Measured or self-reported?Measured evidence beats self-reported belief
Bring your contextWhat you already knowHelps you interpret or question the finding
Why hedging is honest, not weakA claim can only be as wide as its data. One site cannot rule out that other sites differ, so a careful author narrows the verb to match. Hedging is a limitation written at sentence level.
Why measured beats self-reportedAn instrument records what happened; a self-report records what people believe happened, and beliefs are shaped by memory, mood and what they hope to see.

2.3 The four traps when reading research

The dashed link is what the data show; the solid arrows are what really happens.

TrapLooks likeDefence
Correlation as cause"Taps caused the drop" from a before/after or associationAsk what else changed; look for a control group
Cherry-pickingOnly the flattering result is quotedRead the full results
Jargon hiding a weak claimDense terms, thin evidenceRewrite it in plain words
Overgeneralisation"This proves…", "all"Match claim width to sample

2.4 Worked passage: apply all five tools

Worked passage (original) "Over five weeks, eight of sixteen hostel rooms received low-flow taps; the other eight kept their old taps. Water use per room fell 18% in the fitted rooms. Students said they were 'more careful' with water, though showers were not metered. These results suggest low-flow taps may cut consumption. Only one hostel block was studied."
  • Surface: Fitted rooms used 18% less water.
  • Deep: The drop is for eight rooms in one block; "18%" tells us nothing about other blocks. Forcing words: "Only one hostel block".
  • Hedges: "suggest", "may". They tell you the authors are not sure and are saying so.
  • Stronger evidence: the water measure (metered per room) beats "more careful" (self-report). Showers were "not metered", so the shower claim is unsupported.
  • Limitation → blocked conclusion: single block → cannot say it works campus-wide.
Try it: is this safe?Claim: "Low-flow taps reduced water use across the university." Trap: overgeneralisation. Safe version: "In one hostel block, low-flow taps were associated with 18% lower use."

2.5 Exam answers: what to write

QuestionWrite
Surface vs deepThe literal reading, then what it implies; quote the forcing words
HedgingQuote two hedges; say the authors are not certain
TrapName it; cite the passage detail that makes the conclusion unsafe
LimitationsName each + the conclusion it blocks
Stronger evidenceMeasured over self-reported, plus one reason

33The Research Article

Once you know the structure, you can navigate research in any field, even without understanding the science. Each section exists so the next can be checked.

3.1 IMRaD: the universal language of science

SectionQuestion it answers
IntroductionWhat is the problem, why does it matter, why should anyone care?
MethodsHow was it done, and could someone else repeat it?
ResultsWhat did the data show? Facts only
DiscussionWhat does it mean, what are the implications, what next?
Why this orderMethods let you judge Results; Results let you judge Discussion. Because Results carry only numbers, Discussion is the one place interpretation lives, which is what makes a claim testable.

3.2 The full map of an article, and the words that give each part away

PartJobGiveaway
TitleSays exactly what the paper is abouta vague title is a red flag
AbstractThe trailer: 150–250 words, read firstwhat / why / how / so what
KeywordsSearch handles chosen by the authorslist of terms
IntroductionSets the scene; ends with the gap and objective"however", "this paper"
Literature reviewWhat others foundnames and years: "Rao (2022) reported…"
MethodsThe recipe, repeatableprocedure, sample, timing, "was logged"
ResultsThe data, no interpretationnumbers, tables
DiscussionNow the authors get opinions"this suggests", "this indicates"
ConclusionAnswers the opening question; says what remains untested"remains to be tested"
ReferencesThe credibility receiptnumbered or alphabetical list
Misconception"I place an extract by its topic." Judge by wording: "logged every minute" is Methods, "the mean was 52" is Results, "this suggests" is Discussion, even when all three talk about noise. In the exam name the section and its job.

3.3 Writing an abstract: four questions, in order

Worked example (original, 63 words) What: This study examined how under-inflated tyres affect fuel use in city buses, Why: a major cost rarely studied in daily service. How: Twelve buses ran at recommended pressure and twelve at 15% below it, on one route for six weeks, with litres per 100 km logged at each refill. Found + so what: Under-inflated buses used 4% more fuel, so routine pressure checks are worthwhile; other routes remain untested. Checks: four questions in order ✔ · under 70 words ✔ · hedged to one route ✔ · no invented details ✔
PartTick
What was studiedNames the object and setting
Why it matteredStates the gap
HowSample, measure, period
Found + followsA number, then one implication
→ Not every article is IMRaD: humanities papers argue by themes, have no Methods section, and may state the thesis only at the end.

3.4 How an introduction works: three moves

  1. Establish the territory: the field matters; here is accepted knowledge (heavily cited).
  2. Establish the niche: but something is missing. Watch for the turn: however, yet, remains unclear. This hinge sentence is the paper's reason for existing.
  3. Occupy the niche: this paper fills the gap; states the objective and often maps the sections.

3.5 Reading order: not front to back

Abstract (worth the next 20 minutes?) → Conclusion (where the argument ends) → Figures & tables (evidence before opinion) → Methods (can you judge anything without it?) → Introduction & Discussion → References (whom it builds on, how recent).

3.6 Critical reading checklist: six questions, in order

  1. Is the research question stated and specific?
  2. Is the method appropriate to that question?
  3. Are the results presented without spin?
  4. Does the discussion follow from the results?
  5. Are limitations acknowledged honestly?
  6. Is every claim supported? Confidence is not evidence.

3.8 Activity: read, annotate, return

On one article: (1) label the structure; (2) define two terms (one formal, one operational); (3) draw three inferences with forcing lines; (4) write the headline claim beside what the data show; (5) pick the one sentence you would most want defended.
