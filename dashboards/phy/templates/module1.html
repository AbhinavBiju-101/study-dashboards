{% call sec('m1-1','1','Dual nature of radiation and the wave background') %} {% call sub('m1-1-1','1.1','Dual nature of radiation and matter') %}
Why it matters. This is the central idea of the whole course: light behaves like a wave in some experiments and like a stream of particles in others, and (de Broglie) so do electrons and other matter.
{% call box('def') %}Wave theory of electromagnetic radiation explains interference, diffraction and polarisation. Quantum theory (radiation as quanta) explains the photoelectric effect, the Compton effect, blackbody radiation and X-ray spectra. So radiation has a dual nature: wave and particle. De Broglie suggested that particles such as electrons, protons and neutrons also have a dual nature, on the argument that nature loves symmetry: the universe is made of particles and radiation, and the two should be symmetrical. The two bridges are E = hν (wave frequency gives particle energy) and λ = h/p (particle momentum gives wavelength).{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-1-2','1.2','Wave characteristics and the wave equation of a travelling wave') %}
Why it matters. De Broglie waves, phase velocity and interference all use the same handful of definitions.
{% call box('def') %}Amplitude A: maximum displacement. Wavelength λ: distance between successive crests. Wave number k = 2π/λ. Period T: time for one cycle, frequency f = 1/T. Wave speed v = fλ = λ/T. Angular frequency ω = 2πf. A travelling wave: y = A cos(ωt − kx), with phase velocity v = ω/k. Waves are transverse (displacement perpendicular to travel; light, string waves) or longitudinal (parallel; sound). Electromagnetic waves need no medium; mechanical waves do. Wave behaviour: reflection (bounce back), refraction (bending on entering a new medium because speed changes), diffraction (bending round an obstacle or through a narrow opening), interference (waves combining).{% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}
Problem. Green light has λ = 500 nm and travels at c = 3.0×10⁸ m/s. Find f, ω and k.
f = c/λ = 3.0×10⁸/(500×10⁻⁹) = 6.0×10¹⁴ Hz. ω = 2πf = 3.77×10¹⁵ rad/s. k = 2π/λ = 1.26×10⁷ m⁻¹.
Check. ω/k = 3.77×10¹⁵/1.26×10⁷ = 3.0×10⁸ m/s = c ✓, as v = ω/k requires.
{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"Frequency changes when light enters glass." Frequency is fixed by the source; speed and wavelength change (λ = v/f), which is why light bends.{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-1-3','1.3','Superposition and interference') %}
Why it matters. Interference is the signature of a wave. If electrons or photons show it, they are behaving as waves, however particle-like they seem when detected.
{% call box('def') %}Principle of superposition: where two or more waves overlap in a medium, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements, y = y₁ + y₂ + …, and the waves pass through each other undisturbed. It needs waves of the same type in a linear medium. Constructive interference: crest meets crest (phase difference 0, 2π, …); two waves of amplitude A give 2A. Destructive interference: crest meets trough (phase difference π, 3π, …); two equal waves cancel to zero. Coherent waves have the same frequency and a constant phase difference, so give a steady pattern.{% endcall %} {% call fig('Two equal waves (solid) and their sum. In phase (left) the sum has double amplitude; in antiphase (right) the sum is zero. Notice the frequency of the sum equals that of the originals.') %} {% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"Destructive interference destroys energy." The energy is redistributed: where waves cancel, others add; the total is conserved. (This is why Young's fringes have dark and bright bands without energy being lost.){% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-1-4','1.4',"Young's double-slit experiment") %}
Why it matters. In 1801 Young showed light interferes, which settled (for a century) that light is a wave against Newton's particle picture. The same set-up later shows electrons interfering.
{% call box('def') %}Light from a monochromatic source passes a single slit (to make it coherent) and then two close narrow slits S₁, S₂ (separation d), which act as coherent sources. On a screen at distance D, alternating bright and dark fringes form. For a point at height y from the centre, with small angle θ (θ ≈ sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ y/D, valid when D ≫ d): path difference Δl = d sin θ ≈ d y/D. Bright fringes (constructive): Δl = nλ, so y_n = nλD/d. Dark fringes (destructive): Δl = (n + ½)λ, so y = (2n + 1)λD/(2d). Fringe width (between successive bright or dark fringes): β = λD/d. In a medium of refractive index μ, λ becomes λ/μ, so β is divided by μ. With white light each colour makes its own pattern: red fringes are wider than violet, and the centre is white.{% endcall %} {% call fig('Double slit geometry: slits S₁, S₂ a distance d apart, screen at D. The rays to a point P at height y differ in length by Δl = d sin θ. Notice the diagram is not to scale: in reality D ≫ d, so the two rays are almost parallel.') %} {% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}

β = λD/d = (600×10⁻⁹ × 1.5)/(0.50×10⁻³) = 1.8×10⁻³ m = 1.8 mm.

Check. Small-angle condition: y/D = 5.4×10⁻³/1.5 = 3.6×10⁻³ rad, so sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ is excellent ✓. Check the path difference: d y/D = 0.5×10⁻³ × 3.6×10⁻³ = 1.8×10⁻⁶ m = 3 × 600 nm ✓ (n = 3).
{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"Dark fringes use Δl = nλ." That is the bright condition. Dark needs a half-integer number of wavelengths: Δl = (n + ½)λ.{% endcall %}

{% call sec('m1-2','2','Blackbody radiation') %} {% call sub('m1-2-1','2.1','Thermal radiation, the blackbody and Kirchhoff\'s law') %}
Why it matters. Hot objects glow, and the colour depends on the temperature, not the material. An ideal absorber is the cleanest object to study, and explaining its spectrum is what started quantum theory.
{% call box('def') %}Thermal radiation is the radiation emitted by a body because of its temperature. A blackbody is an ideal body that absorbs all radiation falling on it, at every wavelength and angle, independent of the material. Kirchhoff's law: a body in thermal equilibrium emits power in proportion to the power it absorbs, so a good absorber is a good emitter; at constant temperature it absorbs at the same rate as it emits. The spectral radiance is the distribution of blackbody radiation over wavelength. Hotter objects glow red, then yellow, then white-blue; at room temperature most emission is infrared and invisible.{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"A blackbody looks black." Only when cold. A blackbody at 6000 K is white-hot; "black" refers to perfect absorption.{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-2-3','2.3','Rayleigh-Jeans law and the ultraviolet catastrophe') %}
Why it matters. The classical prediction blows up at short wavelengths. The failure is so dramatic that it forced a new idea.
{% call box('def') %}Rayleigh and Jeans treated the cavity as a set of standing waves (counting modes: the number per unit volume between ν and ν + dν is 8πν²dν/c³) and gave each mode the classical equipartition energy ε = kT. The result: u(ν)dν = (8πν²/c³) kT dν, or in wavelength u(λ) = 8πkT/λ⁴. It fits experiment at long wavelengths but grows without limit as λ → 0 (ν → ∞): the ultraviolet catastrophe. The total energy density, found by integrating over all frequencies, would be infinite, which is absurd.{% endcall %} {% call fig('Computed from the formulas: Planck spectra for 3000, 4000 and 5000 K (solid) and the classical Rayleigh-Jeans curve for 5000 K (dashed). The shaded band is visible light (400 to 700 nm). Notice the dashed curve shoots off to infinity at short wavelength while the real spectrum turns over and falls to zero, and that the two agree at long wavelength.') %} {{ planck_plot()|safe }} {% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"The ultraviolet catastrophe is a catastrophe in the lab." It is a failure of the theory: real hot bodies do not emit infinite energy.{% endcall %}

{% call sec('m1-3','3',"Planck's hypothesis") %} {% call sub('m1-3-1','3.1','Quantisation of oscillator energy') %}
Why it matters. Planck found that the data fit only if the walls of the cavity could exchange energy in lumps proportional to the frequency. Classical physics has no link between an oscillator's energy and its frequency; this is that link.
{% call box('def') %}Planck's hypotheses (slide 25): (1) The cavity contains simple harmonic oscillators (resonators of molecular size) that exchange energy with the radiation. (2) They can vibrate at all frequencies. (3) An oscillator of frequency ν can have only the discrete energies Eₙ = nhν, n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …. (4) Emission is a decrease and absorption an increase of oscillator energy, in steps of hν. (5) The energies are weighted by the Boltzmann distribution (probability ∝ e−E/kT), not by classical equipartition. Here h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant. Energy is emitted or absorbed in quanta, each E = hν = hc/λ, so longer wavelength means smaller energy per quantum.{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-3-2',"3.2","Planck's radiation law and its limits") %}
Why it matters. One formula reproduces the whole measured curve, and in two limits it reduces to the two older laws. Showing those limits is a standard derivation question.
{% call box('def') %}With Eₙ = nhν and Boltzmann weights, the average energy of a mode is ⟨ε⟩ = hν/(ehν/kT − 1). Multiplying by the mode density 8πν²/c³: u(ν)dν = (8πhν³/c³) dν/(ehν/kT − 1) (Planck's radiation formula). Low frequency (hν ≪ kT): ehν/kT ≈ 1 + hν/kT, so ehν/kT − 1 ≈ hν/kT and ⟨ε⟩ → kT, giving u(ν)dν = (8πν²kT/c³)dν, the Rayleigh-Jeans law. High frequency (hν ≫ kT): ehν/kT → ∞, so u(ν) → 0 (the Wien form), and the ultraviolet catastrophe disappears.{% endcall %}

{% call sec('m1-4','4','The photoelectric effect') %} {% call sub('m1-4-1','4.1','Observations and the laws of photoelectric emission') %}
Why it matters. Classical wave theory predicts the wrong answer for three of the observations below. This is the clearest proof that light comes in photons.
{% call box('def') %}Photoelectric effect: electrons (photoelectrons) are emitted from a metal surface exposed to light of suitable frequency; the current they carry is the photoelectric current. Experimental facts: (1) For a fixed frequency the photocurrent is proportional to the intensity. (2) The current saturates when all emitted electrons reach the anode (saturation current), and falls to zero at a negative stopping potential V_s, which does not depend on intensity. (3) V_s increases with frequency. (4) Below a threshold frequency ν₀, which depends on the metal, there is no emission however intense the light. (5) The emission is practically instantaneous (delay about 10⁻⁹ s). (6) One photon ejects one electron.{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-4-2',"4.2","Einstein's photoelectric equation") %}
Why it matters. A one-line energy balance explains every observation and lets you calculate the work function, threshold, stopping potential and electron speed.
{% call box('def') %}Einstein: an electron is ejected by a collision with a single photon, which transfers all its energy hf. Part of it (the work function W₀, a few eV for most metals) frees the electron; the rest is kinetic energy: hf = W₀ + KE_max, where KE_max = ½mv²_max = eV_s. The threshold frequency f₀ = W₀/h and threshold wavelength λ₀ = hc/W₀. If hf < W₀ no electron is ejected. A graph of V_s against f is a straight line of slope h/e that cuts the frequency axis at f₀ (this is how h can be measured).{% endcall %} {% call fig('Stopping potential against frequency: a straight line with slope h/e, intercept on the f axis at the threshold f₀. Notice the line does not pass through the origin; nothing is emitted below f₀.') %} {% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}
Problem. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal with work function 2.0 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy, the stopping potential, the maximum speed and the threshold wavelength.
Photon energy: hc/λ = 1240/400 = 3.10 eV. KE_max = 3.10 − 2.0 = 1.10 eV (= 1.76×10⁻¹⁹ J).
V_s = KE_max/e = 1.10 V. v_max = √(2KE/m) = √(2 × 1.76×10⁻¹⁹/9.11×10⁻³¹) = 6.2×10⁵ m/s.
λ₀ = hc/W₀ = 1240/2.0 = 620 nm.
Check. 400 nm < 620 nm (shorter wavelength than the threshold) so emission occurs ✓. v ≪ c (6×10⁵ vs 3×10⁸), so the non-relativistic KE formula is fine ✓.
{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"Brighter light gives faster electrons." Brighter light gives more electrons (larger current); faster electrons need higher frequency. Also, if f < f₀, no amount of intensity helps.{% endcall %}

{% call sec('m1-5','5','The de Broglie hypothesis and matter waves') %} {% call sub('m1-5-1','5.1','De Broglie wavelength') %}
Why it matters. This is the formula you will use most in numerical questions: a particle of momentum p behaves like a wave of wavelength h/p.
{% call box('def') %}For a photon, E = hc/λ and E = mc² (with p = mc) give p = h/λ. De Broglie proposed the same relation for any particle: λ = h/p = h/(mv). In terms of kinetic energy E_k: λ = h/√(2mE_k). For a charge q accelerated through a potential V (E_k = qV): λ = h/√(2mqV). For a particle in thermal equilibrium at temperature T, with E = (3/2)kT: λ = h/√(3mkT). For an electron accelerated through V volts: λ = 12.26/√V Å. Your deck also gives alpha particle 0.1012/√V Å, proton 0.286/√V Å, deuteron 0.202/√V Å.{% endcall %}

{% call sub('m1-5-2','5.2','Properties of matter waves') %}
Why it matters. A standard short-answer question: list properties of matter waves, and say how they differ from electromagnetic waves.
{% call box('def') %}(i) λ is inversely proportional to velocity (faster means shorter); for a particle at rest λ is infinite. (ii) λ is inversely proportional to mass (heavier particles have shorter wavelength). (iii) λ is independent of the charge of the particle. (iv) Matter waves travel in vacuum, so they are not mechanical waves. (v) They are not electromagnetic, since they are not produced by accelerated charges. (vi) They are probability waves: the square of the amplitude gives the probability of finding the particle. (vii) Particles have wave-like properties and waves have particle-like properties, and these descriptions are linked but separate (slide 7: the wave representation is symbolic).{% endcall %}
Next: 5.3 builds a localised "particle" out of waves.
{% endcall %}
{% call sub('m1-5-3','5.3','Wave packets, phase velocity and group velocity') %}
Why it matters. A single sine wave fills all space and cannot represent a particle located somewhere. Adding many waves of slightly different wavelengths gives a localised packet, which moves at the group velocity.
{% call box('def') %}A wave packet is a superposition of many waves that interfere constructively near the particle and destructively elsewhere; it has an envelope that travels at the group velocity v_g, while the ripples inside travel at the phase velocity v_p. For y = A cos(ωt − kx): v_p = ω/k = fλ and v_g = dω/dk. For de Broglie waves: v_p = c²/v (greater than c, which is allowed because it carries no energy or signal) and v_g = v (the particle's speed). Hence v_g v_p = c². In general v_g = v_p − λ dv_p/dλ: in a dispersive medium v_g ≠ v_p (de Broglie waves); in a non-dispersive medium v_g = v_p (light in vacuum).{% endcall %} {% call fig('A wave packet made by adding 15 cosines with wave numbers spread around a central value (computed). Notice the packet is localised: large in the middle where the components are in phase, vanishing at the edges where they cancel. Adding more spread in k would make it narrower.') %} {{ wavepacket()|safe }} {% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}

{% call sec('m1-6','6','The Heisenberg uncertainty principle') %} {% call sub('m1-6-1','6.1','Statement of the principle') %}
Why it matters. A wave packet that is narrow in position needs many wavelengths; so a particle cannot have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum. This is a property of nature, not of the instruments.
{% call box('def') %}Heisenberg uncertainty principle (1927): it is impossible to determine both the position and the momentum of a particle simultaneously and exactly. The product of the uncertainties can never be smaller than h/4π: Δx Δp ≥ h/4π = ħ/2. The limitation exists in nature itself. The companion relation for energy and time: ΔE Δt ≥ h/4π. Order-of-magnitude versions Δx Δp ≈ h (or ≈ ħ) are used for estimates.{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}Your slides (6-8) state the principle in three forms: Δx Δp ≈ h (a rough estimate from the microscope argument), "Δx Δp ≥ h" (the "final minimum uncertainties") and Δx Δp ≥ h/4π (the "final accurate" form). The accurate bound is h/4π = ħ/2. Use the form the question asks for; if it asks for the "minimum uncertainty" or says "≥ h/4π", use that, and if the lecturer's problem sets say "Δp = h/Δx" (as in your Class 8 problem 7) use the order-of-magnitude form and say so.{% endcall %}
Next: 6.2 gives the physical argument.
{% endcall %}
{% call sub('m1-6-2','6.2','The microscope thought experiment') %}
Why it matters. It shows why you cannot get around the principle by a cleverer instrument.
{% call box('def') %}To see an electron, at least one photon must bounce off it. To locate it accurately you need a photon of short wavelength (the position uncertainty is about the wavelength: Δx ≈ λ). But a short-wavelength photon has large momentum h/λ and gives the electron a large, uncertain kick: Δp ≈ h/λ. To disturb the electron less you need long wavelength, which blurs the position. So Δx Δp ≈ λ × h/λ = h, whatever λ you choose.{% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}
Problem. A photon microscope locates an electron to within 0.1 Å. Find the minimum uncertainty in its momentum and velocity (a) with your deck's estimate Δx Δp ≈ h, and (b) with Δx Δp ≥ h/4π.
(a) Δp = h/Δx = 6.63×10⁻³⁴/(0.1×10⁻¹⁰) = 6.6×10⁻²³ kg·m/s; Δv = Δp/m = 6.63×10⁻²³/9.1×10⁻³¹ = 7.3×10⁷ m/s (your problem 7).
(b) Δp = ħ/(2Δx) = 1.055×10⁻³⁴/(2 × 10⁻¹¹) = 5.3×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s, so Δv = 5.8×10⁶ m/s.
Check. The two answers differ by a factor 4π = 12.6 (6.6×10⁻²³/5.3×10⁻²⁴ = 12.5 ✓). Both give a velocity uncertainty far larger than the speed an electron in an atom has (~10⁶ m/s), showing an electron cannot be pinned to 0.1 Å without huge momentum uncertainty.

{% endcall %} {% endcall %}{# end sec 6 #}
{% call sec('m1-7','7','The wavefunction') %} {% call sub('m1-7-1','7.1','Meaning of the wavefunction and probability density') %}
Why it matters. In quantum mechanics a particle is described by a state, not a trajectory; the wavefunction is that state.
{% call box('def') %}In 1926 Schrödinger proposed a wave equation for matter waves. The state of a particle is described by a complex wavefunction Ψ(r, t), a probability amplitude that depends on position and time. Ψ itself has no direct physical meaning; the physical quantity is |Ψ|² = Ψ*Ψ, the probability density. The probability of finding the particle in the volume dx dy dz at time t is |Ψ|² dx dy dz; in one dimension, the probability between a and b is P = ∫ₐᵇ |Ψ|² dx. The sign of Ψ has no physical significance (positive and negative regions give the same |Ψ|²). Schrödinger's equation is iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ (Module 3, Section 5).{% endcall %} {% call box('mis') %}"Ψ is the probability." |Ψ|² is the probability density; Ψ can be complex or negative, so cannot itself be a probability.{% endcall %}
Next: 7.2 lists what makes a wavefunction acceptable.
{% endcall %}
{% call sub('m1-7-2','7.2','Acceptable wavefunctions and normalisation') %}
Why it matters. Not every function can be a wavefunction. These conditions are used to throw out solutions and to fix constants.
{% call box('def') %}A wavefunction must be: (i) single-valued; (ii) finite everywhere; (iii) continuous (except where the potential is infinite); (iv) have a continuous first derivative (slope); (v) square-integrable and normalisable. It usually vanishes at the boundaries of a confined region. Normalisation: the particle must be somewhere, so ∫|Ψ|² dV = 1 over all space. Two wavefunctions are orthogonal if ∫ ψₘ* ψₙ dx = 0.{% endcall %} {% call box('ex') %}
